# X129 — systems-safety → reliability: an evidence-diversity interlock for agent conclusions

**Unit:** X129  
**Lens:** cross-domain borrow from systems safety (STAMP/STPA, defence in depth and the Swiss-cheese model, safety/assurance cases)  
**Target failure:** an agent confidently reports a wrong conclusion from a single, unvalidated probe  
**Scope discipline:** ideation and external research only. I did not inspect or re-derive the local harness. The six-plus observed instances and the mandatory two-signal rule are treated as pinned facts supplied in the unit brief, not as findings of this unit.

## Executive result

The borrow transfers, but only partially. The specific transferable mechanism is an **independent-and-diverse evidence barrier, expressed as a miniature assurance case and placed as an interlock before a consequential claim is released**. STPA supplies the right unit of analysis: not “did a tool fail?”, but “under what context does the controller issue an unsafe control action because its internal process model is wrong?” Defence in depth supplies the rule that multiple layers count only when a common cause does not defeat their assumed independence. Assurance-case practice supplies the explicit claim → argument → evidence structure and tests for coverage, dependency, definition, directness, relevance, and robustness.

Translated to the harness, the controller is the agent, tool observations are sensor feedback, the agent’s working belief is its process model, and a categorical statement such as “X is absent,” “Y is broken,” or “the task is done” is the control action. The unsafe control action is releasing that categorical statement when the evidence is one unvalidated observation, is out of scope, is stale, or shares a failure mode with its apparent corroboration.

The proposed implementation is a small **Claim-Release Interlock (CRI)** on the existing hook bus. It does not decide whether the conclusion is true. It deterministically prevents a protected categorical claim from being presented as verified unless the transcript contains a machine-checkable micro-case with (1) a scope statement, (2) a known-present positive control proving the detector can see something in that scope, (3) two target signals from mechanically independent evidence families, (4) a common-cause/dependency declaration, and (5) evidence receipts tied to actual tool results. If those conditions are absent, the allowed output is an inline `UNVERIFIED` claim naming the settling experiment—not a confident conclusion.

**Transfer verdict:** it is **very likely (80-90%)**, based on the close structural correspondence between STPA’s controller/process-model/feedback failure model and an agent reasoning from tool feedback, plus the GSN and NASA warnings that nominally multiple evidence strands may share a flawed model or common cause, that this mechanism directly addresses the observed *class* of single-probe overclaim. This does **not** establish its empirical catch rate in this harness. That performance premise is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is the historical counterfactual replay described in “Evaluation and falsification.”**

The proposal is deliberately not “run a second agent.” A second model reading the same context is a correlated opinion, not mechanically independent evidence. Nor is it a second orchestration runtime, vector database, knowledge graph, durable workflow engine, trained critic, observability SaaS, or guardrail framework.

## 1. What systems safety solved in its own field

### 1.1 STAMP/STPA: constrain unsafe control actions caused by an incorrect process model

STAMP reframes safety as a control problem rather than merely a chain of component failures. In STPA, an **unsafe control action (UCA)** is a control action that, in a particular context and worst-case environment, leads to a hazard. The STPA Handbook then examines four forms: the needed action is not provided; an unsafe action is provided; an action is too early, too late, or out of order; or it is stopped too soon/applied too long ([MIT STPA Handbook, pp. 34–35](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf)).

The especially relevant STPA mechanism is its treatment of the controller’s **process model**. The controller chooses actions using beliefs about the controlled process. A process-model flaw exists when those beliefs do not match reality. The handbook identifies four feedback routes to that mismatch: incorrect information is received; correct information is misinterpreted or ignored; needed information is delayed or absent; or the necessary feedback does not exist. It explicitly directs the analyst to start with a UCA, identify the controller beliefs that could cause it, and then explain how feedback or its absence could create those beliefs ([MIT STPA Handbook, pp. 44–46](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf)).

That is more than a slogan. The method forces a causal scenario all the way through the feedback channel. The handbook says an inadequate-feedback scenario is unfinished until the analyst explains why the feedback could be inadequate; it enumerates transmission loss and delay, sensor failure, measurement inaccuracy, configuration errors, environmental conditions, specification flaws, and sensors that are correct but misleading ([MIT STPA Handbook, pp. 46–47](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf)). It also warns against assuming that issuing a control action means the action was actually executed—an exact systems form of mistaking “command returned” for “postcondition holds” ([MIT STPA Handbook, Appendix G, p. 181](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf)).

In its native field, the mechanism therefore works as follows:

1. Define unacceptable losses and system hazards.
2. Model controllers, control actions, controlled processes, and feedback.
3. Identify the contexts in which providing, withholding, mistiming, or over-applying an action becomes unsafe.
4. Generate causal scenarios, including incorrect controller beliefs and the feedback defects that produce them.
5. Convert those scenarios into enforceable constraints and verification requirements.

This matters because a device can be functioning exactly as implemented while the system is unsafe: the controller can take a locally plausible action based on a false model of the world. STPA is consequently a stronger source-field match than a simple “retry on tool error” rule.

### 1.2 Defence in depth: redundancy is not evidence unless common causes are controlled

The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission defines defence in depth as multiple independent and redundant defensive layers so that no single layer, however robust, is exclusively relied on; it also names diversity among the means used ([NRC glossary](https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/basic-ref/glossary/defense-in-depth)). NASA’s system-safety requirements make the crucial qualification explicit: when redundancy is used, common-cause failures must be checked so they do not invalidate the assumption of independence, and multiple inhibits must have their independence verified under operational conditions ([NASA NPR 8715.3C, §§1.7.4.2(g), 1.7.5.1(d)](https://nodis3.gsfc.nasa.gov/displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_8715_003C_&page_name=Chapter1)).

James Reason’s Swiss-cheese model is useful here, but only as an explanatory model. It treats defenses, barriers, and safeguards as imperfect layers whose weaknesses (“holes”) change; loss occurs when weaknesses in multiple layers align. It distinguishes active failures near the event from latent conditions created earlier in the system ([Reason, “Human error: models and management,” *BMJ* 320 (2000), pp. 768–770](https://www.bmj.com/content/320/7237/768)).

The transferable lesson is **not** “two checks are always better than one.” It is that layers must fail differently. Two invocations of the same search command, two shells wrapping the same executable, two agents reading the same truncated transcript, or a file listing and a glob backed by the same broken path expansion may create two observations while retaining one failure mode. NASA’s common-cause rule and the NRC’s diversity language are what prevent the cheese metaphor from degenerating into box-checking.

### 1.3 Assurance cases: force the claim, scope, argument, and evidence into the open

A safety/assurance case is a structured argument connecting a safety or dependability claim to evidence. An FAA-hosted overview defines it as linking specific claims to evidence with an explicit justification for how engineering evidence supports safety claims in a specified environment ([FAA, “Towards an Ontological Basis for Safety / Assurance Cases,” slide 2](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/air_traffic/technology/swim/governance/Safety%20Cases.pdf)).

The Goal Structuring Notation (GSN) community standard is particularly valuable because it gives concrete review tests rather than merely asking for “more evidence”:

- **Coverage:** does the argument cover the whole conclusion rather than a subset?
- **Dependency:** do supposedly multiple evidence strands actually depend on one flawed model?
- **Definition:** is the operating context improperly narrow or ambiguous?
- **Directness:** does the evidence address the claim itself rather than only a process proxy?
- **Relevance:** does the evidence concern the version, scope, and object claimed?
- **Robustness:** does the conclusion survive reasonable changes in evidence or assumptions?

These criteria and the example of two evidence forms sharing a common flawed model appear in the [GSN Community Standard v1, pp. 56–57](https://www.faa.gov/about/office_org/headquarters_offices/ang/redac/redac-sas-201503-gsn-community-standard-v1.pdf). NASA’s IV&V guidance adds that independent examination should ask both whether the right system was built and whether it was built correctly, and defines independence technically, managerially, and financially ([NASA Software Engineering Handbook, SWE-141](https://swehb.nasa.gov/spaces/SWEHBVB/pages/32604595/SWE-141%2B-%2BSoftware%2BIndependent%2BVerification%2Band%2BValidation)). The harness translation needs technical independence of observations, not the organizational and financial dimensions used for full IV&V.

## 2. The concrete translation

### 2.1 Mapping the control structure

| Systems-safety element | Agent-harness counterpart | Failure relevant to this unit |
|---|---|---|
| Controller | Agent/model deciding what to report | Converts limited evidence into a categorical conclusion |
| Controlled process | Filesystem, repo, service, configuration, task state, or external system being examined | Actual state differs from the agent’s belief |
| Sensor/feedback | Tool output, exit status, API response, parser result, file contents | Output is empty, truncated, stale, scoped incorrectly, misleading, or produced by a broken detector |
| Process model | Agent’s working beliefs about scope, tool semantics, freshness, and task postconditions | “No matches” becomes “absent”; “exit 0” becomes “done” |
| Control action | Final or intermediate assertion that guides owner action | “Absent,” “broken,” “fixed,” “complete,” “only cause,” “all tests pass” |
| Hazard | Owner acts on an unjustified categorical claim | Needed capability is removed/rebuilt, a defect is missed, or work stops prematurely |
| Safety constraint | Claim cannot be released as verified without adequate, independent evidence | Weak evidence is downgraded to inline `UNVERIFIED` with a settling test |

The analogy captures the actual epistemic failure: the first probe is feedback, not ground truth. The agent has a controller model of what the probe means. “Empty output” can mean target absence, wrong scope, detector malfunction, permission filtering, truncation, unsupported syntax, stale state, or a legitimately empty target. A second signal helps only if its failure causes are sufficiently distinct.

### 2.2 Protected claim classes

The first implementation should protect only categorical, decision-changing claims, not every sentence:

- **ABSENT:** an item, capability, event, file, configuration, or behavior does not exist.
- **BROKEN/DISABLED:** a mechanism cannot operate or did not operate.
- **COMPLETE/FIXED/PASSING:** a requested postcondition now holds or no required work remains.
- **EXHAUSTIVE:** “all,” “none,” “only,” “every,” or an equivalent closed-world claim.
- **EXCLUSIVE CAUSE:** a stated cause is the sole root cause, or alternatives have been ruled out.

This risk-scoped set is **likely (55-80%)**, based on the unit brief’s observed false conclusions and STPA’s focus on hazardous context rather than universal checking, to capture substantially more damaging overclaims per unit of friction than gating all factual statements. It is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is to label the historical correction candidates by claim class and measure what fraction of consequential corrections fall inside this set.**

### 2.3 The Claim-Release Interlock

#### Proposal A — Claim-Release Interlock — UNVERIFIED pending installed-version contract test

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)**

Implement one deterministic command hook plus a compact policy instruction. Claude Code’s native `Stop` hook receives the last assistant message and transcript path, can block stopping, and returns the reason to the agent; the official hook reference documents those fields and blocking behavior ([Claude Code hooks reference, “Stop” and “SubagentStop”](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks)). Thus the mechanism can ride the existing bus rather than introduce an orchestrator.

The agent must attach a compact machine-readable evidence block for each protected claim. The user-facing prose remains normal; the block can be folded or placed after the answer. A conceptual record is:

```json
{
  "claim_id": "C1",
  "class": "ABSENT",
  "text": "No project-scoped formatter configuration exists",
  "scope": {"root": "<resolved path>", "revision": "<identity>", "time": "<timestamp>"},
  "control": {"receipt": "T17", "expected": "known-present sentinel", "observed": true},
  "signals": [
    {"receipt": "T18", "family": "filesystem-enumeration", "result": "no target"},
    {"receipt": "T19", "family": "versioned-manifest", "result": "no target"}
  ],
  "dependency": {"shared_roots": [], "known_common_causes": []},
  "coverage": "all relevant names and locations under the declared root",
  "status": "VERIFIED"
}
```

The hook performs deterministic checks only:

1. Locate protected claim markers and their evidence blocks.
2. Verify every receipt names a real prior tool result in the current transcript; capture the tool name, normalized target, exit status, and a digest of the observed output. The agent may summarize evidence but cannot mint a receipt.
3. Verify the positive control ran in the same relevant scope and was observed. A detector that cannot find a known-present sentinel is disqualified.
4. Require two target signals with distinct registered evidence families. Repeating the same family does not count.
5. Reject declared independence when the registry says the families share a disqualifying root (same parser, same index, same cache, same command implementation, same remote endpoint, or one signal derived from the other).
6. Require explicit scope and coverage for closed-world claims.
7. If the case passes, allow `VERIFIED`. If it fails, block once with the missing fields and instruct the agent either to gather evidence or change the claim inline to `UNVERIFIED — settle by <specific experiment>`.
8. If the hook itself errors, **fail open only to an explicitly `UNVERIFIED` output**, never to a verified categorical claim. This avoids claiming the barrier worked when its own detector did not.

A Stop-hook interlock is **very likely (80-90%)**, based on the documented availability of the final message and transcript path plus the ability to block stopping, to be implementable without a daemon. This is still **UNVERIFIED on the owner’s installed Claude Code version; the settling experiment is a disposable-session contract test that emits one synthetic protected claim, confirms the hook sees the final message and transcript receipt, blocks exactly once, and then permits the downgraded response.**

The under-200-line estimate is **roughly even (45-55%)**, based on the narrow deterministic schema and no network/service requirement, because robust transcript parsing and Windows path normalization could exceed the threshold. This estimate is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is a spike implementing only the receipt validator and measuring nonblank, noncomment source lines.** If it exceeds roughly 200 lines, split the schema/policy data from code rather than promote it to a service; the architecture remains BUILD-SMALL unless it actually requires a daemon/runtime.

#### Proposal B — evidence-family and common-cause registry — UNVERIFIED pending mutation test

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only)**

Maintain a small JSON registry describing which probe families count as distinct and which common roots invalidate independence. Example entries:

```json
{
  "filesystem-enumeration": {"roots": ["filesystem-api"]},
  "shell-find": {"roots": ["process-spawn", "filesystem-api"]},
  "repo-index": {"roots": ["git-object-database"]},
  "service-api": {"roots": ["network", "remote-service"]},
  "rendered-ui": {"roots": ["ui-renderer", "application-query"]}
}
```

Independence is claim-relative. For a working-tree absence claim, `find` and a language glob may share filesystem access but differ in parser and process-spawn paths; whether that difference is adequate depends on the suspected failure. For a permission-filtering hazard, neither is independent if both run under the same denied identity. For a Git history claim, working-tree enumeration is not coverage at all. The registry must therefore express disqualifying roots per claim/scenario rather than assert universal independence.

An initial hand-authored registry is **likely (55-80%)**, based on the finite set of high-use evidence mechanisms and the GSN common-model warning, to catch obvious pseudo-independence such as identical retries and wrapper-equivalent commands. It is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is mutation testing with injected common failures (bad glob parser, denied directory, stale index, truncated output, disconnected endpoint) and checking whether any registered pair incorrectly passes as independent.**

#### Proposal C — STPA-derived adversarial contract tests — UNVERIFIED pending mutation-score experiment

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)**

Add a table-driven test fixture for the barrier. This is not a production runtime. Generate scenarios from the four STPA control-action categories:

| UCA form | Harness scenario | Required safe behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Unsafe claim provided | Single empty search leads to “absent” | Block or force inline `UNVERIFIED` |
| Needed qualification not provided | Evidence is incomplete but caveat omitted | Block until qualification is present |
| Claim released too early/out of order | “Fixed” precedes postcondition test | Block until postcondition evidence occurs after the change |
| Gate applied too long | Agent has valid diverse receipts but Stop hook repeats | Permit stop; loop guard reports hook defect |

Add causal mutations for wrong root, stale revision, success exit with empty/truncated output, known-present sentinel missed, two receipts from the same raw output, two shell aliases resolving to one executable, and a target changed after evidence collection. The last mutation tests evidence freshness rather than mere existence.

These tests are **very likely (80-90%)**, based on STPA’s explicit UCA categories and feedback-causal-scenario method, to find barrier implementation defects that happy-path examples miss. The magnitude is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is the mutation score itself, with each seeded defect required to cause a failing contract test before implementation is accepted.**

### 2.4 Claim-specific evidence patterns

The rule is not “always run command A and command B.” It is a structured case selected for the claim:

#### ABSENT

- Positive control: same detector and scope finds a known-present item with comparable naming/path characteristics.
- Signal 1: direct enumeration or content inspection over a declared, resolved scope.
- Signal 2: a mechanically different source of inventory or authority—for example, a version-control object listing, application API, manifest, or UI query—not a reformatted copy of signal 1.
- Coverage: explain why the searched scope closes the world relevant to the claim; otherwise say “not found in the inspected scope,” not “absent.”

The targeted failure set is an empty result caused by the wrong directory, a dead search path, an unsupported matcher, ignored/hidden paths, a stale index, or an incomplete branch view. The aggregate catch premise and its settling experiments are stated in §3.

#### BROKEN/DISABLED

- Positive control: prove the observation path detects a known-good event/mechanism of the same class.
- Signal 1: direct failure/absence of expected behavior under a controlled trigger.
- Signal 2: independent state or telemetry showing the mechanism was invoked and failed, or was never registered/routed.
- Counterfactual: where safe, drive a known-good input through the same path.

This distinguishes “mechanism did not fire” from “detector did not record it”—the central disambiguation demanded by the unit brief.

#### COMPLETE/FIXED

- Positive control: the verifier demonstrably detects a seeded or known failing condition.
- Signal 1: direct postcondition test after the change.
- Signal 2: independent artifact/state inspection, or a second test that exercises a different path.
- Freshness: receipts must postdate the last relevant mutation.

This catches success exit codes with skipped tests, a command that ran against the wrong artifact, a write acknowledged but not persisted, or a stale pre-change test result.

#### EXCLUSIVE CAUSE

- Positive control: the diagnostic method can reveal a known alternative cause.
- Signal 1: evidence that the proposed cause is present and causally relevant.
- Signal 2: intervention or counterfactual evidence—remove/alter the cause and observe the effect—or a mechanically independent causal trace.
- Coverage: enumerate plausible alternative cause classes and state which remain open.

This is intentionally demanding. In many agent tasks the safe result will be “a supported cause” rather than “the only root cause.”

## 3. What the interlock catches that a two-probe prompt alone misses

1. **Detector blindness.** A positive control tests the observation channel before negative evidence is trusted.
2. **Pseudo-redundancy.** The common-cause registry rejects two wrappers over the same executable, two reads of the same cached index, or two summaries derived from one raw output.
3. **Scope leakage.** The micro-case ties claims to a resolved root, revision/state identity, and time; “not found here” cannot silently become “does not exist anywhere.”
4. **Evidence/claim mismatch.** GSN-style directness and relevance reject process proxies such as “the command returned zero” as proof that a postcondition holds.
5. **Incomplete closed-world reasoning.** The coverage field makes “all/none” claims expose why the inspected inventory is complete.
6. **Stale completion evidence.** Receipt ordering prevents a pre-change test from proving a post-change state.
7. **Fabricated verification narratives.** Host-issued receipt lookup ensures the evidence block points to actual observed tool results. It cannot guarantee the interpretation is correct, but it prevents evidence that never occurred from satisfying the gate.
8. **Barrier self-failure.** A hook failure cannot silently authorize `VERIFIED`; only an inline `UNVERIFIED` output may fail open.
9. **Infinite assurance loops.** STPA’s “applied too long” category forces a stop-loop guard and a test that valid evidence is eventually released.

It is **very likely (80-90%)**, based on the direct relationship between each item and a deterministic schema check, that the interlock would detect missing controls, same-family duplicate receipts, stale ordering, absent scope, and nonexistent receipts. It is only **likely (55-80%)**, based on the need for a correct hand-authored dependency registry, to detect subtler common-cause dependence. Both are **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiments are respectively schema contract tests and the common-cause mutation suite described above.**

## 4. Adversarial transfer analysis

### 4.1 Why the analogy does transfer

The source and target share four structural properties:

1. **A controller acts on an internal model, not the world directly.** STPA explicitly describes unsafe action arising when process-model beliefs diverge from reality; an agent likewise converts tool feedback into a belief and then a report.
2. **Feedback can be missing, delayed, misleading, or misinterpreted without an obvious component crash.** The STPA Handbook’s feedback taxonomy maps directly to empty, truncated, stale, mis-scoped, or semantically misunderstood tool results.
3. **The consequential object is an action in context.** “No matches” is not itself the loss. Releasing a categorical “absent” claim, in a context where the owner will act on it, is the hazardous control action.
4. **Redundancy is vulnerable to common causes.** GSN gives the exact epistemic example of two evidence forms sharing a flawed system model, while NASA requires verification that common causes do not invalidate independence.

Those correspondences are about causal structure rather than surface vocabulary. That is why the borrow is stronger than merely renaming checks “barriers.”

### 4.2 Where the analogy breaks

The transfer is not complete; extending it further would violate the structural boundaries below.

**First, safety engineering controls physical or socio-technical losses; the harness controls epistemic confidence.** A safety barrier can often be assigned a physical failure probability or verified against bounded requirements. Open-ended agent claims frequently have no closed world and no measurable truth predicate. Two negative probes cannot prove universal absence when the scope is intrinsically open.

**Second, mechanical independence is easier to name than to establish.** Different tools may share an OS API, cache, permission boundary, dataset, model-generated query, or erroneous scope assumption. The GSN standard itself warns that apparently multiple evidence strands can share a flawed model. Therefore the registry can reject known dependencies but cannot certify philosophical independence.

**Third, the agent is simultaneously claimant, evidence selector, and argument author.** Full IV&V uses technical and organizational separation. A hook that validates receipts supplies some technical separation, but it is not independent judgment. A second instance of the same model is **likely (55-80%)**, based on its shared training, prompt, context, and evidence inputs, to retain correlated reasoning failure modes. This correlation premise is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is a blinded disagreement study comparing same-model replication with mechanically diverse host probes on the replay benchmark.**

**Fourth, natural language is not a stable control interface.** A deterministic hook can miss synonyms, implications, tables, or claims split across prose. Requiring explicit claim markers improves observability but depends on model compliance. An LLM classifier introduces the same class of semantic detector that this unit is trying to constrain; any recall advantage is **UNVERIFIED, and the settling experiment is the red-team semantic-evasion suite in Experiment 5, comparing deterministic markers with an LLM classifier while separately scoring false releases.**

**Fifth, barriers have costs and can create hazards.** False blocks consume time, can cause repeated probes, and can induce the agent to avoid clear language. A broken Stop hook can loop. STPA itself requires examining unsafe provision, non-provision, timing, and duration of the control action; the gate must be analyzed the same way.

**Sixth, the Swiss-cheese metaphor does not calculate sufficiency.** It explains how aligned weaknesses defeat layers but does not say that two layers are enough or how independent they are. The mandatory two-signal rule is a policy floor, not a theorem of truth.

**Seventh, “normal accidents” is mainly a caution, not an implementation mechanism here.** Perrow’s framework analyzes risk along the dimensions of complex versus linear interactions and tight versus loose coupling, and the publisher’s description notes his argument that added precautions can themselves add complexity and create new accident categories ([Princeton University Press, *Normal Accidents*](https://cart.press.princeton.edu/normal-accidents-pb.html)). The design constraint derived here is a visible, deterministic release check rather than a coupled assurance subsystem. A wholesale safety-management program, probabilistic-risk model, or STAMP service is excluded by this unit’s anti-overfit gate.

### 4.3 Boundary of the conclusion

The evidence supports borrowing a **constraint pattern**, not claiming safety certification. Passing the CRI means only: “the protected claim has the configured minimum evidence structure and no registered common-cause violation.” It does not mean the claim is true, complete outside its declared scope, or safe for every downstream decision.

The narrow transfer verdict is **very likely (80-90%)**, based on the explicit source-field correspondence above, to remain valid even if the eventual hook implementation is rejected. Whether CRI improves net harness reliability after false blocks and added tool calls is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is a randomized shadow-mode/release-mode evaluation with the metrics below.**

## 5. Safety constraints for the proposed barrier

Applying STPA to the new interlock prevents the remedy from becoming a new single point of failure:

- **SC-1:** The agent must not label a protected claim `VERIFIED` without a positive control, two target signals, explicit scope/coverage, valid receipts, and no registered disqualifying common cause.
- **SC-2:** The interlock must not convert evidence-format compliance into a claim of truth; its release message must state the limited guarantee.
- **SC-3:** A barrier execution failure must not silently release a protected categorical claim as verified.
- **SC-4:** A valid claim must not be blocked repeatedly. The hook must recognize `stop_hook_active`/an equivalent retry marker and cap corrective cycles.
- **SC-5:** Evidence must be fresh relative to the last relevant state mutation.
- **SC-6:** The positive control must exercise the same detector and materially comparable scope; an unrelated “known-present” item does not validate the probe.
- **SC-7:** Two signals derived from the same raw observation or common authoritative source count as one unless the causal scenario specifically shows their remaining failure modes are distinct.
- **SC-8:** When the world is not closed, the harness must narrow the claim to the inspected scope or label it inline `UNVERIFIED` and name the experiment that would close it.
- **SC-9:** The barrier must expose its own decision and missing evidence to the transcript so the owner can audit why a claim was downgraded or blocked.

## 6. Evaluation and falsification

No deployment recommendation should rest on analogy alone. The following experiments are designed to disprove the proposal cheaply.

### Experiment 1 — historical counterfactual replay

Construct a frozen benchmark containing the six-plus known wrong single-probe conclusions, a stratified sample of owner-correction candidates, and an equal or larger set of correct categorical conclusions. Label claim type, actual truth, scope, original probes, shared failure causes, and whether the correction was consequential. Replay only the claim/evidence transcript slice through the CRI.

Measure:

- recall on wrong categorical conclusions;
- false-block rate on correct conclusions;
- fraction safely downgraded rather than endlessly retried;
- extra tool calls and wall time;
- protected-claim coverage (how many consequential corrections the taxonomy recognizes);
- rate at which two “independent” signals actually share a labeled common cause.

Pre-register acceptance thresholds before seeing results. Candidate thresholds are **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment begins with a cost review by the owner to set acceptable false-block and latency budgets rather than importing safety-industry tolerances into a coding harness.**

### Experiment 2 — detector mutation campaign

Inject one defect at a time: wrong root, empty permission-filtered directory, stale cache, truncated output, unsupported pattern, command alias to the same executable, dead endpoint, success status with skipped work, post-test mutation, and a broken positive-control detector. Require each mutation to prevent `VERIFIED` for the affected case. Then inject paired failures to find common causes that defeat both nominal signals.

The key output is not just mutation score; it is an updated dependency registry recording which pairs failed together.

### Experiment 3 — shadow mode

Run CRI without blocking. Record which claims it would stop, the missing case elements, and subsequent owner corrections. Shadow mode avoids turning an unvalidated safety barrier into a production dependency.

A shadow phase is **very likely (80-90%)**, based on ordinary staged-control practice and the barrier’s uncertain false-positive rate, to expose taxonomy and receipt-parser defects at lower operational cost than immediate enforcement. This operational benefit is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is the shadow phase itself, compared with the immediately preceding baseline on protected-claim corrections and turn latency.**

### Experiment 4 — limited release mode

Enable blocking only for `ABSENT`, `BROKEN`, and `COMPLETE` claims in tasks where a wrong conclusion would change owner action. Keep other classes in shadow mode. Add a kill switch and a deterministic loop cap. Promote a class only after its replay and shadow-mode results meet the pre-registered budget.

### Experiment 5 — adversarial red team of the case format

Try to pass the hook with:

- fabricated receipt IDs;
- two receipts pointing to one tool output;
- two commands resolving to one executable;
- a positive control outside the claimed root;
- a target signal predating the last write;
- scoped evidence paired with an unscoped prose claim;
- an indirect process claim (“command succeeded”) substituted for a state claim;
- a paraphrased protected claim with no marker;
- a hook crash and malformed JSON;
- a valid case followed by contradictory prose.

The paraphrase and contradiction attacks are expected to be the hardest because they require semantic matching. A deterministic, marker-based implementation is **likely (55-80%)**, based on the instability of unrestricted language matching and the availability of explicit structured records, to trade some recall for auditable precision. It is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is the red-team suite, reporting semantic evasion separately from receipt-validation failures.** Do not quietly add an LLM judge to raise recall; that would recreate the single-unvalidated-detector problem unless independently controlled.

## 7. Rollout decision rule

Proceed with the BUILD-SMALL interlock only if replay plus shadow mode show all of the following:

1. It catches a meaningful share of consequential wrong categorical claims, including the known single-probe cases.
2. Correct claims are rarely blocked beyond one corrective cycle under the owner’s predeclared cost budget.
