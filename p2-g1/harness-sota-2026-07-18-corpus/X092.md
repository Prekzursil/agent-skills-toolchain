# X092 — Cross-domain borrow: mechanism design → observability without a baseline

## Executive verdict

The useful borrow is **the full-support decision market with a decision scoring rule**, not a generic prediction market and not “Goodhart's law” as a slogan. In its own field, this mechanism addresses a principal who must choose one action, can observe the outcome only for the chosen action, and wants truthful conditional forecasts for every action. It requires every action to retain nonzero selection probability and scales the score for the realized action by its selection probability. Chen, Kash, Ruberry, and Shnayder state the central result directly: only the selected action's outcome is verifiable, deterministic choice distorts incentives, strictly proper decision markets require the decision maker to risk every action, and reducing that exploration increases worst-case loss ([Harvard repository record and abstract](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-a5f7-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b); [open full paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/DM_full_version.pdf)).

This maps unusually well to a harness rollout. “Old harness” and “candidate harness” are actions; success, correction, cost, latency, and incident outcomes are observed only for the version actually used on a live task; and the person advocating a change can affect which version is selected and which metric is emphasized. A small **Decision Trial Ledger** can pre-register conditional forecasts, randomly route comparable task units with nonzero probability to both versions, log the probability of each assignment, and report both (a) proper scores for forecasts and (b) a randomized treatment-versus-control contrast.

The conclusion is deliberately narrower than “mechanism design solves baseline-free evaluation.” It does not. Proper scoring solves truthful probabilistic elicitation under explicit assumptions. Full-support randomization ensures that each action sometimes becomes observable. Causal attribution comes from the randomized experiment, a neighboring field: controlled experiments compare treatment and control, and Kohavi et al. describe randomized online experiments as a way to evaluate changes rather than relying on intuition ([survey and practical guide](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10618-008-0114-1)). The borrow is valuable because decision-market theory explains **why exploration and scoring must be designed together when forecasts influence rollout**, and why a deterministic “ship the predicted winner” policy destroys information about rejected alternatives.

My bottom-line confidence is **likely (55-80%)**, based on the exact structural match between action-contingent observability in the decision-market paper and version-contingent harness outcomes, reduced from “very likely” because task heterogeneity, interference, delayed owner judgments, and weak incentives can defeat the formal assumptions. The implementation fit is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is a 50-task shadow-mode dry run that validates assignment, logging, stable outcome definitions, and zero cross-arm contamination before any live treatment receives material traffic**.

## 1. The target problem, stated precisely

“No baseline” can mean at least three different deficits:

1. There is no clean historical before-period.
2. There is no contemporaneous control version.
3. There is no trustworthy ground-truth label for whether a task outcome was good.

The proposed borrow addresses (1) by creating a contemporaneous comparison, and addresses (2) by deliberately retaining the old harness as a randomized control. It does **not** solve (3). A proper scoring rule needs an outcome that eventually resolves; a decision market only relaxes the requirement that outcomes for *all* actions resolve. Treating peer agreement as ground truth would answer a different question.

That distinction matters. A ledger can establish that candidate configuration B caused fewer owner corrections than current configuration A during the trial. It cannot establish that “owner corrections” fully represent harness value. Manheim and Garrabrant distinguish regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial forms of Goodhart failure; their point is that proxy breakdown is not one phenomenon and becomes more important under stronger optimization ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.04585)). Therefore the mechanism must preserve raw outcome dimensions and owner adjudication rather than collapse everything immediately into a target score.

## 2. The mechanism in its own field

### 2.1 Strictly proper scoring rules

A scoring rule pays a forecaster as a function of a reported probability distribution and the event that later occurs. It is **proper** when reporting the forecaster's actual belief maximizes expected score, and **strictly proper** when that optimum is unique. Gneiting and Raftery give the general theory and characterize proper scoring rules; their review also connects proper scoring, entropy, and forecast evaluation ([JASA paper](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf), DOI [10.1198/016214506000001437](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437)).

For a binary outcome `y ∈ {0,1}` and report `p`, a convenient bounded loss is the Brier loss:

```text
L(p, y) = (p - y)^2
```

If the forecaster believes `Pr(y=1)=q`, expected loss is uniquely minimized at `p=q`. This is an incentive statement about reporting beliefs, not a claim that the belief is accurate. A forecaster can be honest and badly informed.

The formal incentive also depends on utility. Standard scoring rules assume the participant maximizes expected score, commonly modeled as risk neutrality and no relevant outside stake. Experimental and theoretical work warns that risk aversion and outside stakes can distort reports; Othman and Sandholm explicitly assume a risk-neutral, decision-agnostic expert who cares about the action and outcome only through compensation ([AAMAS 2010 paper, model on pp. 1-2](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~sandholm/www/decision%20rules%20and%20decision%20markets.AAMAS10.pdf)).

### 2.2 Why ordinary scoring fails when a forecast chooses the action

Suppose a principal asks for `P(success | action A)` and `P(success | action B)`, selects the larger report, and scores only the chosen action. The unchosen outcome is counterfactual and never resolves. The cited construction proves that a forecaster can benefit by manipulating which action is chosen when the forecaster has more scoreable information about one action. Chen et al. use essentially this developer-hiring example: conditional markets for two developers can reward an expert for depressing the forecast of the better developer so that the other, on which the expert has profitable information, is selected ([full paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/DM_full_version.pdf)).

Othman and Sandholm prove stronger negative results for deterministic decision rules. They show that no deterministic decision rule/scoring-rule pair is strictly proper for the complete vector of conditional probabilities, and that standard symmetric rules have undesirable incentives under the natural max rule ([AAMAS 2010 paper](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~sandholm/www/decision%20rules%20and%20decision%20markets.AAMAS10.pdf)). They also show multi-agent decision markets can be manipulable even when agents' probability beliefs are accurate. The lesson is not merely “use a better metric”; the decision policy changes the reporting game.

### 2.3 The full-support decision-scoring construction

Chen et al.'s mechanism has these elements:

- For every action `a`, forecasters report a conditional distribution `P_a` over outcomes.
- The decision rule outputs a distribution `d` over actions, not a deterministic winner.
- **Full support** is required: `d_a > 0` for every action that the mechanism claims to elicit information about.
- One action `a*` is drawn, and only its outcome `o*` is observed.
- The decision scoring rule incorporates `d_a`; the intuitive construction scales the ordinary proper score for the realized action by `1/d_a`. In expectation, multiplication by the probability of selecting that action cancels the inverse probability, so each action's conditional forecast contributes as if it were always scoreable.

For an ordinary score `s(P_a,o)`, the core expectation is:

```text
E[ 1{A=a} × s(P_a,O_a) / d_a ]
= d_a × E[s(P_a,O_a) | A=a] / d_a
= E[s(P_a,O_a) | A=a].
```

This is the mechanism-design reason exploration cannot be zero. If `d_a=0`, no realized observation can score the conditional forecast for action `a`. If `d_a` is tiny, `1/d_a` makes the score—and its variance or worst-case liability—large. Chen et al. prove the corresponding tradeoff: full support is necessary for their strict-properness result, and reducing random-action risk increases worst-case loss ([Harvard publication record](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-a5f7-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)).

This does not require money. The proposed harness translation uses score as reputational calibration feedback, a promotion-gate input, or simply a forced forecast ledger. Without a payoff the formal incentive-compatibility guarantee disappears; the remaining benefit is disciplined pre-registration and measurable calibration. That reduced claim is **very likely (80-90%)**, based on the algebra above and on the fact that a timestamped forecast prevents retrospective probability editing, but it is not a proof that humans will care about the score.

## 3. Concrete translation to an agent harness

### Mapping

| Mechanism-design object | Harness object |
|---|---|
| Principal | Harness owner / rollout authority |
| Action `a` | Current harness A or candidate harness B |
| Conditional outcome `O_a` | Task result had A or B processed that task |
| Expert report `P_a` | Change advocate's pre-rollout probability distribution for success/correction/incident under each version |
| Decision rule `d` | Randomized task router with logged assignment probabilities |
| Full support | Neither current nor candidate gets zero traffic during an active trial |
| Realized-action score | Brier/log score for the version actually assigned, weighted by assignment probability if probabilities vary |
| Principal's utility | Owner-defined outcome vector and explicit promotion rule |
| Counterfactual | The unobserved result for the version that did not receive a particular task |

The correspondence is **very likely (80-90%)** strong at the action-observation level, based on the decision-market setup cited in Section 2 and the ordinary live-task constraint: one task is normally completed under only one live harness without doubling owner time, so only one action-contingent outcome is observed. Across repeated randomly assigned tasks, outcomes from both arms become observable. Confidence is not “almost certain” because duplicated execution, shared state, and owner adjudication can change the unit of observation.

## 4. Proposal — Decision Trial Ledger (UNVERIFIED: settle with a 50-task shadow dry run, then a powered low-risk A/A test before A/B)

It has four responsibilities: validate a trial manifest, make or record a randomized assignment, append an immutable JSONL event, and refuse promotion reports when required fields are missing. A separate short analysis command can read the JSONL and emit the trial report; it can remain part of the same sub-200-line component if the existing bus exposes assignment and completion events compactly.

The sub-200-line fit is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is a spike that implements only manifest validation, deterministic seeded assignment, and JSONL append, with line count and event coverage measured on the actual bus**. Consequently the recommendation's heading carries the UNVERIFIED tag. If the spike exceeds the threshold because the bus lacks stable task identifiers or completion events, stop rather than grow it into a daemon.

### 4.1 Pre-register a trial manifest

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only).**

Before routing starts, require a small manifest such as:

```json
{
  "trial_id": "...",
  "control_digest": "...",
  "treatment_digest": "...",
  "unit": "top_level_owner_task",
  "eligible_strata": ["coding", "research", "operations"],
  "assignment": {"control": 0.50, "treatment": 0.50, "seed_commitment": "..."},
  "primary_outcome": "owner_accepts_without_correction_within_24h",
  "guardrails": ["destructive_incident", "secrets_exposure", "task_abandonment"],
  "diagnostics": ["wall_time", "tool_errors", "tokens", "retries"],
  "forecasts": {
    "control_primary_probability": 0.62,
    "treatment_primary_probability": 0.70,
    "treatment_guardrail_probability": 0.01
  },
  "minimum_sample": 100,
  "stop_rule": "...",
  "promotion_rule": "..."
}
```

Store content digests for both harness variants and the manifest. Do not permit edits after the first assignment; amendments create a new trial ID. This catches outcome switching, silent treatment mutation, and the common narrative move from “the primary metric improved” to a different metric after results arrive. It is **very likely (80-90%)** to catch those provenance failures because the detection is a direct comparison of committed fields and digests, not a model judgment.

The manifest must keep one primary outcome, a short guardrail set, and raw diagnostics separate. Do not optimize a weighted mega-score. This is a Goodhart defense: multiple raw dimensions make tradeoffs visible, while a single public scalar invites compensating a serious regression with cheap gains elsewhere. It is **likely (55-80%)** to reduce metric gaming, based on Campbell's warning that decision-linked indicators face corruption pressure ([original 1976 paper reprint](https://www.humanlearning.systems/uploads/08%20Assessing%20the%20Impact%20of%20Planned%20Social%20Change.pdf)) and the Goodhart taxonomy, but it cannot prevent gaming of every disclosed dimension.

### 4.2 Use full-support randomized routing

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).**

Assign at the top-level owner-task unit, using a committed seed and a stable task ID. Stratify before randomization by broad task class or risk tier. Log `trial_id`, task hash, stratum, arm, assignment probability, version digest, timestamp, and any override reason. Never silently reroute a failed treatment task to control and then count the control result as assigned; report intention-to-treat and crossover separately.

Start with A/A routing—both arms use the current harness—to validate that assignment is balanced, outcomes are captured equally, and the analyzer does not invent a difference. Only then make one arm the candidate. The A/A negative control plus known-present synthetic events provide the two mechanically independent signals required before claiming the detector works: (1) assignment balance and null effect from real A/A traffic; (2) successful detection of injected manifest mutation, missing completion, and known arm imbalance. Re-running the same probe is not counted twice.

For low-risk reversible changes, 50/50 gives information fastest. For changes with plausible material downside, lower treatment exposure but keep it nonzero and use explicit safety stops. The safe initial probability is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is a risk-tier simulation using historical task classes and synthetic incident rates, followed by owner approval of maximum exposure before live routing**. No universal 5%, 10%, or 50% value is defensible from mechanism-design theory alone.

This component is **likely (55-80%)** to catch regressions that a before/after dashboard misses—especially time drift, workload-mix changes, and cherry-picked rollout windows—because both arms operate contemporaneously under randomized assignment. Confidence is below 80% because low-volume heterogeneous tasks may remain imbalanced by chance.

### 4.3 Score forecasts, but estimate effects separately

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).**

For each arm's binary forecast, calculate Brier loss. If assignment probabilities change adaptively, calculate the pre-specified inverse-probability decision score using the logged probability. Report calibration by forecaster and change class over many trials; never promote a harness solely because its advocate had a good score.

Separately estimate the randomized arm contrast for each outcome. With fixed assignment probabilities, report arm means and their difference with uncertainty. With unequal or changing probabilities, use the pre-specified randomization-aware estimator and show effective sample size and weight distribution. Do not mistake the inverse-probability **forecast score** for the causal effect estimator; their algebra resembles each other but their questions differ.

This separation catches two failures currently invisible to a simple “metric went up” report:

- **Bad forecast, good change:** the change helped, but the advocate's causal model or confidence was wrong.
- **Good forecast, bad change:** the advocate correctly anticipated a regression or guardrail risk; shipping despite that warning is a decision failure, not a forecast failure.
- **Accurate headline, hidden tail:** mean success improves while incident probability or high-risk strata worsen.
- **Selection-induced optimism:** only promising changes are shipped, so retrospective success among shipped changes is not evidence that rejected changes were worse.
- **Probability laundering:** “I knew this would work” becomes testable against the timestamped probability.

The ledger is **very likely (80-90%)** to expose probability laundering and post-hoc outcome switching because those are provenance checks. It is **likely (55-80%)** to expose heterogeneous harm because success depends on enough observations in each predeclared stratum. It is **unlikely (20-45%)** to estimate rare catastrophic incident rates precisely in ordinary trial sizes; the evidence is simple base-rate arithmetic, and the settling experiment is a power calculation using the maximum tolerable incident probability before trial launch.

### 4.4 Promotion must remain an owner decision

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only).**

Use the trial report as a decision input. The report should state: assignment integrity; exclusions; missing outcomes; primary and guardrail effects; uncertainty; forecast scores; crossover; version digests; and whether pre-registered stopping and promotion rules were met. It must not auto-promote the candidate.

This is a deliberate limit on the analogy. In a textbook mechanism the principal's utility can be encoded. In an owner-operated harness, utility includes unmodeled qualities such as trust, cognitive burden, reversibility, and the severity of a surprising failure. Automatic promotion would turn the measurement rule into the optimization target and invite causal or adversarial Goodhart effects. Keeping promotion human-reviewed is **likely (55-80%)** to preserve scrutiny of unmodeled costs, based on the proxy-failure taxonomy, but may introduce human inconsistency; the ledger should record the final decision and rationale without pretending it is an objective label.

## 5. What this would catch that baseline-free observation misses

1. **Secular drift mistaken for treatment effect.** A historical after-period may contain easier tasks, a model-provider change, or a quieter workload. Simultaneous randomized arms make those shared conditions approximately common. Catch likelihood: **likely (55-80%)**, based on randomized online-experiment practice, conditional on adequate sample and no interference.

2. **Regression hidden by treatment selection.** If candidate B is used only on tasks expected to suit it, its raw success rate is incomparable with A's. Logged randomization exposes the selection policy. Catch likelihood: **very likely (80-90%)** for undocumented routing differences because assignment logs and version digests are direct signals.

3. **Unscored counterfactual claims.** “The old harness would have failed here” is normally unfalsifiable for a completed task. Repeated full-support assignment makes distribution-level claims about both versions resolvable, though never the individual task's missing potential outcome. Catch likelihood: **likely (55-80%)**, based on repeated randomized exposure rather than individual counterfactual recovery.

4. **Advocate overconfidence.** Proper scores distinguish a calibrated 60% forecast from a theatrical 95% forecast over repeated trials. Catch likelihood: **likely (55-80%)**, conditional on enough forecasts and on advocates valuing recorded calibration.

5. **Metric substitution after failure.** Pre-registration records which outcome was primary. Catch likelihood: **very likely (80-90%)**, because manifest immutability can be verified mechanically.

6. **Global improvement masking subgroup harm.** Predeclared risk/task strata reveal sign reversals. Catch likelihood: **likely (55-80%)**, conditional on powered strata; otherwise the report must say “insufficient information,” not “no harm.”

7. **Observer failure itself.** A/A trials, known-present injected events, missing-event accounting, and dual checks on assignment/outcome capture test the measuring system before it judges the harness. Catch likelihood: **very likely (80-90%)** for the injected failure classes, because each has a known expected result; generalization to unknown detector failures is **unlikely (20-45%)** without additional controls.

## 6. Adversarial transfer audit

### 6.1 Where the structure transfers

- **Action-dependent observability:** both fields observe an outcome only for the action actually taken. This is the strongest isomorphism.
- **Endogenous selection:** a forecast or evaluation can influence which harness version runs, just as a report influences the principal's action.
- **Counterfactual censoring:** deterministic selection leaves rejected alternatives forever unscored.
- **Exploration cost:** testing an inferior action can harm the principal; full support buys information at a real cost.
- **Strategic reporting is plausible:** change advocates face sunk-cost, reputation, and promotion incentives even without monetary bets.

Given these correspondences, the transfer of the **design principle**—nonzero exploration plus probability-aware scoring—is **very likely (80-90%)**, based directly on the models and impossibility results cited above.

### 6.2 Where the structure breaks

1. **A harness is not a risk-neutral expert.** It is **likely (55-80%)** that at least some change advocates care about adoption, aesthetics, ownership, or blame in addition to the ledger score; the evidence is the explicit contrast with the decision-agnostic, risk-neutral-agent assumption in Othman and Sandholm's model ([AAMAS paper](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~sandholm/www/decision%20rules%20and%20decision%20markets.AAMAS10.pdf)), not a measurement of this owner. The premise is **UNVERIFIED; settle it by confidentially eliciting contributor objectives before a trial and comparing forecast behavior when scores are and are not reviewed**. Properness does not survive arbitrary outside utilities. Therefore forecast scores are governance evidence, not a theorem of truthful organizational behavior.

2. **Tasks are not identically distributed coin flips.** Coding, research, and operations tasks have different difficulty and different outcome definitions. Randomization balances them only in expectation. Small samples can be misleading.

3. **Interference is plausible.** A result from treatment may change shared caches, files, owner expectations, or future tasks, violating the clean “one task's arm does not affect another task's outcome” picture. This premise is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is an A/A contamination audit that traces shared writes, cache keys, and follow-on task references by arm**. If cross-arm effects are material, randomize at a larger cluster such as workspace-week, accept lower power, or do not claim a causal estimate.

4. **Outcomes are multidimensional and partially subjective.** Completion, correctness, owner effort, cost, latency, safety, and learning do not reduce cleanly to one event. A strictly proper score can elicit a forecast for a declared event; it cannot declare which event represents value.

5. **The selected outcome may not resolve cleanly.** This is **likely (55-80%)** for at least some real tasks because owner silence is logically distinct from acceptance and defects can appear after an arbitrary 24-hour window. Arm-dependent missingness is **UNVERIFIED; settle it in A/A by comparing label latency and missing-label rates by assigned arm, with synthetic known-present completion controls**.

6. **Full support can be unethical or too costly.** A candidate with credible destructive risk should receive zero live probability. Mechanism theory says truthfulness for that arm is then unavailable; it does not require taking an unsafe action. Use sandbox evaluation or abandon causal identification for that change.

7. **Forecast truth is not outcome truth.** A perfectly scored forecaster may be calibrated about a badly chosen proxy. This is the Goodhart boundary.

Because of these disanalogies, a literal internal prediction market with money, tradable shares, many agents, and automated choice does **not** transfer. Rejecting that larger design is **very likely (80-90%)** correct, based on the cited manipulability results, the mismatch between the formal requirement for decision-agnostic participants and the unverified real participant incentives, and the anti-overfit gate.

## 7. Rejected borrow: peer prediction / Bayesian Truth Serum

Peer prediction and Bayesian Truth Serum are tempting because they explicitly address settings without an independently verifiable truth. Prelec's Bayesian Truth Serum rewards answers that are more common than collectively predicted, and asks respondents both for their answer and their predicted population frequencies ([Science paper](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/cs286r/courses/fall12/papers/Prelec04.pdf); [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15486294/)). Miller, Resnick, and Zeckhauser's peer-prediction method uses a proper score on a report-implied prediction of another rater's report; truthful reporting is a Nash equilibrium under its model ([paper](https://nmiller.web.illinois.edu/documents/research/elicit.pdf)).

Do **not** use either as the primary observability mechanism for harness quality. Multiple agents running the same model family, prompt, tools, and context have correlated errors; agreement can reward shared delusion. The common-prior/Bayesian-updating assumptions in Prelec's model and the signal-model assumptions in peer prediction are not established for agent evaluations. More importantly, the target is objective owner value where eventual behavioral outcomes often exist, not an intrinsically subjective survey answer.

The rejection confidence is **very likely (80-90%)**, based on the source assumptions and the structural risk of shared-stack correlation. The existence and magnitude of that correlation here are **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment would run independently seeded evaluators from genuinely different model/tool stacks against a blinded owner-labeled set and measure conditional error correlation**. Even if correlation were low, peer prediction would remain a supplemental triage signal, not ground truth.

## 8. Anti-overfit gate

This proposal passes the stated anti-overfit gate:

- It rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus.
- It adds a manifest, seeded router/event appender, and offline report.
- It does not add a second orchestration runtime.
- It does not add vector/KG memory, a durable-execution engine, a trained critic, observability SaaS, or a guardrail framework.
- It keeps raw JSONL suitable for deletion and independent inspection.
- It begins with A/A and synthetic controls; if the bus cannot support the design compactly, it stops rather than becoming a service.

A BUILD-LARGE decision-market daemon, forecast exchange, or observability platform is explicitly rejected. The expected incremental epistemic value over a small ledger is **unlikely (20-45%)**, based on decision-market manipulability, limited internal forecaster diversity, and the fact that causal identification comes from assignment rather than market sophistication.

## 9. Minimum settling experiment

The recommendation is not production-ready until this sequence succeeds:

1. Define one low-risk reversible harness change and one binary primary outcome that the owner can actually adjudicate.
2. Implement manifest validation, seeded assignment, version digesting, JSONL append, and report generation on the existing bus.
3. Inject known-present synthetic records for each arm, a missing completion, a digest mutation, and an impossible assignment probability. Confirm the detector finds all four.
4. Run at least 50 eligible tasks as A/A. Verify assignment counts against the committed seed, arm parity in event capture, crossover accounting, and a null-compatible outcome contrast. Do not call an imprecise A/A estimate “no difference.”
5. Audit cross-arm shared state using a mechanically different signal: trace shared file/cache mutations and inspect follow-on task references.
6. Perform a prospective power/safety calculation. Choose sample size, minimum effect of interest, treatment probability, and incident stop before A/B.
7. Run the low-risk A/B. Publish the manifest, event completeness, raw arm summaries, uncertainty, forecasts, scores, deviations, and final owner decision.

Success criterion: the design is worth retaining if it detects the injected faults, produces complete assignment/outcome provenance, and changes at least one rollout belief or decision in a way traceable to contemporaneous evidence. Its chance of meeting that criterion is **likely (55-80%)**, based on the small mechanical scope and established experiment design, but the estimate is intentionally broad because the actual bus and owner-label latency were out of scope for this ideation unit.

## 10. Gaps and limits

- This unit intentionally did not inspect the local harness, per the IDEATION-lens instruction. Therefore hook event availability, stable task identity, version isolation, and actual line count are **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is the bus spike and A/A trial specified above**.
- This unit did not verify whether reputational forecast scoring changes this owner's or contributors' behavior. That incentive response is **UNVERIFIED; settle it by randomizing whether forecasts are merely recorded or periodically reviewed, then compare forecast completeness and calibration over multiple trials**, provided the owner considers that organizational experiment worthwhile.
- No universal task-level success label is proposed. That is a feature, not an omission: the owner must define trial-specific outcomes, and the report must retain raw dimensions.
- Random assignment does not recover the missing potential outcome for an individual task; it estimates distribution-level effects under the trial's eligibility and interference assumptions.
- Rare catastrophic harms cannot safely be learned by live exploration. Exclude them from full-support routing and use sandbox tests, formal constraints, or manual review instead.

## 11. Final answer to the cross-domain question

Mechanism design has already solved a close subproblem: **how to elicit conditional forecasts when the act of choosing makes all unchosen outcomes counterfactual and gives reporters incentives to manipulate the choice**. The solution is a full-support randomized decision rule coupled to a strictly proper, probability-aware decision score. The harness translation is a small Decision Trial Ledger that pre-registers forecasts and outcomes, randomizes current versus candidate versions, records propensities and immutable digests, scores forecast calibration, and separately estimates randomized effects.

The analogy survives because action-dependent observability, endogenous selection, and costly exploration are structurally shared. It stops at causal identification and value definition: randomization, not scoring, estimates improvement; the owner, not the score, defines value. With those boundaries, adoption is **likely (55-80%)** to provide materially better evidence than baseline-free before/after dashboards, based on the decision-market results and controlled-experiment literature. A literal prediction market or peer-consensus truth mechanism is rejected.
