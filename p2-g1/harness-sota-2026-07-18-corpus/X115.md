# X115 — Cross-domain borrow: reliability engineering → observability without a baseline

**Unit:** X115  
**Source field:** reliability engineering (reliability demonstration, reliability growth, fault trees, common-cause failure)  
**Target problem:** deciding whether an agent-harness change actually helped when no baseline exists  
**Lens constraint:** external research and design only; no local-repository grounding was performed.

## Executive verdict

The direct analogy does **not** transfer: no reliability-engineering technique can recover the missing counterfactual and prove that a past harness change *improved* reliability when the old configuration was not measured and cannot be run. Reliability demonstration testing answers a different question—whether a fixed configuration meets an absolute reliability requirement at a stated confidence—and reliability-growth analysis observes a sequence of tested configurations. Neither creates evidence about an unobserved old configuration. This conclusion is **almost certain (90-99%)**, based on the logical need for observations from both configurations for a comparative claim and on NIST's description of reliability-test planning as beginning with the decision, assumed model, test size/time, and acceptable decision risk, rather than inferring a missing comparison ([NIST, “How do you plan a reliability assessment test?”](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/apr/section3/apr31.htm)).

There is, however, a strong and useful *prospective* borrow:

1. Use an **operational-profile-weighted, fixed-configuration reliability demonstration test** to establish an absolute floor even when there is no historical baseline.
2. Preserve the incumbent and candidate configurations and run the same sampled missions against both, producing a paired counterfactual from this point onward.
3. Use a small fault tree and failure-mode catalogue to stratify the trials and expose common-cause failures, but never treat an FMEA score or a fault-tree probability assembled from guesses as evidence that a change helped.
4. Declare the decision rule, failure semantics, workload, confidence, and stopping rule before observing candidate results.

This prospective design is **very likely (80-90%)** to distinguish large harness regressions from large improvements under the represented workload, because paired trials remove task-mix differences and exact binomial bounds quantify sampling uncertainty; it is not 90%+ because agent/model outputs are stochastic, missions are not naturally independent, and the operational profile can be wrong. The settling experiment is a preregistered backtest in which known synthetic regressions of several effect sizes are injected blindly and the evaluator's true-positive and false-positive rates are measured.

The compact mechanism rides the existing evaluation/hook/logging bus and passes the anti-overfit gate. It requires no second orchestration runtime, vector database or knowledge graph, durable-execution engine, trained critic, observability SaaS, or guardrail framework.

## 1. The specific source-field mechanism

### 1.1 Reliability demonstration testing: prove a floor, not improvement

In reliability engineering, a reliability demonstration test (RDT) fixes the item/configuration, defines an operating environment and success criterion, runs a prespecified amount of exposure, and accepts or rejects an absolute reliability requirement with controlled statistical risk. NASA's *Practical Reliability, Volume III* describes reliability demonstration as testing and analysis that supplies evidence that reliability meets a requirement, with a lower one-sided confidence limit and an explicit probability that the statement is wrong ([NASA, *Practical Reliability, Volume III*, §14.1](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19680023307/downloads/19680023307.pdf)). NIST likewise says test planning starts by stating what is to be proved, what model is assumed, and what risk of a wrong conclusion is acceptable ([NIST reliability assessment planning](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/apr/section3/apr31.htm)).

For a repairable system under the homogeneous Poisson/exponential model, NIST gives a zero-failure one-sided lower MTBF bound

\[
\mathrm{MTBF}_{lower}=\frac{T}{-\ln(\alpha)},
\]

where \(T\) is exposure and confidence is \(1-\alpha\). NIST is explicit that zero failures yields only a *lower bound*, not a point proof of perfection ([NIST, constant repair-rate model, “Zero Fails” case](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/apr/section4/apr451.htm)). NIST also warns that few failures contain little information for accurately estimating failure rates or discovering failure modes; the precision depends heavily on the number of failures, not merely a large nominal sample ([NIST, “Lack of failures”](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/apr/section1/apr132.htm)).

For independent binary missions with true pass probability \(R\), observing zero failures in \(n\) trials demonstrates \(R\ge R_0\) at one-sided confidence \(1-\alpha\) when

\[
n \ge \frac{\ln(\alpha)}{\ln(R_0)}.
\]

Illustrative zero-failure budgets at 95% confidence are therefore 299 trials for \(R_0=0.99\), 598 for \(R_0=0.995\), and 2,995 for \(R_0=0.999\) (rounded upward). These are direct binomial calculations, not estimates from a harness corpus. They show an important discipline: “we saw no failures in a demo” is weak evidence unless the exposure was sized against a declared reliability floor.

What RDT solves without a baseline is: **“Does the current fixed configuration clear this absolute bar under this stated workload?”** It does not solve: **“Did the change improve the system relative to its predecessor?”**

### 1.2 Reliability growth: test–analyze–fix–test, with configurations kept distinct

Reliability-growth management repeatedly exposes failure modes, analyzes them, applies corrective action, and retests. The active U.S. Defense handbook describes itself as guidance for managing reliability growth and explicitly says it must be tailored with knowledge of the system and acquisition program ([DLA ASSIST, MIL-HDBK-189, *Reliability Growth Management*](https://quicksearch.dla.mil/qsDocDetails.aspx?ident_number=53928)). The mechanism matters here less as a fitted growth curve than as configuration discipline: a repair restores an item without changing its failure rate, while a fix changes the design and therefore the configuration being assessed. A post-fix reliability claim must identify the exposure accumulated by the post-fix configuration.

This is structurally relevant to harnesses. If a prompt rule, hook, skill, model, tool route, or environment changes during an evaluation window, aggregating the whole window into one “failure rate” silently combines unlike systems. A growth chart can be useful operationally, but fitting a smooth curve does not turn time order into causal evidence. Later trials can differ because tasks, providers, model versions, load, or evaluators changed.

### 1.3 Operational profiles: reliability is conditional on use

Reliability is not a context-free property of software. A NASA report defines software reliability as failure-free operation for a specified time under specified circumstances and notes that circumstances—an operational profile—can materially change observed reliability ([NASA, *Quality Measures and Assurance*](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19880020920/downloads/19880020920.pdf)). A NASA assessment of COTS reliability says the operational profile identifies component criticality, duration, and frequency of use, and determines what to test, with what intensity, and for how long; it also says a component must be tested in both its own profile and that of the integrated system ([NASA, *Issues and Methods for Assessing COTS Reliability*](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000054545/downloads/20000054545.pdf)). NASA research also cautions that development tests chosen to find bugs or cover functions are not necessarily representative of anticipated operational use ([NASA-CR-158960, discussion of “Testing for Software Reliability”](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19790007503/downloads/19790007503.pdf)).

This is the bridge from “a benchmark score” to “harness reliability.” An agent harness can look better on a curated regression suite while becoming worse on the owner's actual mission mix. The benchmark must either sample that mix or state explicitly that it estimates only a synthetic stress profile.

### 1.4 Fault trees and minimal cut sets: enumerate pathways to the top event

Fault-tree analysis starts with an undesired top event and decomposes its immediate necessary/sufficient causes. NASA defines a minimal cut set as the smallest combination of basic events that produces the top event; the set of minimal cut sets represents the distinct ways basic events can cause it ([NASA, *Fault Tree Handbook with Aerospace Applications*, pp. 15-16 and 43-44](https://s3vi.ndc.nasa.gov/ssri-kb/static/resources/Fault%20Tree%20Handbook_NASA.pdf)). Single-event cut sets reveal single points of failure; multi-event cut sets reveal combinations that evade component-local metrics.

The handbook also provides an unusually relevant warning: probabilistic analysis of redundant systems can be “wildly optimistic” if redundant components are assumed independent when they share common causes ([NASA Fault Tree Handbook, p. 118](https://s3vi.ndc.nasa.gov/ssri-kb/static/resources/Fault%20Tree%20Handbook_NASA.pdf)). Its network example uses a functional-dependency gate: one network-interface failure isolates multiple processing nodes ([same handbook, p. 111](https://s3vi.ndc.nasa.gov/ssri-kb/static/resources/Fault%20Tree%20Handbook_NASA.pdf)).

For an agent harness, plausible top-event pathways include:

```text
mission not completed correctly
  OR
  ├── no viable execution path
  │     ├── required provider unavailable
  │     ├── route still selects unavailable provider
  │     └── fallback shares the same credential/network/runtime  [common cause]
  ├── incorrect completion accepted
  │     ├── generated output is wrong
  │     └── verifier observes the same misleading evidence       [common cause]
  ├── execution cannot start
  │     ├── process/argument limit exceeded
  │     └── launcher and fallback use the same launch mechanism  [common cause]
  └── recovery amplifies failure
        ├── retry classifier treats permanent error as transient
        └── repeated attempt consumes remaining budget
```

This tree is illustrative, not a claim about any particular installed harness. Its value is to force whole-path observability: a nominal fallback is not diverse redundancy if it shares the same failure domain.

### 1.5 FMEA is a hypothesis generator, not the baseline

FMEA's bottom-up inventory—failure mode, cause, local/system effect, detection and mitigation—is useful for defining event classes and ensuring trials exercise known pathways. It is not a measured before/after comparison. The NASA Fault Tree Handbook warns that patching FMEAs together does not make a valid fault tree because FMEA does not encode the necessary relationships among causes; it says FMEA can instead help check completeness or decide where a fault tree is needed ([NASA Fault Tree Handbook, pp. 66-67](https://s3vi.ndc.nasa.gov/ssri-kb/static/resources/Fault%20Tree%20Handbook_NASA.pdf)).

Therefore an RPN, severity score, or expert-ranked FMEA must not become the outcome metric. It can decide *what to instrument and stress*. Only observed outcomes under declared exposure support a reliability claim.

## 2. Concrete translation to an agent harness

### Proposal A [UNVERIFIED — configuration capture; mutation experiment below] — Frozen configuration identity and exposure ledger — **BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)**

Add one compact run-envelope record at mission start and one outcome record at mission end. The record should contain:

- opaque `configuration_id`, deterministically derived from model identifier, harness rules/settings, enabled hook/skill/plugin manifests, tool-routing table, and evaluator version;
- `mission_id`, workload stratum, random seed where controllable, start/end time, attempt count, and resource exposure (tool calls, tokens, wall time);
- terminal outcome from a small closed taxonomy: `pass`, `wrong`, `aborted`, `blocked_external`, `budget_exhausted`, `evaluator_unknown`;
- failure-mode codes and the earliest observed causal boundary, while retaining raw evidence pointers;
- environment/provider version identifiers when supplied by those systems.

Never merge observations across different `configuration_id` values into a single reliability estimate. Display configuration boundaries as discontinuities, not as a smooth trend. This is a direct translation of fixed-configuration demonstration and reliability-growth configuration control.

It is **very likely (80-90%)** that this will catch false “improvements” caused by mixing pre-change and post-change exposure, because explicit configuration identity makes that aggregation mechanically detectable. Evidence: RDT applies to a specified configuration and NIST requires assumptions and decision goals to be fixed in the test plan ([NIST test planning](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/apr/section3/apr31.htm)); uncertainty remains because some upstream changes may not expose version identifiers. **UNVERIFIED:** whether every relevant model/provider/runtime version can be captured. Settling experiment: replay two deliberately different configurations, mutate each known input dimension one at a time, and require the identifier to change; separately change irrelevant timestamps and require it to remain stable.

### Proposal B — Absolute reliability demonstration card — **BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)**

For each release candidate, generate a machine-readable and human-readable card containing:

1. fixed `configuration_id`;
2. top event and exact success/failure rubric;
3. declared target (for example, mission pass probability at least \(R_0\));
4. declared confidence and allowed failures;
5. operational profile and explicit exclusions;
6. planned sample/exposure and stopping rule;
7. observed failures, exposure, exact one-sided confidence bound, and failure-mode counts;
8. verdict: `demonstrated`, `not demonstrated`, or `invalidated by protocol deviation`.

The key wording is “demonstrated a floor under profile P,” never “proved reliable” and never “improved,” unless a valid comparative trial also exists. If zero failures occur, show the lower bound; do not show 100% as the estimated truth. NIST's exact zero-failure MTBF treatment supports precisely this discipline ([NIST constant repair-rate confidence bounds](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/apr/section4/apr451.htm)). For binary missions, use an exact binomial bound rather than an exponential MTBF model unless mission exposure genuinely satisfies the latter's assumptions.

This will **almost certainly (90-99%)** catch underpowered “0 failures, therefore fixed” claims when the declared floor and confidence imply more trials than were run, because the calculation is deterministic once the protocol is fixed. It will not catch unrepresented workloads.

### Proposal C — Prospective paired incumbent/candidate replay — **BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)**

The phrase “did the change help?” requires a comparator. From now on:

1. Freeze the incumbent before applying the candidate.
2. Draw missions from the same operational-profile snapshot.
3. Execute each mission under both configurations, randomizing order to reduce time/provider-load effects.
4. Keep external artifacts immutable or snapshot them when authorized; otherwise record that the pair was contaminated.
5. Score both outputs with the same blinded rubric/evaluator version.
6. Report the four paired outcomes: both pass, incumbent-only pass, candidate-only pass, both fail. Improvement evidence lives in the asymmetry between candidate-only and incumbent-only wins, not in the raw two percentages alone.
7. Report cost and latency separately; do not collapse correctness, cost, and latency into a single opaque score.

This is “baseline creation,” not “baseline recovery.” If the old configuration still runs, the baseline is absent historically but can be measured now. If it cannot run, causal improvement remains unknowable; only Proposal B's absolute demonstration is available.

The paired design is **very likely (80-90%)** to reduce task-mix confounding compared with independent before/after windows because each mission is its own control. **UNVERIFIED:** stochastic provider state, mutable web data, rate limits, and cache effects may still make paired executions nonexchangeable. Settling experiment: repeat order-randomized A→B and B→A pairs across time blocks; test whether win direction depends materially on order or block. If it does, report stratified effects rather than one pooled number.

### Proposal D [UNVERIFIED — future-profile validity; four-week experiment below] — Operational profile plus stress annex — **CONFIG (settings/JSON only)**

Maintain two explicitly separate distributions:

- **Operational profile:** weighted mission strata estimating owner use, such as editing, diagnosis, research, build/test, multi-tool workflows, and long-context work. Weights are versioned and accompanied by effective dates and source counts.
- **Stress annex:** deliberately overweighted rare/high-severity cases selected from the failure-mode catalogue and fault tree: provider loss, malformed structured output, launch limits, quoting/path edge cases, evaluator disagreement, exhausted budgets, and fallback/common-dependency failures.

Never average these into one unlabeled score. The operational score estimates performance under the sampled use profile; the annex estimates resilience to named perturbations. NASA's software-reliability literature supports conditioning reliability on operational circumstances and warns that ordinary development tests need not represent operation ([NASA *Quality Measures and Assurance*](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19880020920/downloads/19880020920.pdf); [NASA-CR-158960](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19790007503/downloads/19790007503.pdf)).

This is **likely (55-80%)** to catch benchmark overfitting and rare but damaging failure paths that a frequency-weighted suite misses. The confidence is lower because the operational weights and stress cases can become stale. **UNVERIFIED:** the proposed strata approximate future owner work. Settling experiment: for four weeks, assign incoming missions to the frozen strata without changing weights; compare predicted and observed stratum frequencies and failure-mode coverage, then publish total-variation error and uncategorized share before revising the profile.

### Proposal E — Fault-tree coverage and common-cause drills — **BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)**

Store the top event and a shallow set of minimal failure paths as ordinary versioned data, not a new graph service. For each leaf/path, record:

- detector and known-positive control;
- test or fault-injection scenario;
- whether the scenario reached the leaf, whether the top event occurred, and whether recovery worked;
- dependencies shared by primary and fallback paths;
- last observed operational occurrence and last synthetic exercise.

At release time, report *coverage of named cut sets*, not a guessed probability of the top event. Explicitly drill shared-dependency failures: same credential, network path, process launcher, evaluator/model family, context window, filesystem, or routing table. NASA's warning that independence assumptions make redundant systems look wildly optimistic is the source-field justification ([NASA Fault Tree Handbook, p. 118](https://s3vi.ndc.nasa.gov/ssri-kb/static/resources/Fault%20Tree%20Handbook_NASA.pdf)).

This is **likely (55-80%)** to reveal false redundancy where fallback paths share a failure domain, because the test deliberately removes the shared dependency. **UNVERIFIED:** synthetic failure injection predicts recovery during real provider/runtime incidents. Settling experiment: compare outcomes from injected incidents with naturally occurring incidents for the same failure-mode codes; require agreement in route selected, recovery state transitions, and terminal outcome before treating injection coverage as operational evidence.

### Proposal F — Sequential test–analyze–fix ledger, not a smooth “growth” claim — **BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)**

For every discovered failure mode, append:

`observed configuration → evidence → cause status → corrective action → new configuration → targeted recurrence test → fixed-profile demonstration result`.

Distinguish:

- **repair:** retry/restart made this mission continue but did not change future failure propensity;
- **fix:** configuration/design changed and was separately retested;
- **containment:** impact was limited but initiating failure remained;
- **unknown:** evidence does not isolate the cause.

Do not fit a reliability-growth curve by default. Agent missions violate the simplest growth-model assumptions: the workload changes, software failures are input-triggered rather than wear-out events, fixes can introduce new failure modes, and model/provider versions drift. A curve can be added only after its operational-profile and stochastic-process assumptions pass a documented goodness-of-fit check; NIST says goodness-of-fit tests determine whether observed data are consistent with an assumed reliability model and control rejection risk ([NIST, reliability-model goodness-of-fit](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/apr/section2/apr232.htm)).

The ledger is **very likely (80-90%)** to catch “retry succeeded, therefore fixed” and “targeted test passed, therefore globally improved” category errors, because repair, targeted recurrence, and fixed-profile demonstration become separate fields. It cannot by itself prove root cause.

## 3. What this catches that baseline-free dashboards miss

| Missed condition | Reliability-engineering lens | Observable evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate has zero observed failures only because the test was tiny | Reliability demonstration sample/exposure planning | Lower confidence bound fails the declared target |
| Apparent improvement is merely an easier later task mix | Operational profile + paired configuration comparison | Paired discordance disagrees with raw window averages |
| Failures vanish from a pooled trend after configuration changes | Fixed-configuration accounting | Configuration boundary invalidates pooled estimate |
| Retry restores one run but recurrence risk is unchanged | Repair-versus-fix distinction | Same failure mode recurs under unchanged configuration |
| Two fallbacks fail together | Common-cause analysis | Shared dependency appears in a minimal cut set and fails under drill |
| Component/tool metrics look healthy while missions fail | Top-event fault tree | Multi-event cut set reaches mission-level failure |
| Benchmark improves but owner work regresses | Operational profile | Weighted operational score and stress/benchmark score diverge |
| No failures are observed and dashboard reports 100% | Censoring/confidence bounds | One-sided lower bound exposes residual uncertainty |
| “Fix verified” means only the original example passed | Test–analyze–fix plus fixed-profile demonstration | Targeted recurrence test passes but release demonstration does not |
| Instrumentation silently stops observing a class | Detector with known-positive control in each fault-tree leaf | Control fails, so absence is reported as detector invalidity, not zero events |

The final row deliberately imports the program's two-signal honesty rule into the design. A detector's “zero” is accepted only when a mechanically separate known-positive control proves the detector can see a present event. The control and operational observation are different signals; repeating the same query is not.

## 4. Adversarial analysis of the analogy

### 4.1 Structural assumptions that transfer

**Explicit top event and environment transfer well.** Both engineered equipment and a harness are systems whose reliability statement is meaningless without a function, interval/mission, and operating conditions. This transfer is **almost certain (90-99%)**, supported by NASA's definition tying software reliability to specified time and circumstances ([NASA *Quality Measures and Assurance*](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19880020920/downloads/19880020920.pdf)).

**Fixed-configuration accounting transfers well.** Harness rules, routes, and model selections are configuration just as hardware/software/training changes are configuration in reliability-growth programs. Pooling exposures across them answers no clean question. This transfer is **very likely (80-90%)** to be operationally useful because configuration fingerprints are mechanically comparable; uncertainty comes from opaque upstream changes.

**Common-cause skepticism transfers exceptionally well.** Agent “redundancy” often consists of paths sharing infrastructure or epistemic sources. Reliability engineering already distinguishes redundancy from diversity and warns against independence assumptions ([NASA Fault Tree Handbook, p. 118](https://s3vi.ndc.nasa.gov/ssri-kb/static/resources/Fault%20Tree%20Handbook_NASA.pdf)). The transfer is **very likely (80-90%)** to expose brittle fallbacks when dependencies are observable; opaque service internals limit certainty.

**Confidence-bounded acceptance transfers with caveats.** Binary mission outcomes support exact binomial methods if trials are representative and sufficiently independent. This transfer is **likely (55-80%)**, not stronger, because repeated agent missions may share provider state, prompts, evaluator biases, and cached artifacts.

### 4.2 Structural assumptions that do not transfer cleanly

**MTBF is often the wrong primary metric.** Hardware failures are frequently modeled in exposure time; agent-harness failures are commonly deterministic for a particular input/configuration and recur on every identical attempt. “Hours between failures” can rise simply because easy or idle periods dominate. Mission pass probability by operational stratum is usually more interpretable. An exponential/HPP MTBF calculation is valid only when constant-rate and exposure assumptions are defended; NIST expressly frames its MTBF method under a constant repair-rate model ([NIST HPP/exponential model](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/apr/section4/apr451.htm)).

**Independent identical trials are doubtful.** Shared prompts, provider incidents, evaluator versions, caches, and rate limits correlate outcomes. Treating 1,000 near-duplicate replays as 1,000 independent missions produces overconfident bounds. Block by incident/time/task family, report cluster-level sensitivity, and do not use the nominal trial count as effective sample size without evidence.

**Failure semantics are socially and epistemically contested.** A bearing seizure is easier to classify than an agent answer that is partly correct, useful, or unverifiable. The outcome rubric and evaluator are themselves measurement instruments with failure modes. Blinding and adjudication reduce but do not eliminate this.

**Fault trees are not discovery-complete.** A tree enumerates modeled pathways. Novel model behaviors and interactions can sit outside it. NASA advises enough depth to identify dependencies but calls “depth for depth's sake” misguided and notes that overly deep quantification is sparse or bloated with uncertainty ([NASA Fault Tree Handbook, pp. 65-66](https://s3vi.ndc.nasa.gov/ssri-kb/static/resources/Fault%20Tree%20Handbook_NASA.pdf)). Therefore use a shallow tree as a coverage map, preserve an `unmodeled` bucket, and revise it from observed failures.

**Accelerated/stress testing may not extrapolate.** Hardware acceleration models relate high stress to normal stress using physical/statistical models. Prompt adversariality, injected provider errors, or artificially tiny budgets may activate different agent policies rather than accelerate the same failure mechanism. NIST explicitly says high-stress tests require a model that relates high stress back to normal use ([NIST, “Lack of failures”](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/apr/section1/apr132.htm)). Harness stress results must therefore remain an annex unless an empirical bridge to operational incidents is validated.

**Reliability growth is confounded with changing tests and models.** A sequence that improves over calendar time might reflect easier work, evaluator drift, provider recovery, or learning in the test suite rather than corrective action. Smooth Crow/AMSAA-style growth curves are not causal estimators. The proposed ledger preserves the valuable test–analyze–fix discipline without importing an unjustified stochastic growth model.

### 4.3 The hard stop

If the previous configuration cannot be reconstructed and no contemporaneous control, randomized rollout, shadow execution, or equivalent counterfactual exists, **do not claim the change helped**. Report only:

- current absolute demonstration result under the declared profile;
- known targeted recurrence tests;
- historical observations as non-comparable context;
- the missing counterfactual as a gap.

This is not excessive caution; it is the boundary of the evidence. A confident baseline-free improvement score would be a fabricated comparison.

## 5. Rejected borrows and dead ends

### Rejected: FMEA/RPN as the improvement metric

Why rejected: severity, occurrence, and detectability rankings are planning judgments. Changing the rank can reflect assessor opinion or detection changes rather than system reliability. FMEA does not encode all causal combinations, and NASA explicitly warns against patching FMEAs into a fault tree ([NASA Fault Tree Handbook, pp. 66-67](https://s3vi.ndc.nasa.gov/ssri-kb/static/resources/Fault%20Tree%20Handbook_NASA.pdf)). Use FMEA only to seed failure classes and tests.

### Rejected: infer a baseline from fault-tree priors

Why rejected: guessed leaf probabilities create a model-based prior, not observations of the old harness. The output may prioritize experiments, but it cannot establish actual before/after change. This would hide absence of evidence behind quantitative-looking arithmetic.

### Rejected: raw MTBF trend across all sessions

Why rejected: workload and configurations are nonstationary, agent failures are not necessarily exponential, and correlated incidents violate independent-event intuition. The trend can be retained descriptively with configuration/profile boundaries, not called reliability growth.

### Rejected: identical retries as more sample size

Why rejected: repeated deterministic or highly correlated attempts do not supply independent exposure. Retries are recovery behavior and resource cost. Count the mission outcome once, then separately measure attempt-level recovery.

### Rejected: one composite “harness health” score

Why rejected: it allows correctness gains to conceal cost/latency regressions, or abundant low-severity successes to conceal rare catastrophic pathways. Keep correctness, abort/block rate, latency, cost, recovery amplification, and named severe failure modes visible; publish any weighting if a release decision needs a scalar.

### Rejected: an elaborate reliability platform

Why rejected: a new daemon, durable workflow engine, graph database, or observability SaaS is unnecessary for the core mechanism and violates the anti-overfit gate. Versioned JSON/JSONL, existing run hooks, a small calculator, and ordinary reports suffice. The complexity would itself create new configuration and common-cause failure surfaces.

## 6. Minimal release protocol

1. **Preregister.** Freeze incumbent/candidate IDs, mission profile, stress annex, rubric, evaluator, confidence, minimum effect of interest, maximum sample, and stopping rule.
2. **Validate measurement.** Run known-positive detector controls and rubric calibration cases. If controls fail, mark the run invalid; do not report zero events.
3. **Randomize pairs.** Draw a mission once, execute both configurations in randomized order, and record contamination.
4. **Classify whole-mission outcome.** Preserve component failures and retries as causal detail, but the top event is mission success/failure.
5. **Analyze strata and pairs.** Report discordant pairs, exact interval/test appropriate to the paired binary outcome, and profile-weighted absolute bounds. Show raw counts.
6. **Run common-cause drills.** Disable shared dependencies and prove that claimed fallback diversity survives where expected.
7. **Demonstrate the candidate floor.** On a fixed candidate configuration, run the prespecified RDT. A comparative win does not waive the absolute floor.
8. **Decide.** `adopt`, `reject`, or `insufficient evidence`; never silently reinterpret “not significant” as “equivalent.”
9. **Continue surveillance.** Compare post-release profile and failure-mode distributions with the evaluation assumptions. Treat profile drift as invalidating extrapolation, not necessarily the configuration.

The protocol is **likely (55-80%)** to remain under roughly 200 lines of new glue if run envelopes and hooks already exist; this is an architectural estimate, not a local-repository finding. **UNVERIFIED:** the existing bus exposes start/end events and stable configuration inputs sufficient for the design. Settling experiment: implement a throwaway recorder against three representative mission types and count executable non-test lines plus missing event fields before accepting the BUILD-SMALL classification. If it exceeds the threshold, reduce scope rather than introducing a new runtime.

## 7. Decision semantics and honest language

Use these exact distinctions in reports:

- **“Candidate demonstrated ≥99% mission reliability at 95% confidence under profile P.”** Valid only when its fixed-configuration test meets the prespecified bound and assumptions.
- **“Candidate outperformed incumbent on paired profile-P trials.”** Valid only with preserved paired evidence and an effect interval/test.
- **“Targeted recurrence was not observed.”** Valid for the named failure-mode test; not a global reliability claim.
- **“No operational failures were detected; detector control passed.”** Valid absence statement for the detector's scope.
- **“Insufficient evidence.”** Required when intervals include meaningful harm and benefit, when protocol contamination is material, or when evaluator validity fails.
- **“Current configuration meets an absolute floor; improvement is unknown.”** Required when no runnable incumbent/counterfactual exists.

## 9. Anti-overfit gate

All retained proposals ride the existing hook/evaluation/logging/configuration bus. The design intentionally avoids every previously rejected component named in the brief:

- no second orchestration runtime;
- no vector database or knowledge graph memory;
- no durable-execution engine;
- no trained critic;
- no observability SaaS;
- no guardrail framework.

The fault tree is versioned ordinary data, not a graph service. The demonstration calculator is a small report function, not an analytics platform. The paired runner uses the current harness twice, not a new orchestrator. This keeps the mechanism falsifiable and removable as models improve.

## 10. Gaps

1. No local subject tree, corpus, audit finding, or defect file was consulted because this unit was explicitly assigned an ideation lens and told not to ground in the local repository. Consequently, this artifact makes no claim about currently implemented observability, hook availability, schema shape, or exact line count.
2. Public authoritative sources establish RDT, operational-profile, FTA, and common-cause principles. They do not validate the proposed agent-harness translation. Every consequential translation uncertainty is marked inline as **UNVERIFIED** with a settling experiment.
3. Exact paired-test sample size depends on incumbent pass rate, expected discordant-pair rate, minimum effect of interest, desired power, and clustering. Those inputs were not provided; inventing a universal number would be false precision. The release protocol must calculate it after those quantities are declared.
4. Human/evaluator agreement is part of measurement reliability but was not expanded into a separate evaluator-quality subsystem because that would overlap neighboring observability research and risk scope creep. The protocol nevertheless requires blinded, versioned scoring and invalidation when rubric controls fail.

## Bottom line

Reliability engineering has not solved the impossible problem of proving improvement against an unobserved baseline. It has solved the adjacent—and operationally decisive—problems of (a) demonstrating an absolute floor with explicit statistical risk, (b) keeping configuration/exposure boundaries honest during test–analyze–fix cycles, (c) conditioning reliability on an operational profile, and (d) challenging false redundancy through fault trees and common-cause analysis.

The correct harness borrow is therefore not a dashboard or an FMEA score. It is a compact evidence protocol: freeze identities, create a prospective paired baseline, demonstrate an absolute candidate floor, stratify by real use and named stress paths, exercise shared dependencies, and say “improvement unknown” when the counterfactual does not exist.
