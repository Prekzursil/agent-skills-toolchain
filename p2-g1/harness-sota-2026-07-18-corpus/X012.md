# X012 — Cross-domain borrow: clinical medicine → agent trust calibration

**Unit:** X012  
**Lens:** clinical medicine only; external research and design ideation  
**Target:** knowing how much to believe a given agent output, conditional on agent and task type  
**Research date:** 2026-07-18  
**Scope discipline:** Per the unit brief, I did not inspect or characterize the local harness. Statements about “what we currently miss” below therefore mean failure modes missed by an ordinary global-success-rate or self-reported-confidence trust scheme, not verified claims about the subject installation.

## Executive finding

The borrow transfers, but only after a sharp correction to the tempting analogy.

Clinical medicine has **not** solved trust by assigning each physician or test a single reputation number. Its reusable achievement is a layered decision discipline:

1. treat the current case as a prior distribution over possible states, then update it with evidence whose diagnostic value is known in a relevant population;
2. externally validate predicted probabilities in the intended setting and distinguish calibration from discrimination;
3. make action thresholds depend on the relative consequences of false reassurance and needless intervention;
4. encode a small set of context-specific contraindications where a normally useful intervention becomes unacceptable;
5. continuously collect suspected iatrogenic harms after deployment, treating reports as signals to investigate rather than proof of causation; and
6. communicate intervention value as absolute benefit and absolute harm over a stated population and time horizon, rather than as an impressive relative percentage.

The concrete harness translation is a **task-conditioned trust ledger plus a decision policy**, not a new orchestration runtime and not another judging agent. For each `(agent version, task stratum, evidence state)` cell, record externally resolved outcomes and expose: observed correctness/completion, uncertainty interval, calibration curve if the agent emits confidence, harmful-action rate, abstention/verification coverage, and the absolute difference versus the current routing baseline. At dispatch and acceptance time, apply a short contraindication registry and one of three consequence-sensitive dispositions: accept, verify/escalate, or abstain. After execution, log suspected agent-caused harms into a signal queue; only validated outcomes update the trust estimates.

**Overall transfer judgment:** **very likely (80-90%)**, because the target and source share the same decision-theoretic structure—heterogeneous cases, imperfect evidence, asymmetric consequences, and delayed outcome feedback—and because medical prediction-model guidance explicitly requires external validation in the target population, calibration, subgroup assessment, and clinical utility rather than one aggregate accuracy statistic ([BMJ external-validation guidance](https://www.bmj.com/content/384/bmj-2023-074820)). The residual uncertainty is structural: agent tasks mutate faster than most clinical endpoints, outcomes are often subjective or only partially observed, and agent actions alter later evidence. The settling experiment is the prospective shadow-mode trial specified below: compare the proposed policy with current routing on pre-registered task strata and measure severe-error rate, useful-work coverage, cost, and latency.

## 1. The specific clinical mechanisms, in their own field

### 1.1 Differential diagnosis plus Bayesian updating: belief is case-conditioned

In clinical diagnosis, the clinician does not ask whether a laboratory test is “85% trustworthy” in the abstract. The diagnostic process begins with candidate hypotheses and prior probabilities informed by presentation and prevalence. New history, examination, and test results update those probabilities. The US National Academies’ account describes diagnosis as a continuous loop of information gathering, integration, hypothesis generation, and updating; each condition in the differential is checked against expected findings, with more testing or consultation when the evidence does not cohere ([National Academies/NCBI, *The Diagnostic Process*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK338593/)).

A likelihood ratio (LR) makes the update explicit. It is the probability of a test result in someone with the target disorder divided by the probability of the same result in someone without it. In odds form:

`post-test odds = pre-test odds × likelihood ratio`.

Oxford’s Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine explains both the definition and how LR+ and LR− change post-test probability; it also notes that likelihood ratios help select sequences of tests and can be calculated for multiple result levels ([CEBM likelihood-ratio guide](https://www.cebm.ox.ac.uk/resources/ebm-tools/likelihood-ratios)).

Two details matter for the borrow:

- Predictive value depends on the starting prevalence or pre-test probability. A result with the same sensitivity and specificity implies a different posterior in a rare condition than in a common one.
- A differential keeps alternatives alive. A strong signal for one hypothesis is not logically identical to excluding every competing explanation.

Clinical diagnostic-error analysis separately distinguishes hypothesis generation from weighing/prioritizing the differential and adds urgency as another dimension: a less likely diagnosis can still demand attention when delay is dangerous ([AHRQ/NCBI diagnostic-error taxonomy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK20492/)). That is the origin of the useful “must-not-miss” idea: probability and consequence are different axes.

### 1.2 External validation and calibration: a score must agree with outcomes in the target setting

Clinical prediction models distinguish at least three questions:

- **Discrimination:** can the model rank people who experience an outcome above people who do not?
- **Calibration:** when the model predicts a risk of `p`, does the event occur about `p` of the time in comparable cases?
- **Clinical utility:** does acting on the prediction produce more benefit than harm at relevant decision thresholds?

The BMJ’s current external-validation guidance defines external validation as testing predictions on a different but relevant dataset, requires the validation dataset to represent the intended population and setting, and recommends evaluating overall fit, calibration, discrimination, key subgroups, and clinical usefulness ([BMJ, part 2](https://www.bmj.com/content/384/bmj-2023-074820)). A calibration plot compares observed and predicted outcomes across the range of predicted probabilities. Calibration-in-the-large detects systematic under- or overprediction; a calibration slope below one commonly means predictions are too extreme. The companion sample-size paper emphasizes uncertainty intervals because small validation samples can give misleading reliability claims ([BMJ, part 3](https://www.bmj.com/content/384/bmj-2023-074821)).

This is a direct rebuke to two weak trust proxies:

- aggregate accuracy can conceal failure in a particular task population; and
- self-confidence is not trust unless predicted confidence has been compared with externally adjudicated outcomes in a relevant population.

### 1.3 Decision curves: good predictions are not automatically good decisions

Medicine does not stop after calibration. Decision-curve analysis evaluates net benefit across decision thresholds, comparing a model-guided policy with meaningful defaults such as “treat all” and “treat none.” The threshold encodes the tolerated exchange rate between false positives and false negatives. Vickers and colleagues explain that sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve do not by themselves say whether using a test does more good than harm; net benefit places benefits and harms on one scale and should be plotted over a plausible range of exchange rates ([BMJ net-benefit tutorial](https://www.bmj.com/content/352/bmj.i6)). The external-validation guidance likewise says thresholds should be chosen before utility analysis based on the clinical context and preferences, and warns that a well-calibrated model can still have poor utility ([BMJ, part 2](https://www.bmj.com/content/384/bmj-2023-074820)).

Thus “believe this output?” is incomplete. The operational question is “given the posterior reliability and the consequences here, which action—accept, verify, or abstain—has the best expected value?”

### 1.4 Contraindications and warnings: context can dominate average benefit

Prescription labeling deliberately separates indications, contraindications, and warnings. The FDA defines a contraindication as a situation in which the risk clearly outweighs any possible therapeutic benefit and the medicine must not be used; warnings and precautions cover serious risks plus prevention, monitoring, or discontinuation instructions ([FDA prescription-labeling FAQ](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/fdas-labeling-resources-human-prescription-drugs/frequently-asked-questions-about-labeling-prescription-medicines)). FDA’s oncology labeling guide further says contraindications account for the underlying disease’s seriousness and the availability of alternatives ([FDA labeling guide](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/oncology-center-excellence/how-do-i-use-prescription-drug-labeling)).

This mechanism is not a giant denylist. It is a compact registry of **interaction terms**: product X may be broadly beneficial but unacceptable in context Y, while a warning may permit use only with dose adjustment or monitoring.

### 1.5 Iatrogenics and pharmacovigilance: deployment creates new evidence about harm

Pre-deployment trials cannot expose every harm. WHO explains why post-market surveillance is needed: trials are limited by duration, sample size, and exclusion of groups such as pregnant people, infants, older people, and people with other diseases. It defines pharmacovigilance as detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or other medicine-related problems ([WHO pharmacovigilance tools and overview](https://www.who.int/teams/regulation-prequalification/regulation-and-safety/pharmacovigilance/guidance/operations/tools-innovations)).

EMA’s signal-management process uses multiple sources—spontaneous reports, clinical studies, and literature—and explicitly cautions that a safety signal does **not** establish that the medicine caused the event. The underlying illness or another medicine could explain it; assessment determines whether a causal relationship exists ([EMA signal management](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/human-regulatory-overview/post-authorisation/pharmacovigilance-post-authorisation/signal-management)). EMA also maintains a small list of designated medical events that deserve attention regardless of statistical prioritization, while warning that this list is not a comprehensive detector. Its training material names five stages: detection, prioritization, validation, confirmation, and assessment ([EMA pharmacovigilance training](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/pharmacovigilance-overview/pharmacovigilance-training-materials)).

The conceptual win is disciplined asymmetry: collect low-threshold **suspicions**, but do not convert them directly into causal truth or permanent prohibitions.

### 1.6 NNT/NNH: report absolute marginal value, with population, comparator, outcome, time, and uncertainty

For a binary outcome, number needed to treat (NNT) is the reciprocal of the absolute risk difference: the average number treated with one intervention rather than its comparator for one additional beneficial outcome. Its harm counterpart is NNH. Altman emphasizes that NNT requires a confidence interval and becomes infinite at zero effect ([BMJ on NNT confidence intervals](https://www.bmj.com/content/317/7168/1309)). CONSORT guidance recommends reporting absolute as well as relative effects because relative-only presentation can cause effect overestimation, and notes that absolute risk difference is less transportable because baseline risk varies across populations ([CONSORT 2025 explanation](https://www.bmj.com/content/389/bmj-2024-081124)). Cochrane similarly warns that risk differences are often inconsistent across baseline event rates and therefore are often unsuitable for a single pooled NNT ([Cochrane Handbook, chapter 15](https://training.cochrane.org/handbook/current/chapter-15)).

NNT is therefore not a universal rank for a drug. It is shorthand for an absolute effect under a specific comparator, outcome definition, population, and time horizon, with sampling uncertainty.

## 2. Concrete translation: the Task-Conditioned Trust Card

### Proposal A — Outcome-conditioned trust ledger

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).** A post-task hook emits compact JSONL records; an existing scheduled job or skill aggregates them into trust cards. No daemon, database, vector store, or second orchestration layer is required.

One card is keyed by:

`agent identity/version × task family × risk tier × environment/evidence state × evaluation horizon`.

Minimum input record:

```json
{
  "agent_version": "...",
  "task_family": "code-edit|research|ops|planning|...",
  "risk_tier": "reversible|material|high-stakes",
  "context_flags": ["network_required", "dirty_worktree", "ground_truth_delayed"],
  "self_confidence": 0.73,
  "disposition": "accepted|verified|abstained",
  "verifier": "tests|human|external-source|none",
  "outcome": "correct|incorrect|partial|unknown",
  "severity": "none|minor|major|critical",
  "cost": {"tokens": 0, "latency_ms": 0, "tool_calls": 0},
  "outcome_timestamp": "..."
}
```

The card displays, with denominators and intervals rather than naked percentages:

- resolved-task success and major/critical failure rate;
- unresolved-outcome fraction (never silently counted as success);
- calibration curve and Brier score when a confidence signal exists;
- discrimination separately from calibration;
- accept/verify/abstain coverage;
- absolute benefit and harm versus the current routing baseline;
- time window and version boundary; and
- an explicit “insufficient evidence” state below the pre-registered sample threshold.

This catches failures a global reputation misses:

- an agent that is excellent at repository navigation but poor at shell quoting;
- an agent that ranks easy versus hard tasks correctly yet says 95% when it is right only 70%;
- a seemingly reliable agent whose evaluated cases are mostly easy, while difficult outcomes remain unresolved;
- regression after a model, prompt, tool, or environment version change;
- a low-frequency severe failure hidden by high ordinary-task accuracy; and
- verification policies that improve correctness but cost more than their marginal benefit.

**Expected result:** **likely (55-80%)** that task-conditioned cards produce materially better routing than one per-agent success rate, because target-population and subgroup calibration are necessary in medical external validation and the proposed strata expose analogous case-mix variation ([BMJ external validation](https://www.bmj.com/content/384/bmj-2023-074820)). This is **UNVERIFIED** for this harness; settle it with Experiment 1 below, comparing held-out log loss/Brier score and severe-error routing under global versus hierarchical task-conditioned estimates.

#### Important statistical restraint

Do not create thousands of brittle cells. Start with a small predeclared taxonomy and use partial pooling: a sparse cell shrinks toward its parent task family and ultimately the agent-wide base rate. Always show effective sample size and interval width. Version changes start a new card but may borrow the older card only as an explicit prior, never by silently merging outcomes.

**Expected result:** **likely (55-80%)** that partial pooling avoids both the global-average failure and noisy tiny-cell rankings; the evidence is the medical insistence on representative external-validation samples and precise uncertainty estimates rather than subgroup point estimates ([BMJ sample-size guidance](https://www.bmj.com/content/384/bmj-2023-074821)). This is **UNVERIFIED** for the available task volume; settle it by time-split simulation over historical outcomes, scoring hierarchical, unpooled, and global estimators on future cases.

### Proposal B — Evidence updates, not self-confidence laundering

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only).** Extend the task result schema with an evidence vector and have the existing acceptance hook apply a declarative likelihood/weight table learned from adjudicated history.

For an output, begin with the relevant card’s base rate, then update using evidence that has measured diagnostic value in that task stratum:

- deterministic test passed or failed;
- cited source resolves and directly entails the claim;
- expected artifact exists and passes a structural validator;
- independent verifier agrees or disagrees;
- tool execution produced an error or partial result;
- agent abstained or marked a premise unverified.

The analogy says **not** to treat all signals as independent. Two agents using the same model, prompt, retrieved page, or broken tool can make correlated errors; multiplying their apparent likelihood ratios would double-count evidence. The first implementation should therefore use a conservative rule: only update strongly from mechanically independent evidence channels, and cap corroboration from correlated model judgments.

**Expected result:** **likely (55-80%)** that deterministic and provenance-grounded evidence will improve acceptance decisions over verbal confidence alone, because medicine updates priors with test characteristics and validation requires predicted risk to match observed outcomes ([CEBM likelihood ratios](https://www.cebm.ox.ac.uk/resources/ebm-tools/likelihood-ratios); [BMJ calibration guidance](https://www.bmj.com/content/384/bmj-2023-074820)). This is **UNVERIFIED** because no evidence-channel likelihood ratios have been estimated here; settle it by labeling a stratified sample of outputs, fitting channel effects on an earlier window, and measuring calibration on a later untouched window.

Do **not** expose fake precision. Until enough outcomes exist, emit qualitative bands such as “insufficient evidence,” “verify,” and “eligible for auto-accept,” accompanied by denominators. A numerical posterior is allowed only after prospective calibration.

### Proposal C — Consequence-sensitive decision policy and contraindication registry

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only).** Put a short ordered rule set on the existing pre-action/post-output hook bus.

Each rule has:

```yaml
- id: destructive-with-unresolved-target
  applies_when:
    action_class: destructive
    target_resolution: unresolved
  disposition: hard_stop
  rationale: "possible loss dominates expected convenience"
  evidence_required_to_clear:
    - explicit_target_listing
    - independent_scope_check
  owner: harness-safety
  expires_or_review_by: 2026-10-18
```

Use three levels modeled on labeling:

- **Contraindication / hard stop:** the interaction between agent/action/context is unacceptable until a named clearing condition is met.
- **Warning / verify:** allow only with a named independent check, narrower scope, approval, or rollback path.
- **Ordinary use:** route according to the task-conditioned card.

The registry is about context, not stigmatizing an agent. Examples of candidate predicates include irreversible action plus unresolved target, legal/medical/financial claim plus no authoritative source, mutation plus unknown repository state, or a tool/agent combination with a validated current severe-harm signal. These are candidate examples, not findings about the harness.

This catches a high-performing agent whose rare failure is intolerable in one context, and prevents average success from overriding a known dangerous interaction. It also catches “correct answer, wrong action”: a prediction can be epistemically plausible but still not justify an irreversible step.

**Expected result:** **very likely (80-90%)** that explicit consequence tiers prevent at least some high-severity errors that aggregate accuracy cannot, because FDA labeling treats context-specific contraindication as categorically distinct from average indication and warnings ([FDA labeling FAQ](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/fdas-labeling-resources-human-prescription-drugs/frequently-asked-questions-about-labeling-prescription-medicines)). The claim that the net effect is positive in this harness is **UNVERIFIED**; settle it with shadow evaluation of every triggered rule, recording counterfactual prevented harm, false blocks, delay, and workarounds before any hard enforcement.

The registry must remain small and expiring. Every entry needs an owner, causal rationale, clearing condition, review date, and trigger count. A rule with no triggers over its review interval is not automatically useless—the event may be rare—but it must be re-justified from evidence. A warning that operators routinely bypass is an iatrogenic failure of the control itself.

### Proposal D — Agent pharmacovigilance queue

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).** Add an “adverse output/action” event type to the same append-only outcome stream and a periodic review skill. Do not create an observability service.

Lifecycle:

1. **Detect:** accept owner corrections, failed independent checks, rollbacks, severe tool errors, and downstream incidents as suspected signals.
2. **Prioritize:** severity, recurrence, reversibility, exposure count, and whether the event is a designated “never event” determine review urgency.
3. **Validate:** confirm that the detector worked, reconstruct the sequence, and seek at least two mechanically independent signals before asserting absence/breakage/causality.
4. **Assess:** compare alternative causes—agent reasoning, prompt, stale context, tool defect, environment, evaluator mistake, or interaction.
5. **Act:** no action, gather more data, add a temporary warning, change routing, add a contraindication, or retire a version.
6. **Reassess:** measure whether the mitigation reduces the signal without creating another harm.

The event record must keep `suspected`, `validated association`, and `causal finding` as distinct statuses. This directly borrows EMA’s warning that a safety signal is not proof of causality ([EMA signal management](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/human-regulatory-overview/post-authorisation/pharmacovigilance-post-authorisation/signal-management)).

This catches:

- rare but severe failures that do not change average accuracy much;
- delayed harm discovered after an output was initially marked complete;
- clusters that arise only for one tool × task × agent interaction;
- safety controls that themselves cause work loss, delay, or risky bypasses; and
- spurious blame of the agent when the tool, prompt, evaluator, or environment caused the outcome.

**Expected result:** **likely (55-80%)** that a structured signal lifecycle surfaces rare severe interactions earlier than aggregate dashboards, because pharmacovigilance exists precisely to detect harms missed by bounded pre-deployment studies and aggregates heterogeneous post-market evidence ([WHO pharmacovigilance overview](https://www.who.int/teams/regulation-prequalification/regulation-and-safety/pharmacovigilance/guidance/operations/tools-innovations)). This is **UNVERIFIED** for agent incidents; settle it through a six-week retrospective/prospective pilot measuring time-to-detection for known seeded incident patterns, reviewer false-positive rate, and whether validated signals predict recurrence in the following period.

### Proposal E — “Number needed to verify” and “number needed to harm,” always conditional

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).** Add absolute-effect calculations to the trust-card report; no runtime component.

For a task stratum and fixed horizon, compare policy A (for example, automatic acceptance) with policy B (independent verification):

- `absolute error reduction = error_rate(A) − error_rate(B)`
- `NNV = 1 / absolute error reduction`: verifications required to prevent one additional erroneous acceptance
- `NNH_delay = 1 / absolute increase in deadline misses` when verification adds delay
- `NNH_block = 1 / absolute increase in useful outputs wrongly blocked`

Always print the comparator, task stratum, outcome, evaluation window, cost, and confidence interval. If the interval crosses no effect, present the benefit-to-harm interval through infinity rather than a single promotional number. Also report natural frequencies: “in 1,000 comparable tasks, verification prevented an estimated X erroneous acceptances and caused Y additional misses.”

This catches relative-risk theater. “Verification reduced errors by 50%” might mean a drop from 2 in 1,000 to 1 in 1,000, which is a very different operational proposition from 200 to 100. It also forces the harness to count iatrogenic costs: extra latency, unnecessary escalation, blocked correct work, and evaluator-induced error.

**Expected result:** **very likely (80-90%)** that absolute natural-frequency reporting improves the owner’s ability to compare routing interventions, because NNT was created to express marginal absolute benefit and CONSORT warns that relative-only presentation leads to overestimation ([BMJ NNT](https://www.bmj.com/content/310/6977/452); [CONSORT 2025](https://www.bmj.com/content/389/bmj-2024-081124)). Whether an NNV metric changes actual harness choices is **UNVERIFIED**; settle it with a blinded decision study in which maintainers select policies from relative-only reports versus full absolute benefit/harm cards, then score choices against predeclared utility.

## 3. Worked example (illustrative, not measured)

Suppose Agent A has 92% resolved-task success overall. A global scheme auto-accepts its outputs.

The trust ledger reveals the following **invented example only**:

| Task stratum | Resolved N | Observed success | Major harm | Self-confidence calibration |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Read-only repository explanation | 800 | 96% | 0.1% | close to observed |
| Shell mutation with complex quoting | 70 | 69% | 7% | predicts 91% |
| Source-backed research | 240 | 90% | 1% | predicts 88% |

The medical translation produces different actions:

- For read-only explanation, a low-consequence threshold makes auto-accept reasonable.
- For shell mutation with complex quoting, the relevant prior is 69%, not 92%; overconfidence further weakens self-report as evidence.
- If the proposed command is destructive and its target is unresolved, a contraindication hard-stops execution regardless of average success.
- A deterministic dry-run plus independent target listing can provide new evidence and clear the warning.
- If verification reduces error from 31% to 8%, the illustrative NNV is about `1 / 0.23 = 4.35`: roughly five verifications prevent one erroneous acceptance in this stratum. This number cannot be exported to other tasks.
- A later rollback becomes a suspected adverse event, not immediate proof that Agent A caused it; validation tests alternative causes.

This is the full point of the borrow: trust attaches to a **decision under conditions**, not to a persona.

## 4. Adversarial transfer analysis

### What genuinely transfers

1. **Heterogeneous base rates.** Disease prevalence changes predictive value; task difficulty and environment plausibly change agent reliability. The shared mathematics is conditional probability, not a metaphor.
2. **Imperfect tests/evidence.** A lab result is not the disease; a test pass, citation, or second-agent agreement is not correctness. Each evidence channel has false-positive and false-negative behavior.
3. **Asymmetric loss.** Missing a dangerous disease can cost more than an unnecessary test; approving an irreversible bad action can cost more than verifying a harmless explanation.
4. **Distribution shift.** Clinical models require external validation in the target population; agent versions, tools, repositories, and prompts similarly define a target environment.
5. **Post-deployment harm discovery.** Pre-market studies miss rare/contextual harms; offline agent benchmarks miss tool interactions, delayed consequences, and operational adaptations.
6. **Intervention side effects.** Tests and treatments can cause harm; verifiers, blockers, retries, and escalations consume time and can introduce errors.

These commonalities make the bounded transfer **very likely (80-90%)**, supported by the source mechanisms above. The remaining uncertainty is whether the available agent outcome labels are adequate; Experiment 0 below directly tests labelability before implementation.

### Where the analogy breaks

1. **There may be no stable latent “disease.”** Many agent outputs are creative, negotiated, or multi-objective. “Correct” can be observer-dependent. Clinical calibration assumes a sufficiently defined outcome; without one, a trust probability is meaningless.
2. **Agent identity is unusually mutable.** Model snapshots, prompts, tools, permissions, retrieved context, and task decomposition can change between runs. Pooling under a stable agent name can create a false longitudinal record.
3. **The agent changes the case.** A diagnostic test often observes a patient state; an agent acts on the environment, and early actions alter later evidence and outcome opportunities. Simple independent Bayesian updates can fail under this endogeneity.
4. **Evidence channels are correlated.** Multiple agents may share a base model, retrieval source, or prompt. “Second opinion” is not independent merely because it has a second process ID.
5. **Outcome ascertainment is selective.** Easy tasks are checked quickly; subtle failures may remain unknown; owner corrections preferentially reveal salient errors. Naively treating unlabeled outputs as successes creates verification bias.
6. **Feedback can be adversarial.** Once a metric controls routing, agents or workflows may optimize its proxy. Clinical prediction also faces behavior changes, but agent systems can adapt far faster.
7. **NNT assumes a defined intervention and comparator.** “Use Agent A” is not sufficiently specified if routing changes prompts, tools, review, or case mix simultaneously. NNV must be tied to one policy contrast and time window.
8. **Contraindication registries ossify.** A safety rule can survive after the model or tool changes, block beneficial work, or drive unsafe workarounds. Expiry and counterfactual review are mandatory.
9. **Differential diagnosis can become expensive theater.** Generating multiple agent hypotheses does not create information when all agents share blind spots. The transferable part is explicit alternatives and discriminating evidence, not more prose or more agents.

### Rejected borrows

- **Rejected: one “trust score” per agent.** This is the opposite of prevalence-conditioned predictive value and external validation.
- **Rejected: literal diagnostic differential for every task.** Routine low-risk work does not justify multi-hypothesis deliberation; it would add latency and correlated text without necessarily adding evidence.
- **Rejected: multiplying confidence from several agents.** Independence is unproven and often implausible.
- **Rejected: permanent blacklists from single incidents.** Pharmacovigilance separates suspicion, validation, assessment, and action.
- **Rejected: context-free NNV leaderboard.** Absolute effects depend on baseline risk, comparator, and horizon.
- **Rejected: a new trust daemon or knowledge graph.** It is unnecessary for the first test and violates the anti-overfit gate.
- **Rejected: “clinically validated” language for the harness.** The design borrows mechanisms; it has not undergone clinical validation, and agent outcomes are not patient outcomes.

## 6. Experiments and falsification criteria

### Experiment 0 — Can outcomes be labeled well enough?

Before building trust estimates, sample at least 200 completed tasks across candidate strata. Two evaluators independently assign outcome, severity, and evidence quality from the artifact and downstream state; disagreements are adjudicated. Report agreement, unresolved fraction, time-to-resolution, and whether evaluators were blinded to agent identity.

**Go condition:** task strata with operationally defined outcomes and acceptable agreement can enter the ledger.  
**Falsifier:** if most high-value tasks remain subjective/unresolved or agreement is poor after rubric refinement, calibrated “trust” does not transfer for those tasks; retain only provenance and consequence controls.  
**Prediction:** **roughly even (45-55%)** that all major task families will support reliable binary outcomes, because code/tests may be objective while research quality and planning often are not. This is **UNVERIFIED**; this labeling study is the settling experiment.

### Experiment 1 — Global reputation versus task-conditioned calibration

Use time-ordered, version-aware data. Train/estimate on an earlier window and test on a later untouched window. Compare:

1. global per-agent base rate;
2. unpooled per-task rates;
3. hierarchical/partially pooled per-agent × task estimates;
4. the same models with evidence-channel features.

Primary metrics: Brier score or log loss, calibration intercept/slope, expected calibration error with a reliability plot, and severe-error recall at fixed review coverage. Secondary metrics: cost and latency.

**Go condition:** hierarchical conditioning improves held-out calibration and/or severe-error routing with intervals excluding a trivial effect.  
**Falsifier:** no held-out benefit, unstable estimates across time, or benefits vanish after controlling for outcome-missingness. In that case, keep consequence tiers and provenance, but do not route on task cards.

### Experiment 2 — Shadow decision curve

Run accept/verify/abstain policy in shadow mode. Predeclare several exchange rates representing low-, medium-, and high-consequence tasks. Compare with accept-all, verify-all, and current policy. Report natural frequencies per 1,000 tasks and absolute benefit/harm, not only AUC.

**Go condition:** positive net utility over a non-trivial range of predeclared thresholds, without unacceptable coverage loss.  
**Falsifier:** the policy is dominated by a simpler default, or utility exists only at a threshold selected after seeing results.

**Prediction:** **likely (55-80%)** that different risk tiers require different thresholds, because clinical decision curves explicitly vary the acceptable false-positive/false-negative exchange rate by context ([BMJ net benefit](https://www.bmj.com/content/352/bmj.i6)). This harness result is **UNVERIFIED**; the shadow curve is the settling experiment.

### Experiment 3 — Contraindication registry safety and iatrogenics

Seed or replay known examples for every rule to prove the detector finds a known-present condition. Independently validate triggers using both the rule-engine trace and the underlying action/context fields. For four weeks of shadow mode, review every trigger and a random sample of non-triggers.

Measure: true dangerous interactions caught, false blocks, missed cases, added latency, bypass attempts, and downstream harm. Promote a hard stop only with two mechanically independent signals for the interaction and an explicit clearing path.

**Go condition:** serious-risk recall improves with tolerable false blocks and no worse workaround behavior.  
**Falsifier:** the rules are mostly redundant, produce alert fatigue, or displace work into less observable channels.

### Experiment 4 — Pharmacovigilance signal validity

Retrospectively seed known incident patterns and prospectively collect suspected events. Ensure the detector finds seeded known-present items before making absence claims. Have assessment blinded, where feasible, to agent identity. Track how often suspected signals become validated associations or causal findings and whether they predict recurrence.

**Go condition:** faster detection of recurrent/severe patterns than ordinary aggregate reporting at an acceptable review burden.  
**Falsifier:** reports mostly duplicate known failures, causal status collapses into opinion, or review costs exceed prevented harm.

### Experiment 5 — NNV/NNH communication

For each candidate verification policy, report absolute effects, natural frequencies, intervals, cost, and horizon alongside relative effects. Ask maintainers to choose a policy before and after seeing the full card; compare choices to predeclared utility.

**Go condition:** full cards reduce selection of dominated policies and reveal baseline-risk reversals.  
**Falsifier:** decisions do not improve, or the outcome utilities cannot be specified consistently enough for NNV to guide action.

## 7. Rollout and stop conditions

1. Start with labeling feasibility, not automation.
2. Add outcome emission in observation-only mode.
3. Publish trust cards with denominators, unresolved fractions, version boundaries, and uncertainty.
4. Run routing and contraindications in shadow mode.
5. Enable only rules whose prospective decision curves show net benefit.
6. Revalidate after agent, prompt, tool, or environment changes; do not inherit “approval” by name.
7. Audit iatrogenic effects of the harness controls themselves.

Stop or narrow the project if any of the following occurs:

- outcomes cannot be defined or independently adjudicated for a task family;
- unresolved outcomes are too selectively missing to estimate reliability;
- conditioned estimates do not outperform global estimates out of time;
- a simple accept-all, verify-all, or existing policy dominates the trust policy;
- registry false blocks or bypass behavior cause more harm than they prevent;
- version churn invalidates estimates before they accumulate useful evidence; or
- review overhead grows into a de facto second orchestration system.

## 8. What this would catch that simple trust calibration misses

The package specifically exposes:

- **case-mix masking:** high global performance supported by easy tasks;
- **calibration failure:** confident outputs whose stated probabilities exceed observed correctness;
- **good ranking but bad probabilities:** discrimination without calibration;
- **good prediction but bad decision:** accuracy that does not justify action under asymmetric harm;
- **must-not-miss rare events:** severe failures invisible in averages;
- **contraindicated interactions:** reliable agent, wrong context/tool/action;
- **verification iatrogenics:** blockers and critics that delay or damage correct work;
- **delayed adverse effects:** failures discovered after initial completion;
- **causal overclaim:** incident correlation incorrectly blamed on the agent;
- **correlated corroboration:** multiple nominally separate agents sharing one blind spot;
- **version drift:** historical reliability attached to a changed system;
- **outcome verification bias:** unknown cases silently treated as wins;
- **relative-effect theater:** large percentage improvement with tiny absolute value; and
- **false precision:** sparse task cells rendered as confident rankings.

## 9. Final recommendation

### Adopt a task-conditioned trust card and shadow decision policy

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).** Build the outcome schema and read-only aggregation first; pair it with **CONFIG (settings/JSON only)** shadow contraindications. Do not initially auto-route from self-reported confidence.

This recommendation is **UNVERIFIED** until Experiments 0–3 establish labelability, out-of-time calibration gain, and positive decision utility. Conditional on those gates passing, it is **very likely (80-90%)** to be a better trust mechanism than a global per-agent score, because it preserves the clinical mechanisms that actually transfer: population-conditioned priors, external outcome calibration, consequence-dependent action thresholds, explicit high-risk interactions, and post-deployment harm surveillance. If the labelability gate fails, the honest result is narrower: keep the contraindication/provenance controls and adverse-event review, and do not manufacture a probability of correctness.

The deepest lesson from medicine is not “be cautious.” It is: **separate belief, evidence, action, and harm; condition all four on the case; and make uncertainty visible.**
