**Target:** verify an agent's claims without trusting the agent and without requiring a human to read everything

## Executive conclusion

The useful borrow is **committed reporting followed by an unpredictable verification lottery, with a strictly proper score for the reporter's pre-audit confidence**. Mechanism design calls the core problem *costly verification*: the principal cannot cheaply observe the agent's private state, so it commits to an audit rule and consequences that make false reporting unattractive. Proper scoring rules separately make honest probability reports optimal in expectation when outcomes are eventually observed.

The transfer is **partial, not literal**. An audit can independently establish an audited, operationally decidable claim; random auditing can estimate and deter error over a population; neither one proves every unaudited claim. A language model is also not safely assumed to be a stable expected-utility maximizer. Therefore the harness must obtain its guarantee from an **output gate and independent verifier receipts**, not from asking the model to care about points.

My central assessment is **very likely (80-90%)**, because the economic structure matches on four observable dimensions—private report, costly checking, ability to commit to a randomized check, and an enforceable accept/reject consequence—and because the proposed gate does not depend on the model experiencing incentives. The remaining uncertainty is whether claim extraction and cheap independent resolution cover enough real claims to justify their friction; that premise is **UNVERIFIED pending the “shadow claim-ledger trial” specified below**.

Peer prediction—paying one reporter based on agreement with another—is a tempting but bad default here. Research already identifies uninformative equilibria and shows that, when limited ground truth is available, simple spot checking can require less ground truth and provide stronger incentive guarantees than a broad class of spot-checking peer-prediction mechanisms ([Gao, Wright & Leyton-Brown, 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07042)). Multiple agents sampled from the same model family can share training priors, retrieval failures, prompts, and hallucinations. Agreement is then not mechanically independent evidence.

## 1. What mechanism design solved in its own field

### 1.1 Costly state verification and stochastic auditing

In the costly-state-verification model, an informed agent observes a state that the principal cannot see unless the principal pays a verification cost. A contract specifies reports, when verification occurs, and transfers or penalties conditional on the report and verified state. Townsend's foundational treatment explicitly models endowments known only to their owner unless a verification cost is paid, then characterizes consistent Pareto-optimal contracts ([Townsend, 1979, Journal of Economic Theory](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-0531(79)90031-0); accessible abstract at [Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/fip/fedmsr/45.html)).

Border and Sobel's “Samurai Accountant” makes the audit instrument especially concrete: a risk-neutral principal chooses pre-audit payments, post-audit payments, and report-dependent audit probabilities for an agent whose wealth is private. Their efficient schemes generally use **stochastic auditing** and rebates after audit; audit probability is part of the mechanism rather than an ad hoc afterthought ([Border & Sobel, 1987](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-pdf/54/4/525/4506308/54-4-525.pdf), DOI [10.2307/2297481](https://doi.org/10.2307/2297481)).

A more directly allocation-oriented result has the same shape. Ben-Porath, Dekel, and Lipman study a principal who can check an agent's private information at a cost but cannot make monetary transfers. Their favored-agent mechanism avoids checks in one report region and checks the highest reporter outside it, awarding the object only if the report is confirmed ([Ben-Porath, Dekel & Lipman, 2014](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.104.12.3779)). Li's extension with limited punishments obtains report regions with no inspection, probabilistic inspection, and certain inspection, demonstrating that optimal audit intensity can depend on the report and the available consequence ([Li, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jet.2020.105000)).

The solved pattern is not “audit everything.” It is:

1. make the report before the audit decision;
2. commit to an audit policy the reporter cannot retroactively influence;
3. spend verification where its marginal incentive/information value is high; and
4. attach a consequence to detected falsehood.

This is an incentive-compatibility result only under the paper's assumptions about preferences, information, commitment, verification quality, and enforceable consequences. It is not a universal theorem that random checks make arbitrary systems truthful.

### 1.2 Strictly proper scoring rules

A scoring rule maps a probabilistic forecast and a realized outcome to a score. It is **proper** when a forecaster maximizes expected score by reporting the forecaster's actual distribution, and **strictly proper** when that optimum is unique. Gneiting and Raftery give this definition and a general treatment of strictly proper scoring rules ([Gneiting & Raftery, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437); [author-hosted PDF](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/people/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf)). Savage's earlier treatment connects proper scores to eliciting personal probabilities ([Savage, 1971](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1971.10482346)).

For a binary claim with stated probability `p` and verified outcome `y ∈ {0,1}`, a positively oriented quadratic score can be written:

`S(p,y) = 1 - (p-y)^2`.

Expected score is uniquely maximized by reporting the reporter's true probability. The important feature is not the particular scale; it is that an unsupported `p=0.99` is punished much more when false than a cautious `p=0.55`, while systematically underconfident true reports also leave score on the table.

Proper scoring solves **probability elicitation when an outcome is observed and the reporter responds to expected score**. It does not establish the outcome, make an ambiguous statement binary, or cause an inference-time model to value the score.

### 1.3 Goodhart's law is the design warning, not the mechanism

The familiar formulation is that a measure ceases to be a good measure when it becomes a target. Recent formal work shows that the effect depends on the distribution of discrepancy between proxy and true objective; heavy-tailed discrepancies can make optimizing the proxy counterproductive ([El-Mhamdi & Hoang, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09638)). This matters because “citation count,” “test pass count,” “verifier agreement,” and even “Brier score on resolved claims” are proxies for the owner's real objective: correct, useful work.

The mechanism-design response is not to find one perfect metric. It is to keep the audit draw unpredictable, retain direct ground-truth checks, use several outcome types, log selection propensities, and keep consequential gates tied to independently resolved facts rather than raw proxy totals.

## 2. Exact structural mapping

| Mechanism-design object | Agent-harness object | Transfer quality |
|---|---|---|
| Agent privately observes wealth, cost, value, or outcome | Producing agent has its chain of tool observations and drafts a factual claim | Strong for claims about files, commands, APIs, and tests; weak for taste and synthesis |
| Report/message | Atomic claim plus probability, evidence pointers, and claim type | Strong if committed before audit |
| Principal | Harness output gate acting for the owner | Strong if policy is outside the model's control |
| Costly audit | Fresh file read, independent command, schema validator, API query, test, or source retrieval | Strong only where the verifier is genuinely independent and authoritative |
| Transfer/penalty | Withhold the claim, require revision, expand audit, or deny “verified/done” status | Partial: it controls output, but is not a utility transfer to the model |
| Allocation decision | Permit claim into final answer; permit completion status; allocate more verification budget | Strong as an enforceable system action |
| Prior/type distribution | Historical base rate by claim type, tool, domain, and verifier | Partial; distributions drift as models and tasks change |
| Incentive compatibility | Truthful probability/report yields best expected acceptance or downstream selection | Weak as a behavioral model of one LLM call; stronger as a pipeline-selection property |

The decisive disanalogy is agency. Economic mechanisms assume an agent with coherent preferences who anticipates the mechanism. An LLM generates tokens from conditioning; it may simulate compliance without optimizing a durable payoff. Therefore, **“score the agent” alone does not transfer**. The valuable transfer is the principal-side commitment device: claim commitment, randomized checking, and an accept/reject consequence enforced by code.

## 3. Proposal — UNVERIFIED: claim escrow plus verification lottery

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)**

The recommendation rests on an **UNVERIFIED premise: that at least 60% of consequential factual claims in representative harness runs can be atomized and resolved by cheap independent checks; the “shadow claim-ledger trial” below settles it**. The proposal inherits the tag in this heading accordingly.

Build one small hook-bus component, not a second orchestrator. It consumes a compact claim ledger before completion and emits verifier receipts plus a gate decision. It should have no vector database, knowledge graph, durable-execution engine, trained critic, observability SaaS, or guardrail framework.

### 3.1 Claim escrow record

Before the audit seed is revealed, require each consequential factual claim to be registered as:

```json
{
  "claim_id": "c17",
  "statement": "The targeted test exits 0 on the current tree",
  "claim_type": "command_outcome",
  "probability": 0.94,
  "confidence_band": "almost certain (90-99%)",
  "impact": "completion-critical",
  "evidence_refs": ["tool-call-81"],
  "subject": {"cwd": "...", "revision": "..."},
  "proposed_verifier": {"adapter": "fresh-command", "args_hash": "..."}
}
```

The mandatory verbal confidence band remains visible to the owner; numeric `probability` exists solely because proper scoring needs a number. The harness, not the reporting agent, resolves the actual adapter and arguments from an allowlisted claim type and immutable subject identity. An agent-supplied verifier recipe is a hint, never evidence.

Claims that are subjective, normative, future-dependent, or not operationally decidable must be typed `non_resolvable`; they cannot receive a “verified” badge. This avoids laundering judgment into a binary test.

### 3.2 Audit selection

After commitment, derive a sample from a seed unavailable at drafting time. For claim `i`, start with an explicit inclusion probability:

`q_i = min(1, q_floor + impact_i + novelty_i + evidence_weakness_i + calibration_debt_i)`.

These are policy terms, not a claim that this additive formula is optimal. Always set `q_i=1` for completion-critical assertions such as “done,” “tests pass,” “absence,” “deleted,” “deployed,” or “no remaining errors.” For lower-impact claims, preserve a nonzero random floor so reporters cannot identify a safe-to-fabricate class. Record `q_i` and the seed commitment so population error can later be estimated without pretending the sample was uniform.

The value of a secret post-commitment draw is **very likely (80-90%)**, supported by the costly-verification literature's use of committed stochastic auditing and by the direct inability of the reporter to condition a particular lie on a known audit result. Whether secrecy materially changes this harness's observed claim accuracy is **UNVERIFIED pending the randomized A/B arm in the shadow trial**.

### 3.3 Mechanically independent verification

For an audited claim, the gate executes a verifier that does not reuse the producing agent's conclusion:

- file-content claim → fresh bounded read plus parser/hash check;
- command-outcome claim → fresh execution from recorded working directory and revision;
- external fact → fresh retrieval from an authoritative source, with response identity and timestamp;
- schema claim → machine parser/validator;
- UI claim → fresh rendered artifact or screenshot comparison;
- absence/broken/done claim → a known-present positive control **and two mechanically independent signals**, matching the program's honesty requirement.

The audit receipt contains subject identity, verifier identity/version, inputs hash, raw result reference, resolution (`true`, `false`, `inconclusive`), and timestamp. `Inconclusive` is not silently scored as false and does not authorize “verified.” The agent cannot self-issue a receipt.

This would be **very likely (80-90%)** to catch some currently missed cases where a final assertion contradicts observable tool state, because the gate re-observes that state rather than trusting prose. The magnitude is **UNVERIFIED; replay the shadow trial against human-adjudicated claims to measure incremental precision and recall**.

### 3.4 Scoring and consequences

For every conclusively resolved audited binary claim, compute the quadratic score and retain calibration statistics by claim type. Do not optimize a single global score. Use scores for three bounded purposes:

1. increase future audit probability for claim types with calibration debt;
2. compare confidence buckets with empirical truth frequencies;
3. decide whether a model/prompt may use strong completion language without full checking.

The immediate enforceable consequence is not a fictional payment. A false completion-critical claim is removed, completion is denied, and the relevant claim family is fully audited for that response. An inconclusive critical claim is rewritten as uncertain or blocked. A verified true claim receives a receipt-backed status.

This gate is **likely (55-80%)** to reduce confidently false completion language, with evidence from the direct mechanical consequence and strict-propriety result; the wide band reflects the disanalogy that the LLM may not behaviorally respond to longitudinal scoring. This effect is **UNVERIFIED until the preregistered paired-task experiment below compares false-completion rates**.

### 3.5 Population estimates without reading everything

Because audited claims have known inclusion probabilities, estimate total false claims with a Horvitz–Thompson-style inverse-probability estimator rather than treating the selected set as representative:

`estimated false total = Σ(audited i) 1[false_i] / q_i`.

Report uncertainty intervals and strata; do not rank models on tiny samples. The estimator's classical unbiasedness requires positive known inclusion probabilities and correct resolutions. Those assumptions fail when verification is systematically inconclusive, so inconclusive rate must be a first-class metric rather than discarded.

The estimator can **likely (55-80%)** provide a more honest fleet-level error estimate than raw audited error rate when sampling is impact-weighted, because it corrects known unequal inclusion probabilities. Its usefulness under sparse and clustered harness data is **UNVERIFIED; settle it with simulation using trial-observed strata and resolution rates**.

## 4. What it catches that ordinary “show evidence” misses

The mechanism targets failures that evidence formatting alone does not:

- **Cherry-picked evidence:** a claim cites one favorable observation while the independent adapter rechecks the actual subject.
- **Stale-state claims:** the receipt binds working directory, revision, timestamp, and input hash.
- **Confident fabrication:** quadratic loss distinguishes a false 0.99 claim from an honestly uncertain 0.55 claim.
- **Completion laundering:** “done” cannot pass on prose or a self-issued test summary.
- **Safe-class gaming:** a nonzero audit floor means no ordinary factual class is known to be exempt.
- **Detector failure mistaken for absence:** required positive controls and two independent signals prevent a single broken detector from certifying absence.
- **Metric gaming:** independent random audits remain connected to ground truth while calibration debt changes sampling pressure.

Coverage of the first six categories is **likely (55-80%)**, based on the direct mapping between each failure and a gate invariant. End-to-end coverage is **UNVERIFIED; the shadow trial must label which historical false claims each invariant actually detects**.

It will not catch:

- false premises shared by the reporter and supposedly independent verifier;
- semantic claims whose truth is not reduced correctly to the adapter;
- normative bad judgment that is factually consistent;
- malicious changes that also corrupt the verifier or receipt store;
- errors in unaudited individual claims.

It is **almost certain (90-99%)** that stochastic auditing alone cannot prove each unaudited claim, because no observation of one sampled item logically entails the truth of a different unsampled item. The mechanism offers selective proof plus statistical assurance and deterrence, not universal proof.

## 5. Adversarial transfer audit

### Assumption A: the verifier reveals truth

Economic models often give verification a well-defined result. Real verifiers can be flaky, aliased to the same data source, scoped incorrectly, or wrong. This is the largest transfer risk. A “second agent agrees” check is not independent. The gate should classify verifier independence (same transcript, same source, fresh source, executable oracle) and refuse strong status where independence is weak.

Residual risk remains **likely (55-80%)**, because software and web evidence frequently share dependencies; evidence is the structural disanalogy, and the settling experiment is fault injection that corrupts one source and measures whether each adapter falsely confirms it.

### Assumption B: the reporter responds to incentives

This does not reliably transfer. No claim is made that a stateless model maximizes future Brier score. The mechanism remains useful only because output admission and completion state are controlled externally. Longitudinal scores may help choose prompts/models, but are not the safety boundary.

It is **very unlikely (5-20%)** that points alone make a one-shot LLM incentive compatible, because the model has no demonstrated persistent utility over those points; the settling experiment is an ablation comparing score-only prompting with the same prompt plus a mechanical output gate.

### Assumption C: reports are atomizable and outcomes resolve

Many valuable conclusions combine facts, causality, and judgment. Forcing all prose into binary claims can reward trivialities and omit hard-to-check assertions—a Goodhart failure. Claim ledgers must cover only consequential checkable facts and preserve `non_resolvable` as a legitimate type.

It is **roughly even (45-55%)** that the proposed extraction threshold (60% of consequential factual claims cheaply resolvable) is met across heterogeneous tasks; there is no local measurement in this ideation unit. This is **UNVERIFIED pending manual labeling in the shadow claim-ledger trial**.

### Assumption D: the principal can commit and enforce consequences

If the agent can see the random draw before committing, rewrite the ledger afterward, choose arbitrary verifier code, or declare completion despite gate failure, the mechanism collapses. Append-only commitment and an outside-the-model gate are necessary conditions, not optional hardening.

It is **very likely (80-90%)** that post-commitment sampling plus immutable receipts is technically feasible on an existing hook bus, based on the small data flow described here; actual integration is **UNVERIFIED pending a <200-line spike that demonstrates commit → sample → verify → gate on three adapter types**.

### Assumption E: correlated agents provide independent truth

This assumption does not transfer, so peer prediction is rejected as the verification oracle. Miller, Resnick, and Zeckhauser show truthful reporting is a Nash equilibrium under their peer-prediction construction ([Miller, Resnick & Zeckhauser, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1050.0379)), but later work notes common-prior requirements and undesirable non-truthful equilibria ([Kong & Schoenebeck, 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.07751)). Gao et al. further show low-cost coordination signals create unavoidable low-effort equilibria for broad peer-prediction approaches and find simple ground-truth spot checking stronger under their assumptions ([Gao et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07042)).

Thus “ask three agents and take majority” is **unlikely (20-45%)** to be a reliable truth mechanism for shared-model agents, based on the literature's equilibrium problems and the plausible shared-error channel. The exact harness rate is **UNVERIFIED; settle it by comparing majority agreement against independent executable ground truth, stratified by model family and shared context**.

## 6. Anti-overfit gate

This proposal passes the required anti-overfit gate:

- It rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus as one bounded ledger/audit/gate component.
- It does not introduce a second orchestration runtime.
- It does not introduce vector/KG memory, a durable-execution engine, a trained critic, observability SaaS, or a guardrail framework.
- The intelligence remains in ordinary task execution and concrete verifier adapters; the mechanism adds commitment and selection, not a competing agent architecture.
- The first version is deliberately limited to three high-yield machine-decidable adapter types: fresh command, fresh file/parser, and authoritative HTTP/API retrieval.

Keeping the core below roughly 200 lines is **likely (55-80%)**, because ledger validation, seeded sampling, quadratic scoring, and gating are individually small, but platform-specific receipt plumbing may exceed the bound. This is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is the three-adapter implementation spike, counting non-generated source lines separately for core and adapters**.

## 7. Required settling experiment: shadow claim-ledger trial

This is the minimum experiment before enforcement.

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)**

1. Sample at least 100 completed tasks across at least five task classes; keep the sampling frame fixed before labeling.
2. Have a human adjudicator mark consequential factual claims, whether each is atomic, whether it is machine-resolvable, and its true state. This is research labeling, not a permanent human review requirement.
3. Run the ledger in shadow mode. Commit claims before sampling; audit with `q=1` for completion-critical claims and randomized positive `q` for others.
4. Compare four preregistered arms on matched tasks: no ledger; visible claim ledger only; score-only feedback; mechanical gate plus hidden post-commitment draw.
5. Inject faults: stale revision, wrong working directory, fabricated passing test, missing resource, broken absence detector, correlated second-agent answer, and verifier timeout.
6. Measure: false completion rate; verifier precision/recall against adjudication; resolvable fraction; inconclusive fraction; added latency/tool calls; calibration by claim type; and weighted population-error interval coverage.
7. Ship enforcement only if the gate reduces false completion by a preregistered practically meaningful margin, verifier false-confirmation stays below a preregistered ceiling, and operational overhead fits an explicit budget. Do not invent thresholds after seeing results.

The trial is **very likely (80-90%)** to distinguish whether the value comes from mere structured prompting, scoring, or the mechanical gate, because the arms isolate those components and fault injection supplies known ground truth. The remaining uncertainty comes from task heterogeneity and sample size.

## 8. Rejected alternatives and dead ends

### Peer majority / peer prediction as truth

Rejected. It can be useful for generating challenges or candidate checks, but agreement is not proof and correlated models violate the independence intuition. The cited peer-prediction literature supplies explicit undesirable equilibria and a result favoring direct spot checks when limited ground truth exists.

### Score every sentence

Rejected. Resolution cost would dominate, prose atomization would distort useful synthesis, and Goodhart pressure would encourage safe trivial claims. Only consequential factual assertions enter escrow.

### Audit only suspicious-looking claims

Rejected. A deterministic suspicion classifier creates an unaudited region that becomes the target. Risk-sensitive sampling is useful only with a positive random floor and logged propensities.

### Treat citations or tests as self-authenticating

Rejected. A citation can fail to entail the claim, and a test can run against the wrong tree or omit the relevant behavior. Subject-bound independent receipts are the actual evidence object.

### Use confidence bands without outcomes

Rejected. Labels alone do not produce calibration. Proper scores require independently resolved outcomes over enough claims, and selection into resolution must be accounted for.

### Claim universal proof from random audits

Rejected. Audits establish sampled facts and support population estimates. They cannot logically certify every unsampled statement.

## 9. Decision

Proceed only to the shadow trial and small spike, not immediate enforcement. The cross-domain mechanism **does transfer as a principal-side verification architecture**: commitment, unpredictable selective audit, independent outcome, and enforceable gate. It **does not transfer as a claim that an LLM becomes an economically rational truthful agent**, and peer agreement does not become ground truth merely because it is scored.

The final recommendation is **likely (55-80%)** to be net beneficial if the shadow trial clears its preregistered gates. Evidence: costly-verification mechanisms directly address expensive checking; strictly proper scoring addresses confidence elicitation conditional on resolved outcomes; and limited-ground-truth research warns against peer prediction while supporting simple spot checking. The recommendation remains **UNVERIFIED until the shadow claim-ledger trial measures resolvability, false-confirmation, false-completion reduction, and overhead on representative tasks**.
