# X094 — Mechanism design → trust calibration

**Unit:** X094  
**Lens:** incentive compatibility, proper scoring rules, peer prediction, and Goodhart effects  
**Target:** deciding how much to believe a particular agent on a particular kind of task  
**Scope discipline:** This is an ideation/research unit. Per the task, it does not inspect or make claims about the local harness. The only write is this artifact.

## Executive conclusion

The useful cross-domain borrow is **a precommitted strictly proper scoring rule applied to ex-ante probability reports, with independently resolved outcomes and randomized audits**. The transfer is **partial, not literal**.

In mechanism design, a strictly proper scoring rule makes a risk-neutral forecaster maximize expected score by reporting its true probability. Gneiting and Raftery define propriety exactly this way and characterize proper scores through convex expected-score functions ([JASA paper, DOI landing page](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437); [author-hosted full text](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/people/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf)). For a binary outcome, the Brier loss gives a particularly transparent result: if the forecaster's belief is `q`, its expected loss after reporting `p` is

`q(1-p)^2 + (1-q)p^2 = (p-q)^2 + q(1-q)`.

The unique minimum is therefore `p=q`. This is the incentive-compatible part of the mechanism.

The concrete harness translation is not “reward an LLM with points and assume it has preferences.” It is: require auditable claims to carry probabilities before their outcomes are known; resolve a random, representative subset using evidence independent of the producing agent; compute bounded proper scores and calibration by **agent × task type**; show coverage and sample uncertainty beside every score; and use the result to decide when to trust, verify, abstain, or route elsewhere.

That design is **very likely (80-90%)** to reveal task-conditional overconfidence that raw success rates, self-reported confidence, and undifferentiated agent leaderboards conceal. Evidence: strictly proper scores jointly evaluate probability and outcome ([Gneiting and Raftery](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437)), while the Brier decomposition explicitly separates reliability/calibration from resolution and outcome uncertainty ([Murphy 1973](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0450(1973)012%3C0595:ANVPOT%3E2.0.CO;2)). The band is below “almost certain” because usefulness depends on representative claim capture, enough resolved outcomes per task stratum, and genuinely independent verification.

The design is **likely (55-80%)** to improve routing and verification decisions after a shadow deployment. Evidence: calibration is a joint property of forecasts and realized events, and proper scores plus calibration plots are established diagnostics for forecast deficiencies ([Gneiting, Balabdaoui, and Raftery 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9868.2007.00587.x)). This forward-looking harness effect is **UNVERIFIED; settle it with a preregistered shadow A/B experiment comparing fixed routing with score-informed routing on held-out tasks, measuring owner-accepted outcomes, verification cost, and severe-error rate**.

It would be a category error to claim more. A proper score elicits a probability only under its incentive assumptions; it does not establish that an LLM has a coherent private belief, does not make a false proposition true, does not score prose quality, and cannot evaluate an outcome that never becomes observable. Therefore the harness should treat this as an empirical calibration instrument and routing signal—not as proof of model honesty.

Peer-prediction and “truth serum” mechanisms are valuable negative controls, but they should **not** be used as the production trust grade for agents that can share models, prompts, tools, sources, or training priors. Agreement can then be common-mode error. Miller, Resnick, and Zeckhauser prove honest reporting as a Nash equilibrium by scoring one rater's report against implications for another rater's report ([Management Science paper](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1050.0379); [author-hosted copy](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/rzeckhauser/files/feedback.pdf)). Prelec's Bayesian Truth Serum likewise addresses subjective data without objective truth ([Science paper](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1102081); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15486294/)). Those are impressive solutions in their own fields, but their equilibrium and signal-structure assumptions are not a safe substitute for independent ground truth in this target domain.

## 1. The source-field problem and the specific mechanism

### 1.1 What mechanism design is solving

The principal wants information held by an agent, but the agent's report can differ from that information. A mechanism defines the reports, allocation or decision rule, and transfers so that a desired reporting strategy is optimal. In probabilistic elicitation, the report is a distribution and the eventual outcome supplies the verification signal.

A scoring rule `S(P, y)` is proper when a forecaster whose belief distribution is `Q` maximizes expected score by reporting `P=Q`; it is strictly proper when that report is uniquely optimal. Gneiting and Raftery provide the standard formal definition, characterize regular proper rules via convexity, and connect the associated score divergence to the loss from reporting the wrong distribution ([full text, Sections 2.1–2.2](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/people/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf)). This is not merely an accuracy metric added after the fact. The payment/evaluation rule is designed so that the desired information revelation is incentive compatible before the event resolves.

For trust calibration, the quadratic/Brier rule is the most suitable first mechanism:

- Binary claim outcome `y ∈ {0,1}`.
- Agent report `p ∈ [0,1]`, interpreted as probability that the precisely stated claim will resolve true under a precommitted resolver.
- Loss `L(p,y)=(p-y)^2`; lower is better.
- Expected loss under true belief `q` differs from the optimum by exactly `(p-q)^2`.

The bounded loss is operationally useful: one mistaken near-certainty cannot numerically dominate an entire small stratum in the way unbounded logarithmic loss can. This is a design preference, not a theorem that Brier is universally superior. Gneiting and Raftery catalogue the Brier and logarithmic scores and show both are proper, with different entropies and divergences ([full text](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/people/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf)).

Murphy's decomposition is the second part of the borrow. The mean Brier score can be decomposed into outcome uncertainty, reliability (calibration), and resolution; the source paper explicitly introduces and interprets those three terms ([Murphy 1973](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0450(1973)012%3C0595:ANVPOT%3E2.0.CO;2)). This prevents a key trust mistake: a forecaster can be calibrated but uninformative by always reporting the base rate, or sharp/discriminating but badly calibrated. Gneiting, Balabdaoui, and Raftery state the forecasting objective as maximizing sharpness subject to calibration and recommend calibration plots and proper scores together ([JRSS B 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9868.2007.00587.x)).

### 1.2 Why ordinary success rate is not the same mechanism

A binary “right/wrong” tally discards whether the agent said 51% or 99%. Those outputs demand different levels of trust even when both cross the same decision threshold. Conversely, calibration alone can reward timid base-rate predictions, so the harness must retain resolution, coverage, and task mix. The decomposition source is direct evidence for this distinction ([Murphy 1973](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0450(1973)012%3C0595:ANVPOT%3E2.0.CO;2)).

The mechanism therefore targets a precise object: **probabilistic reliability conditional on the declared task class and verification contract**. It does not target eloquence, usefulness, harmlessness, or total task utility. Those remain separately measured outcomes.

### 1.3 Why precommitment matters

The probability, task label, claim wording, and resolution rule must be fixed before the verifier result is visible. Otherwise the agent or harness can retrospectively reword claims, omit failures, or select favorable resolvers. Properness is a statement about a forecast made before an outcome sampled from the believed distribution; changing the forecast or event after observation breaks that setup ([Gneiting and Raftery](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437)).

## 2. Concrete harness translation

The smallest viable architecture is a ledger on the existing event/hook bus plus an offline scorer. It is deliberately not another orchestration runtime.

### Proposal A — auditable claim envelope — UNVERIFIED

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)**

For claims eligible to affect a decision, require or derive an immutable envelope before verification:

```json
{
  "claim_id": "uuid",
  "run_id": "...",
  "agent_id": "agent/version/prompt-policy",
  "task_type": "code_patch|diagnosis|web_fact|plan|artifact_review|...",
  "claim": "The patch passes test suite T at revision R",
  "p_true": 0.82,
  "resolution": {
    "predicate": "exit_code(T,R)==0",
    "source_class": "test_runner",
    "deadline": "..."
  },
  "evidence_refs": ["..."],
  "decision_impact": "low|medium|high",
  "created_before_resolution": true
}
```

The controlled `task_type` is essential because “agent A is 82% trustworthy” averages across incomparable mixes. The useful report is closer to “agent A, on dependency diagnosis with an executable reproducer, has this calibration curve and this amount of evidence.”

Capturing every sentence would create claim spam and ambiguous resolution. The envelope should apply to (a) completion claims, (b) externally checkable factual claims that support a decision, (c) diagnosis/root-cause claims, and (d) predicted consequences of a proposed change. A small fixed taxonomy plus an escape value is preferable to free-form labels because an agent could otherwise relabel hard tasks into favorable strata.

This proposal is **likely (55-80%)** to obtain sufficiently atomic, resolvable claims from current agents when the schema is concise and examples are supplied. The evidence is indirect: proper scoring rules require a well-defined outcome, but the cited theory does not establish that an LLM will reliably produce good claim envelopes. This is **UNVERIFIED; settle it by double-annotating 300 claims from held-out sessions for atomicity, task label, resolver clarity, and whether independent annotators obtain the same binary outcome**.

If envelope validity is poor, stop. Do not score ambiguous claims; forcing a number onto a vague proposition creates false precision.

### Proposal B — delayed, independent resolution and randomized audit

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)**

Resolution sources should be ordered by mechanical independence from the producing agent:

1. Existing executable outcomes: test runner, compiler/type checker, schema validator, checksum, API response, or owner acceptance captured later.
2. Independent primary evidence: a primary-source record or externally observed state not generated by the agent.
3. Human adjudication using the precommitted predicate, blinded where practical to agent identity and probability.
4. Unresolved. A peer agent's agreement is not silently promoted to truth.

“Independent” here means that the result channel is not merely another sample from the same agent or a judge consuming only its prose. A test can still be incomplete, and a human can still err; the ledger must record resolver class so trust can be stratified by it.

Where automatic resolution is cheap, resolve all eligible claims. Where it is costly, select claims for audit using a seed committed after claim creation, stratified by task type and decision impact. Keep the sampling probability and report it. The selection must not be controlled by the producing agent. This makes selective success reporting harder and permits inverse-probability weighting if audit rates differ across strata.

The randomized-audit addition is **very likely (80-90%)** to catch cherry-picked verification and missing-failure bias better than voluntary agent self-checks, provided the audit frame contains all eligible claims. Evidence: random selection removes the reporter's control over which committed claims enter evaluation; proper scoring then operates on the selected resolved outcomes ([Gneiting and Raftery](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437)). The remaining uncertainty is coverage: omitted claims cannot be sampled.

The stronger claim—random audit yields an unbiased production-wide trust estimate—is **UNVERIFIED; settle it by preregistering the eligible-claim detector, proving inclusion probabilities are nonzero, and comparing weighted audit estimates against a one-week census of all resolvable claims**. It is only **likely (55-80%)** until that experiment passes, because detection omissions and administrative censoring can violate the sampling frame.

### Proposal C — bounded scoring, task strata, and uncertainty-aware display

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)**

For each resolved binary claim, store:

- `brier_loss = (p_true - y)^2`;
- signed calibration residual `p_true - y`;
- confidence bucket and outcome;
- resolver class, task type, impact, horizon, and audit probability;
- whether resolution arrived before a fixed reporting cutoff.

Aggregate only after stratifying at least by `agent_id × task_type`; additionally facet by resolver class and decision impact when sample sizes permit. Display:

- `n_total_eligible`, `n_audited`, `n_resolved`, and resolution rate;
- mean Brier loss with a bootstrap or other interval;
- calibration table/plot (`mean p` versus empirical frequency) with bin counts;
- a reliability/resolution decomposition when the sample is large enough;
- high-confidence error count, especially `p ≥ 0.9` failures;
- task-mix and outcome base rate;
- an explicit `INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE` state instead of a numerical rank for small strata.

No single aggregate “trust score” should be the primary interface. If a summary is unavoidable, it must link to its task mix, coverage, uncertainty, and calibration curve. Murphy's decomposition supplies the technical reason that a scalar score conflates components ([Murphy 1973](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0450(1973)012%3C0595:ANVPOT%3E2.0.CO;2)); Gneiting et al. supply the reason to inspect calibration and sharpness jointly ([JRSS B 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9868.2007.00587.x)).

This display is **very likely (80-90%)** to distinguish an overconfident specialist, a calibrated-but-uninformative agent, and a strong agent evaluated mostly on easy tasks when each stratum has enough resolved claims. The evidence is the Brier reliability/resolution/uncertainty decomposition ([Murphy 1973](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0450(1973)012%3C0595:ANVPOT%3E2.0.CO;2)). “Enough” is deliberately not assigned a universal number; dependence among claims and base rates vary.

Confidence intervals must cluster by run or task when multiple claims share a session, because treating correlated claims as independent would understate uncertainty. This is a statistical safeguard, not a claim derived from the mechanism-design citations.

### Proposal D — trust-aware action policy, initially in shadow mode — UNVERIFIED

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only)**

Map calibrated risk to actions, not to prestige:

- high-impact claim + weak/unknown stratum evidence → require independent verification;
- high-impact claim + demonstrated overconfidence → require verification and cap displayed confidence;
- low-impact claim + well-calibrated stratum → permit cheaper spot checks;
- unresolved claim → display `UNRESOLVED`, never infer truth from score or peer agreement;
- distribution shift (new model, prompt policy, toolchain, or task subtype) → decay or suspend inherited trust until fresh evidence accumulates.

The policy should start in shadow mode: record the action it would have taken without changing execution. Only promote thresholds after a held-out evaluation against owner-relevant utility and severe errors.

This recommendation inherits **UNVERIFIED** because the premise that score-conditioned routing improves this harness has not been tested; **settle it with a preregistered shadow A/B evaluation on held-out tasks, comparing severe-error recall, false verification escalations, owner acceptance, latency, and tool cost**. Improvement is **likely (55-80%)**, based on the established diagnostic value of calibration and proper scores ([Gneiting et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9868.2007.00587.x)), but the cited literature does not establish the operational cost-benefit in an agent harness.

## 3. What this catches that a typical trust view misses

These are mechanism-level detection targets, not claims that the present harness lacks them.

### 3.1 Confidence inflation

An agent that says 95% on claims true only 70% of the time can have a respectable binary accuracy while being dangerously overconfident. Calibration buckets and signed residuals expose the mismatch; high-confidence Brier penalties make it visible. This detection is **almost certain (90-99%)** given correctly resolved, representative observations, because the empirical 95% bucket directly estimates its realized frequency. The residual uncertainty is finite-sample variation and dependence.

### 3.2 Task-conditional reversals

An agent can be reliable for code execution claims and poor for causal diagnosis, or vice versa. A global average can reverse preferences when task mixes differ. Per-task strata expose that aggregation problem. Detection is **very likely (80-90%)** when task labels are stable and each stratum has adequate resolved observations; it falls when agents can relabel tasks or task types are too coarse. The experiment that settles taxonomy adequacy is the 300-claim double-annotation study specified under Proposal A.

### 3.3 Calibrated ignorance versus useful discrimination

Always reporting the base rate may be calibrated yet provide little resolution. Reporting Murphy's reliability and resolution terms separately catches this. Detection is **almost certain (90-99%)** in a sufficiently large stationary sample because those are the decomposition's defined targets ([Murphy 1973](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0450(1973)012%3C0595:ANVPOT%3E2.0.CO;2)). The residual uncertainty concerns whether the operational sample is sufficiently large and stationary.

### 3.4 Selective verification and “only count the wins”

Commitment before outcome plus randomized audit makes it harder to submit checks only for claims expected to pass. Coverage denominators reveal how much remains unaudited or unresolved. Catching deliberate or accidental selection is **very likely (80-90%)** if the eligible-claim detector has high recall and audit selection occurs after commitment; it is **unlikely (20-45%)** if the agent can omit eligible claims before the ledger. That is why detector validation is a gate, not an implementation detail.

### 3.5 Stale reputation after a model or policy change

Binding `agent_id` to model/version/prompt-policy and showing time windows prevents old performance from silently certifying a changed system. It is **very likely (80-90%)** to expose obvious version shifts when identity metadata are reliable. It will not detect silent upstream model changes unless the provider exposes a stable version or canaries reveal a shift; that stronger ability is **UNVERIFIED; settle it with version-pinned replay canaries before and after known model changes**.

### 3.6 Common-mode agreement masquerading as truth

Keeping peer agreement out of the truth label catches a conceptual failure: multiple agents repeating the same source or model prior do not constitute independent evidence. The policy does not identify which shared answer is false; it prevents agreement alone from raising its trust grade. That prevention is **almost certain (90-99%)** if the scorer accepts only the enumerated resolver classes, because peer votes are excluded by construction.

## 4. Adversarial transfer audit: where the analogy holds and breaks

### 4.1 Structural mapping

| Mechanism-design object | Harness object | Transfer quality |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic forecaster | Agent output policy plus the harness policy that selects/prompts/routes it | Partial |
| Private belief `q` | Latent uncertainty represented by an emitted `p_true` | Weak-to-partial |
| Report `p` | Claim-level confidence emitted before verification | Strong |
| Realized state `y` | Independently resolved claim outcome | Strong only for resolvable claims |
| Payment/score | Logged Brier loss and downstream routing/verification consequence | Partial |
| Repeated market | Repeated tasks within a stable agent/task stratum | Partial |

The report/outcome/scoring mathematics transfers cleanly. The preference and private-belief assumptions do not.

### 4.2 Disanalogy 1: an LLM need not be a utility maximizer

Proper scoring rules are incentive compatible for an agent that chooses reports to maximize expected score, with relevant assumptions such as risk neutrality or appropriate utility treatment. A prompted model may instead imitate probability language, follow local instructions, or emit an unstable token-level heuristic. Therefore it is not justified to say the score “makes the LLM honest.”

What survives is empirical: if the emitted probabilities are evaluated on held-out resolved outcomes, their calibration can be measured regardless of why the model emitted them. If the harness later selects models/prompts/routes based on held-out score, the optimization pressure acts on the surrounding system even if the sampled model has no persistent utility.

It is **likely (55-80%)** that explicit probabilities can support useful empirical calibration for at least some stable task strata, because proper scores are established forecast-evaluation tools ([Gneiting and Raftery](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437)). Whether a particular agent's verbal probabilities correspond to stable latent beliefs is **UNVERIFIED; settle it with repeated paraphrase/replay tests plus held-out calibration, measuring probability variance and score stability**.

### 4.3 Disanalogy 2: truth often does not resolve

Many agent outputs are plans, interpretations, aesthetic judgments, open-ended designs, or counterfactual diagnoses. There may be no binary event and no independent resolver. Proper scoring rules cannot create the missing outcome. Converting owner preference into a pseudo-objective “truth” would conflate utility with factual correctness.

The correct result is to mark those claims outside the mechanism's scope. Use separate acceptance, pairwise preference, or decision-utility evaluation, clearly named as such. Do not blend those signals into factual calibration.

### 4.4 Disanalogy 3: the outcome definition can be gamed

“Tests pass” is only as meaningful as the tests; an agent can optimize the resolver while violating the owner's real goal. This is a direct Goodhart channel. Manheim and Garrabrant distinguish regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial forms of Goodhart effects and emphasize that optimization of a proxy can become ineffective or harmful ([arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.04585)).

Mitigations are limited but concrete: precommit resolver predicates, keep owner utility separate, randomly audit against broader acceptance criteria, rotate hidden holdouts, report resolver class, and do not optimize a single public scalar. These controls are **likely (55-80%)** to reduce simple proxy gaming. They are **very unlikely (5-20%)** to eliminate Goodhart effects entirely, because every finite measurement system leaves unmeasured dimensions; the evidence is the taxonomy of distinct failure modes rather than a universal prevention theorem ([Manheim and Garrabrant](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.04585)).

### 4.5 Disanalogy 4: claims are selected, dependent, and nonstationary

Classical exposition often evaluates a defined set of forecast events. Agents choose what to say, claims within a run share evidence, and models/tools/prompts change. A mean score over self-selected claims can reward easy-claim production. Treating many correlated claims as independent exaggerates effective sample size. Historical calibration may not survive a policy change.

Hence the requirements for a fixed eligibility detector, randomized audit, clustered uncertainty, explicit coverage, versioned identity, and time decay. Even with these, external validity to a new task type remains **unlikely (20-45%)** without new observations; this band follows from the structural distribution shift, not from a cited universal numerical estimate.

### 4.6 Disanalogy 5: confidence itself may change behavior

Requiring claim probabilities can induce hedging, verbosity, claim splitting, or systematic omission. A proper rule governs the best report conditional on participating in a fixed event; it does not by itself make event selection truthful. The mechanism must score coverage and audit the eligibility frame, not merely score submitted forecasts.

The claim-envelope intervention avoiding harmful output distortion is **roughly even (45-55%)** before testing. This is **UNVERIFIED; settle it with a randomized shadow trial comparing matched tasks with and without visible confidence elicitation, blind-rating task quality, claim count, omissions, latency, and calibration**.

## 5. Why peer prediction does not safely transfer

### 5.1 What peer prediction solved in its own field

When objective outcomes are unavailable, Miller, Resnick, and Zeckhauser derive a peer-prediction mechanism. A rater's signal implies a posterior distribution over another rater's signal; a proper scoring rule rewards the first report based on the peer report. Under their model, honest reporting is a Nash equilibrium, and payments can be scaled to induce effort ([paper](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1050.0379); [full-text copy](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/rzeckhauser/files/feedback.pdf)).

Prelec's Bayesian Truth Serum asks respondents both for an answer and for a prediction of answer frequencies, rewarding answers that are more common than collectively predicted. It is designed for subjective multiple-choice data without an objective truth criterion ([Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1102081); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15486294/)).

Later surrogate scoring rules attempt to recover strictly proper scores without direct truth by using a noisy reference derived from peer reports, estimating its error rates across multiple tasks, and debiasing the score. Liu, Wang, and Chen state the required multi-task setup and the claim that the expected surrogate score recovers the ground-truth proper score under their assumptions ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.09158); [ACM-author copy](https://econcs.seas.harvard.edu/files/econcs/files/liu_ec20.pdf)).

These are real mechanism-design achievements. They answer “how can reports be elicited when the principal lacks direct verification?” under explicit models.

### 5.2 The target-domain assumptions that fail

Agent replicas may share training data, system prompts, retrieval results, tool failures, and even deterministic code paths. Their errors can therefore remain correlated conditional on the actual truth. A consensus score may reward a shared hallucination or a copied primary error.

The peer mechanisms also reason about strategic equilibria, not just accuracy. Truthful reporting being an equilibrium does not mean it is the unique equilibrium reached by arbitrary language-model policies. Collusion, coordinated conventions, or low-information agreement are especially concerning when agents are spawned from the same base system.

Surrogate scoring rules require enough agents/tasks and assumptions enabling estimation of reference-answer error rates across tasks; the paper's own abstract describes a multi-task setting and reliance on prior-distribution information ([Liu, Wang, and Chen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.09158)). A heterogeneous stream of coding, research, planning, and review tasks does not automatically satisfy stable error-rate assumptions.

Therefore using peer prediction as a production factual-trust label is **unlikely (20-45%)** to be reliably truth-tracking in a same-family agent harness. Evidence: the mechanisms' guarantees depend on peer signal/report structure ([Miller et al.](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1050.0379)) or multi-task proxy-error estimation ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.09158)), while shared model/tool provenance supplies a concrete dependence channel. The exact failure magnitude is **UNVERIFIED; settle it by constructing a benchmark with planted common-mode false premises, then compare peer-consensus scores against independent executable/primary-source labels across same-model and diverse-model panels**.

### 5.3 Permitted role for peers — UNVERIFIED

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only)**

Peer disagreement can be a **triage signal**: it can trigger an independent check. Peer agreement can help cluster duplicate claims or find candidate evidence. Neither becomes the truth label. This limited use is **likely (55-80%)** to reduce verification waste without corrupting calibration if peer signals affect only audit priority and never outcome assignment. This is **UNVERIFIED; settle it with a shadow comparison of random-only audits versus peer-disagreement-prioritized audits, retaining a random audit slice to estimate missed errors**.

## 6. Goodhart-resistant operating rules

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only)**

These are constraints on the proposals above, not a new framework.

1. **Never optimize one public leaderboard number.** Show Brier loss, calibration, resolution, coverage, task mix, and sample uncertainty. Manheim and Garrabrant's taxonomy explains why stronger optimization against a proxy increases the risk of regressional, extremal, causal, or adversarial failures ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.04585)).
2. **Keep the holdout path partially hidden.** Publish the semantics of resolution, not every future audit case. Randomize after claim commitment.
3. **Keep scoring separate from owner utility.** A claim can be true but useless; a patch can pass a narrow test and still be harmful. Owner acceptance and severe-error outcomes remain first-class, separate signals.
4. **Report coverage beside performance.** `10/10 resolved easy claims` must not dominate `80/100 mixed claims` without exposing the selection difference.
5. **Version identity.** Do not transfer trust automatically across model, system-prompt, toolchain, or policy changes.
6. **Use an abstention state.** “Insufficient evidence” and “unresolved” are legitimate outputs; forcing a score rewards fabricated certainty.
7. **Retain a random audit slice.** Even if risk-based auditing is later added, a random slice is needed to observe what the risk model misses.
8. **Audit the detector itself.** Seed known-present eligible claims and prove they enter the ledger before making absence or coverage claims.
9. **Do not let the producing agent adjudicate its own claim.** It may propose a resolver, but resolution comes through an independent recorded channel.
10. **Freeze the scoring rule for an evaluation window.** Retrofitting the rule after seeing outcomes destroys comparability and invites metric shopping.

These controls are **very likely (80-90%)** to make score gaming more visible than an unversioned global success rate, because they expose selection, task mix, and resolution channels. They are **very unlikely (5-20%)** to make the metric strategy-proof against an adaptive optimizer; no cited result guarantees that for this open-ended environment.

## 7. Anti-overfit gate and architecture decision

The proposal passes the stated anti-overfit gate:

- It rides the existing hook/event and skill/tool verification buses.
- It needs an append-only JSONL-style ledger, a small audit sampler, and an offline scorer/display.
- It does not require a second orchestration runtime.
- It does not require a vector database or knowledge graph.
- It does not require a durable-execution engine.
- It does not train a critic.
- It does not require observability SaaS.
- It does not introduce a guardrail framework.

The radical option considered was a prediction-market service in which agents trade claims and prices become trust estimates. That is rejected. It would be **unlikely (20-45%)** to outperform the small ledger for this owner because model replicas do not have naturally distinct endowments or utility, correlated agents undermine the information aggregation story, market liquidity would be artificial, and it creates a second runtime. No evidence found in this unit justifies crossing the BUILD-LARGE threshold.

A second rejected option was a peer-reputation graph. It would reproduce the unverifiable-consensus problem, invite correlated-agent amplification, and violate the no-vector-DB/KG-memory direction. It offers no independent truth channel.

A third rejected option was training a confidence critic. It adds a learned proxy and a new Goodhart surface before the simpler proper-score ledger establishes whether claim-level calibration is useful. That would fail the anti-overfit gate.

## 8. Minimal experiment sequence and stop/go criteria

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only)**

### Experiment 1 — envelope feasibility

Run the 300-claim, held-out, double-annotation study described in Proposal A. Preregister eligibility and atomicity criteria. Include code, factual research, diagnosis, planning, and review.

**Go** only if independent annotators can agree on the claim boundary, task type, and resolver for a large majority. A numeric threshold must be chosen before annotation; this unit does not invent one without owner cost tolerances. **Stop or redesign** if vague/unresolvable envelopes dominate.

### Experiment 2 — retrospective scorer validation

Use only claims whose probabilities were recorded before outcomes; do not backfill confidence from prose after resolution. Validate the scorer on synthetic controls: all-correct 0.9 forecasts, all-wrong 0.9 forecasts, base-rate forecasts, known overconfidence, duplicated correlated claims, and missing outcomes. Confirm formulas against hand calculations.
