# X048 — Cross-domain borrow: nuclear operations → trust calibration

## Scope and method

This unit asks whether mechanisms from nuclear operations can help an agent harness decide how much to believe a particular agent output for a particular kind of task. It is deliberately an ideation lens. I did not inspect, characterize, or modify the local harness, repository, settings, skills, hooks, or session corpus. I used public primary sources from the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC), and International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), plus one peer-reviewed machine-learning study where the transfer hinges on correlated model errors.

The short answer is **yes, but only partially**. Nuclear operations has not solved epistemic truth for open-ended language outputs. It has solved a narrower and highly relevant control problem: how to perform and release consequential work when performers are fallible, uncertainty is asymmetric, errors can be latent, and nominally redundant checks may share a cause. The useful borrow is not “use two agents.” It is a **graded assurance protocol** that separates:

1. the performer’s self-check;
2. an independent check against observable acceptance criteria;
3. authorization for consequential action;
4. multiple failure-containment layers; and
5. empirical feedback that updates trust by performer *and task class*.

My transfer verdict is **very likely (80-90%)** that this separation is structurally useful for an agent harness, because DOE explicitly distinguishes self-checking, concurrent checking, independent verification, and oversight, while modern evidence finds substantial correlation among LLM errors; the remaining uncertainty is whether the extra checks improve net owner outcomes enough to justify latency and cost in this particular harness. That premise is **UNVERIFIED; settle it with the shadow-mode and randomized-gate experiment specified in “Evaluation plan.”**

The analogy does **not** justify a universal two-agent rule, a scalar “agent trust score,” or an elaborate safety runtime. Those would discard the most important parts of the source mechanism: grading by consequence, verification against actual state, and independence from the performer’s cues.

## 1. What nuclear operations actually does

### 1.1 The two-person rule: control of unauthorized action, not proof of correctness

In DOE nuclear-material control, the two-person rule is defined as requiring at least two duly authorized people to be present simultaneously at one work location to decrease the probability of unauthorized actions. That purpose and wording appear in DOE transportation definitions ([DOE, “Definitions,” slide defining Two-Person Rule](https://rampac.energy.gov/docs/default-source/transportation/definitions.pdf?sfvrsn=e40f1e62_4)). A DOE environmental impact statement describes the same measure as one element among physical barriers, access controls, detection and alarm, personnel security, inventory monitoring, measurements, and inspections—not as a stand-alone guarantee ([DOE/NNSA, *Final EIS for Plutonium Pit Production at the Savannah River Site*, §2.1.1, p. 2-11](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2020/09/f79/final-eis-0541-srs-pit-production-vol-1-2020-09.pdf)).

For nuclear explosive configurations requiring person-to-person coverage, DOE training material is more concrete: two designated individuals with technical knowledge must observe or work on the configuration; both observe operations, both ensure only correct and authorized operations occur, and each is responsible for safe conduct ([DOE, *DOE O 452.1D and DOE O 452.2D Self-Study Modules*, scenario answer at p. 4](https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2013/06/f1/O-452-1D_O-452-2D_ssm.pdf)). DOE’s material-control assessment guide even supplies an adversarial test: ask one member of a required pair to leave for supplies and observe whether the remaining person stops and calls for replacement coverage ([DOE, *Material Control and Accountability Assessment Guide*, Appendix A, test C:4](https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2017/02/f34/MaterialControlandAccountabilityAssessmentGuide_Dec2016.pdf)).

Mechanically, this rule provides:

- continuous mutual presence at a defined hazard boundary;
- qualified rather than arbitrary redundancy;
- shared authority and responsibility to stop improper action;
- resistance to a lone malicious or mistaken actor; and
- a testable invariant: no single authorized person can act alone in the protected state.

It does **not** establish that two people independently derived the right answer. Both may share the same misconception, procedure, instrumentation, incentives, or fatigue. Its strongest mapping is therefore to **authorization and execution control**, not to factual confidence in an agent’s prose.

### 1.2 Independent verification: separation of cognition from the performer

DOE’s Human Performance Improvement Handbook draws the distinction that is most valuable for trust calibration. It says independent verification (IV) confirms the condition of equipment or the accuracy of documents or calculations required for safe operation. The verifier is separated in time and distance from the action or document production and checks the condition without relying on the performer’s observation or oral confirmation. DOE says IV tends to catch more errors than peer-checking or concurrent verification because the verifier did not produce or observe the work and is less cognitively affected by the performer. “Freedom of thought” is the operative definition of independence ([DOE-HDBK-1028-2009, Vol. 2, “Independent Verification,” pp. 46-48](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2026-04/1028-2009_Volume%202.pdf)).

The same handbook’s table of contents and tool-selection matrix place peer-checking, concurrent verification, independent verification, and peer review in distinct categories rather than treating all second-person activity as equivalent ([DOE-HDBK-1028-2009, Vol. 2, pp. iii and 3](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2026-04/1028-2009_Volume%202.pdf)). Its foreword is also explicit that these are illustrative tools to anticipate, prevent, and catch active errors and identify latent organizational errors; it is not a requirements document and organizations are not expected to use every tool ([DOE-HDBK-1028-2009, Vol. 2, Foreword, pp. i-ii](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2026-04/1028-2009_Volume%202.pdf)).

The source-field mechanism is therefore not “a second opinion.” It is an engineered attempt to reduce dependence:

- the verifier is qualified for the object being checked;
- the verifier receives the desired condition or acceptance criterion;
- the verifier directly observes the resulting object or state;
- production and verification are separated enough to limit cueing and anchoring;
- the verification method is designated for the component or document type; and
- the check is reserved for conditions whose undetected error could have adverse consequences.

The caveat is fundamental: organizational separation is a proxy for cognitive and causal independence, not proof of it. NRC human-reliability work treats dependency explicitly. NUREG/CR-6883 varies dependence based on whether the same crew, location, time, and cues are involved and makes recovery less credible as those factors align ([NRC, NUREG/CR-6883, dependency assessment worksheet, Appendix E](https://ww2.nrc.gov/sites/default/files/doc_library/cdn/legacy/reading-rm/doc-collections/nuregs/contract/cr6883/cr6883.pdf)). NRC also documents how human errors repeated across redundant trains can create common-cause failure ([NRC, Generic Safety Issue 172, “Common Cause Failures Related to Human Errors”](https://www.nrc.gov/sr0933/0399_0006_issue_172_multiple_system_responses_program_%28rev._2%29.html)).

### 1.3 STAR self-checking: local error interception around an action

DOE’s self-checking practice uses STAR: Stop, Think, Act, Review. Before acting, the performer pauses, focuses on the intended component or activity, predicts the expected outcome, takes the action, and then compares the result with that expectation. DOE applies it not only to physical manipulation but also to data entry, calculations, experiments, computer readouts, and changes to drawings or procedures ([DOE-HDBK-1028-2009, Vol. 2, “Self-Checking,” pp. 18-19](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2026-04/1028-2009_Volume%202.pdf)).

STAR works in its own field by forcing an expected result to exist *before* action and by closing the loop on observed state *after* action. It deliberately slows a critical step. DOE says these human-performance tools do not guarantee perfect performance; they reduce error probability when applied thoughtfully and rigorously ([DOE-HDBK-1028-2009, Vol. 2, “Human Performance Tools for Individuals,” p. 5](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2026-04/1028-2009_Volume%202.pdf)).

STAR is not independent verification. The same cognitive system chooses the action, predicts the result, and interprets the result. It is best at slips, wrong-target actions, omitted expectations, and failures to notice an anomalous response. It is weak against a coherent but false mental model.

### 1.4 Conservative decision-making: asymmetric burden under uncertainty

The NRC’s published safety-culture components say safety-affecting decisions should use a systematic process under uncertain or unexpected plant conditions. Conservative decision-making uses conservative assumptions and rejects an option unless it is demonstrated safe, rather than requiring proof that it is unsafe. It also calls for contingencies, abort criteria, interdisciplinary review of safety-significant decisions, and later effectiveness review of the assumptions and unintended consequences ([NRC, “Safety Culture Components,” Decision-Making and Work Control](https://www.nrc.gov/about-nrc/regulatory/enforcement/sc-componets011306)).

This is not generic risk aversion. It is an asymmetric decision rule tied to consequence and uncertainty:

- uncertainty does not silently become permission;
- the burden of evidence changes with safety significance;
- authority to choose a safe condition is explicit;
- abort and restart criteria are defined in advance; and
- decisions are reviewed after outcomes become visible.

The NRC’s current safety-culture policy applies expectations commensurate with the safety or security significance and with organizational nature and complexity ([NRC, “Safety Culture Policy Statement”](https://www.nrc.gov/about-nrc/safety-culture/sc-policy-statement)). This matters because conservatism applied indiscriminately would paralyze low-consequence work.

### 1.5 Defence in depth: prevention, detection, control, mitigation, response

IAEA INSAG-10 defines defence in depth as hierarchical levels of equipment and procedures that preserve physical barriers across normal operation, abnormal occurrences, and accidents. Its objectives are to compensate for human and component failure, maintain barriers, and protect people and the environment if barriers fail. Prevention is the first priority; subsequent levels detect/control abnormal operation, control design-basis accidents, limit severe accident progression, and mitigate external consequences ([IAEA, *Defence in Depth in Nuclear Safety*, INSAG-10, paras. 15-23 and Table I](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/publications/PDF/Pub1013e_web.pdf)).

Two details prevent a superficial translation. First, the levels must have independence: a failure at one level, including combinations of failures, should not propagate into later levels. Second, the presence of multiple layers does not justify continued operation with a layer unavailable ([IAEA, INSAG-10, paras. 22-23](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/publications/PDF/Pub1013e_web.pdf)). The IAEA also warns that events revealing failures across more than one level deserve particular attention as hidden deficiencies in the defence ([IAEA, INSAG-10, paras. 77-80](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/publications/PDF/Pub1013e_web.pdf)).

Defence in depth is thus a model of **failure containment**, not a method for averaging opinions. Its transferable unit is a chain of differently purposed controls whose failure modes are deliberately non-identical.

## 2. Adversarial transfer test

### 2.1 Structural features that do transfer

1. **Fallible performers can produce locally plausible errors.** Nuclear practice treats both active errors and latent conditions; agent outputs likewise can look coherent while being wrong. The DOE handbook explicitly applies its tools to engineers, procedure writers, scientists, and other knowledge workers whose mistakes enter documents and later cause events ([DOE-HDBK-1028-2009, Vol. 2, Foreword](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2026-04/1028-2009_Volume%202.pdf)).

2. **Trustworthiness depends on the work, not only the worker.** Nuclear controls are selected by task, component, hazard, and consequence. IAEA describes a graded approach as making analysis, documentation, and action commensurate with likelihood and severity ([IAEA, “IAEA Begins Work on Document to Help Regulators Use Graded Approach”](https://www.iaea.org/newscenter/news/iaea-begins-work-on-document-to-help-regulators-use-graded-approach)). This maps directly to agent × task-type calibration rather than a global leaderboard score.

3. **Evidence after action can differ from confidence before action.** STAR predicts an expected response and then observes the result. Agent work often leaves inspectable state: tests, diffs, exit codes, retrieved passages, database rows, screenshots, or external acknowledgements. Trust can be updated from that state instead of eloquence.

4. **Redundancy can share causes.** Nuclear HRA explicitly models dependency. In machine learning, a 2025 ICML study of more than 350 LLMs found substantial error correlation; on one leaderboard, two models agreed 60% of the time conditional on both being wrong, and high-performing models still had correlated errors across architectures and providers ([Kim et al., “Correlated Errors in Large Language Models,” ICML 2025](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/kim25e.html)). That makes independence a property to measure, not a label granted because two calls were made.

5. **The cost of false trust is asymmetric.** Releasing a wrong informational draft and executing an irreversible external action are not equivalent. Graded controls and conservative release criteria translate cleanly.

### 2.2 Structural features that do not transfer

1. **There is no universal agent “safe state.”** A reactor can be moved toward defined shutdown conditions. An agent asked for legal research, software design, a calendar change, or creative writing has different fallback states. “Stop” may mean abstain, preserve current state, produce a draft, request a human decision, or roll back. Every task class needs its own safe fallback.

2. **Two model instances are not two accountable people.** They cannot bear responsibility, resist coercion as moral agents, or independently refuse based on professional duty. A second agent can be a diagnostic instrument, but it cannot replace the human half of a two-person authorization boundary for highly consequential irreversible action.

3. **Language correctness is often not directly observable.** A valve position can be checked. The truth of a synthesis, strategic recommendation, or novel design may lack an immediate ground-truth sensor. “Independent verification” is therefore meaningful only when decomposed into checkable claims, executable acceptance tests, authoritative sources, or explicit human judgment.

4. **Model independence is unusually fragile.** Separate contexts can still share training data, model family, system prompt, retrieval source, task framing, tools, and benchmark blind spots. A “different model” is not necessarily a diverse failure mode; Kim et al. directly undermine that assumption ([ICML 2025](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/kim25e.html)).

5. **Nuclear procedures operate in a bounded engineered plant.** General-purpose agent tasks are open-world, adversarial, and distribution-shifting. A fixed procedure can become a ritual that increases apparent assurance without increasing truth.

6. **The consequence scale differs by orders of magnitude.** Nuclear controls tolerate burdens that would make routine agent assistance uneconomic. Grading is not an optimization detail; it is necessary for the analogy to remain viable.

### 2.3 Verdict

The two-person rule **does not transfer as a general truth-calibration mechanism**. It transfers narrowly as a control on authority: one agent or one user credential must not be able to carry out a designated high-consequence action alone.

Independent verification, conservative decision-making, STAR, and defence in depth **do transfer as design patterns**, provided that:

- verification checks an artifact or resulting state against predeclared criteria;
- the verifier is blind to unnecessary performer cues;
- dependence is recorded and measured;
- assurance is graded by consequence and reversibility;
- later observed outcomes update empirical trust; and
- no layer is described as proof of semantic truth.

It is **likely (55-80%)** that blind, criterion-based verification will catch materially more consequential agent errors than same-context self-review, because DOE’s mechanism is specifically designed to reduce performer cueing and both NRC HRA and ICML evidence identify dependency as a failure amplifier. The size of improvement in this owner’s workloads is **UNVERIFIED; settle it with a labeled replay comparing same-context review, blind same-model review, blind cross-model review, deterministic checks, and human adjudication.**

## 3. Concrete harness design

### Proposal A — Task-conditioned trust ledger — BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)

Do not assign an agent a scalar reputation. Maintain a small append-only record keyed by a versioned performer identity and a coarse task class:

`performer version × task class × consequence class × verification mode → observed outcome`

The minimum record for each completed consequential task should contain:

- performer identity: provider/model/version or agent/skill revision, with “unknown version” explicit;
- task class: e.g. factual retrieval, synthesis, code edit, shell operation, destructive filesystem action, external communication, scheduling, credential/security, or open-ended design;
- consequence class and reversibility: draft-only, local/reversible, external/reversible, irreversible or safety-/rights-affecting;
- the output’s claims or action acceptance criteria;
- evidence type: deterministic test, direct state observation, authoritative-source match, independent model judgment, human judgment, later operational outcome, or none;
- verification dependence: same context, fresh context/same model, different model/shared evidence, different model/independent evidence, deterministic oracle, or human/domain expert;
- verifier decision and confidence band;
- eventual outcome: confirmed correct, confirmed incorrect, partially correct, disputed, or still unknown;
- failure mode and whether the verifier caught it before release.

Report a trust estimate only when it names the cell and evidence base: for example, “Agent A / code-edit / local-reversible / deterministic-test verified: 47 confirmed, 3 incorrect.” With small samples, display counts and a conservative interval or lower bound rather than a precise percentage. Never treat “no complaint” as “correct”; it remains unknown. Age or segment records when the model, agent instructions, toolchain, or task distribution changes.

What it targets: hidden variation where the same agent is strong at code manipulation but weak at source attribution; upgrades that invalidate old reputation; inflated trust from unverified completions; and verifier modes that approve many later-confirmed failures.

It is **very likely (80-90%)** that task-conditioned records are more informative than one global agent score, because both nuclear graded-control sources and the very definition of the target problem condition assurance on activity and consequence. Whether the proposed taxonomy has adequate sample density is **UNVERIFIED; settle it by replaying 500-1,000 labeled historical outcomes, measuring coverage per cell, and merging cells until each reported rate meets a predeclared minimum sample.**

Implementation constraint: use a flat JSONL or SQLite table already accessible to the harness. Do not add a vector database, knowledge graph, observability SaaS, trained critic, or new orchestration runtime.

### Proposal B — STAR action envelope — BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)

Wrap consequential tool actions in a compact, machine-readable action envelope:

1. **Stop:** identify the exact target, consequence class, and whether authorization or required evidence is missing.
2. **Think:** state the intended state transition, expected observable result, invariants that must remain true, and abort condition.
3. **Act:** execute one bounded action.
4. **Review:** inspect the resulting state through a mechanically independent signal where feasible; compare actual with expected; stop on mismatch.

Example for a file edit:

```text
target: /exact/path/config.json
expected: key X changes from false to true; no other keys change
abort_if: file identity or old value differs
review: parse JSON + inspect diff
```

Example for an external message:

```text
target: named channel and recipients
expected: one draft with specified attachment; no send until authorization
abort_if: recipient resolution is ambiguous
review: API acknowledgement + read-back of message metadata
```

Self-reported STAR completion earns no trust credit by itself. Credit comes from the review evidence. The “Review” signal must not merely reread the tool’s prose acknowledgment when an independent state query is available.

What it targets: wrong-file/wrong-recipient actions, stale preconditions, commands that return success without the expected state, partial application, and unexamined side effects. It does not target a coherent but wrong plan unless the expected-result or invariant exposes it.

It is **likely (55-80%)** that STAR envelopes reduce wrong-target and unnoticed-postcondition errors in consequential tool use, based on DOE’s use of expected outcome and post-action review across equipment, calculations, and computer data entry. This harness effect is **UNVERIFIED; settle it with a randomized trial on reversible tool tasks, scoring wrong-target rate, unexpected-diff rate, recovery rate, latency, and owner intervention.**

### Proposal C — Blind independent-verification packet — BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus) — UNVERIFIED

For selected outputs, create a verifier packet containing only:

- the original task and constraints;
- a neutral task-class rubric;
- the produced artifact or observed post-action state;
- authoritative inputs that the verifier is allowed to use, or permission to retrieve them independently;
- explicit acceptance criteria; and
- a request for claim-level pass/fail/unknown with evidence.

Withhold the performer’s chain of thought, self-confidence, persuasive rationale, prior verifier votes, and desired verdict. Where feasible, randomize answer ordering and strip stylistic identity. The verifier must first generate its own expected checks before seeing optional performer notes. For code or data tasks, deterministic tests and direct state inspection take precedence over model judgment. For factual work, the verifier independently opens the cited source and checks whether it entails the claim. For open-ended design, the verifier checks constraints, counterexamples, and unsupported premises; it does not pronounce the design “true.”

Record an independence vector rather than an “independent: yes/no” flag:

```text
context_shared: false
model_family_shared: true
system_prompt_shared: true
retrieval_shared: false
tools_shared: true
acceptance_oracle: deterministic-test
```

Route disagreements to abstention, further evidence, or human adjudication according to consequence. Do not resolve them by majority vote alone.

What it targets: anchoring on the performer’s explanation, fabricated or non-entailing citations, acceptance criteria silently weakened after seeing the answer, same-context self-rubber-stamping, and errors that persist across repeated samples.

The heading is marked **UNVERIFIED** because the recommendation assumes that blind packaging creates useful cognitive independence in this harness. It is **likely (55-80%)** to improve defect detection over exposed-rationale review: DOE defines independence through freedom from performer cues, and correlated-error evidence shows why repeated similar judgments are insufficient. Settle it with the labeled replay described in the evaluation plan, measuring false accepts and false rejects separately by task class.

### Proposal D — Graded two-key release gate — CONFIG (settings/JSON only)

Use consequence and reversibility—not agent confidence—to choose the release gate:

| Class | Examples | Minimum release condition |
|---|---|---|
| G0: advisory | brainstorming, private draft | performer output; provenance visible |
| G1: local and reversible | bounded edit with clean diff | STAR review plus deterministic/local validation where available |
| G2: external but reversible | draft PR, tentative calendar hold, recoverable cloud edit | independent artifact/state check; explicit target read-back |
| G3: irreversible or rights-/security-affecting | deletion without recovery, credential change, financial/legal submission, public send | qualified human authorization plus independent verification; fail closed on ambiguity |

At G3, an agent verifier is not the second key. One key is evidence that acceptance criteria are met; the other is an accountable human authorization over the exact resolved action. Bind authorization to a digest of target, payload, scope, and expiry so that approval cannot be replayed against a changed action.

For G1-G2, automatic verification may release the result if the check has a validated oracle. For tasks with no objective oracle, downgrade the output to a labeled draft or raise the gate.

What it targets: a single agent both proposing and executing a consequential action; permission granted to an ambiguous target; approval reused after scope changes; and high-confidence prose substituting for owner authorization.

It is **very likely (80-90%)** that a bound human second key reduces lone-actor unauthorized G3 actions, because that is the direct control objective of DOE’s two-person rule. Whether the G0-G3 boundary above matches this owner’s risk tolerance is **UNVERIFIED; settle it with a tabletop review of 100 representative actions, requiring the owner to classify consequence, reversibility, and acceptable friction before enabling enforcement.**

### Proposal E — Conservative uncertainty and abstention rule — CONFIG (settings/JSON only)

For G2-G3 decisions, invert the default burden:

- proceed only when the required positive evidence is present;
- do not require a verifier to prove an output wrong;
- treat missing, conflicting, stale, or non-independent evidence as unresolved;
- preserve the current state or produce a draft when unresolved;
- predeclare abort, rollback, escalation, and restart criteria; and
- record the assumption that caused escalation so later outcomes can calibrate it.

For G0-G1, remain permissive and label uncertainty rather than blocking useful work. “Conservative” must not mean multiplying review on every task. The rule is conditional on consequence and the availability of a safe fallback.

What it targets: confidently proceeding through missing evidence, rationalizing an ambiguous target, using “not proven unsafe” as authorization, and quietly weakening acceptance criteria to finish.

It is **likely (55-80%)** that this rule reduces severe false releases while increasing some false blocks, because it directly changes the decision threshold in the direction described by NRC conservative decision-making. The net utility and false-block cost are **UNVERIFIED; settle them with shadow decisions for four weeks, then compare severe false-release rate, unnecessary escalation rate, median delay, and owner override direction against the current path.**

### Proposal F — Five-layer assurance path with independence audit — BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus) — UNVERIFIED

Translate defence in depth by function, not by nuclear terminology:

1. **Prevent:** constrain target scope, use typed parameters, pin authoritative inputs, and state acceptance criteria before generation/action.
2. **Detect:** STAR review, parsers, tests, schema checks, citation entailment checks, diff inspection, and postcondition reads.
3. **Control:** blind independent verification, permission boundary, rate/impact limit, and stop-on-disagreement.
4. **Recover:** transactional staging, backup/version history, idempotency key, compensating action, or quarantine of the untrusted result.
5. **Respond and learn:** human notification for consequential failures, outcome adjudication, trust-ledger update, and an extent-of-condition search for similar outputs.

Every task route should declare which layers exist and their failure dependencies. A later layer must not make an earlier required layer optional. If prevention and detection share the same parser, source, or model assumption, record them as a common-cause cluster rather than two independent defences.

What it targets: controls that all fail from one stale source or shared prompt, “verification” that merely repeats generation, successful mitigation being used to excuse weak prevention, and repeated failures that never alter future trust.

The heading is **UNVERIFIED** because the useful granularity of these five layers and the adequacy of a sub-200-line router are harness-specific. It is **likely (55-80%)** that explicitly recording shared dependencies exposes false redundancy, based on IAEA’s independence requirement and warnings about hidden multi-level failures, plus NRC and ICML evidence on common causes. Settle it by dependency-mapping 200 consequential traces and injecting one failure at a time (stale source, wrong target, dishonest tool acknowledgment, same-model misconception, verifier outage) to observe whether at least one genuinely distinct layer detects or contains it.

## 4. How the pieces calibrate trust

The design changes the question from “Do I trust Agent A?” to five narrower questions:

1. **Prior:** What happened on previous *adjudicated* outputs from this performer version in this task class?
2. **Evidence:** What directly observable evidence supports this particular output?
3. **Independence:** How causally independent are the evidence and verifier from the performer’s failure modes?
4. **Consequence:** What happens if the output is wrong, and is the action reversible?
5. **Decision:** Given the prior, evidence, dependence, and consequence, release, draft, abstain, escalate, or recover?

A useful release record could look like:

```json
{
  "performer": "agent-A@model-version+prompt-revision",
  "task_class": "factual-synthesis",
  "consequence": "G2",
  "prior": {"confirmed_correct": 31, "confirmed_incorrect": 4, "unknown": 19},
  "evidence": ["source-opened", "claim-entailment-human-sample"],
  "verification": {
    "mode": "blind-cross-model",
    "shared_model_family": false,
    "shared_retrieval": false,
    "decision": "partial",
    "unsupported_claims": ["c7"]
  },
  "release": "draft-with-c7-removed"
}
```

The unknown count is important. Excluding unknown outcomes would create survivorship bias: easily checked tasks would dominate the trust score, while polished but never-adjudicated work would appear harmless.

No confidence number should be emitted from the agent’s verbal certainty alone. The Nature Machine Intelligence study on perceived versus model confidence shows that users’ confidence from generated language can diverge from model confidence and actual accuracy; presenting uncertainty conditioned on measured model confidence improved calibration in its studied question-answering settings ([Steyvers et al., “What large language models know and what people think they know,” 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-024-00976-7)). That result does not establish calibration for autonomous tool tasks. It supports the narrower warning that linguistic confidence and empirical reliability are different objects.

## 5. What this would catch—and what it would still miss

Because this unit did not inspect the harness, it cannot honestly claim that any listed control is currently absent. The following are failure classes the proposed mechanism is designed to catch *if the existing path does not already catch them*:

| Failure class | Nuclear-derived catch mechanism | Residual weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Correct-looking answer unsupported by cited source | Blind claim-level source verification | Both agents may retrieve the same incomplete source universe |
| Agent strong in one task type trusted in another | Task-conditioned ledger | Sparse cells and distribution shift |
| Wrong file, account, recipient, or environment | STAR precondition + exact target read-back + post-state query | Bad identity resolution shared by both queries |
| One agent proposes and executes an irreversible action | Human-bound two-key G3 gate | Human automation bias or careless approval |
| Second agent repeats the first agent’s misconception | Independence vector + outcome-measured verifier score | Different models can still share training and framing |
| Tool reports success but state did not change | Mechanically independent postcondition read | Read path can share the same faulty backend |
| Multiple controls depend on one stale source | Defence-dependency audit | Dependencies can be unknown or misrecorded |
| Missing evidence treated as permission | Conservative positive-evidence release rule | Excessive abstention if thresholds are badly graded |
| Later-discovered defect does not affect future routing | Outcome adjudication + trust-ledger update + extent-of-condition search | Many outcomes remain unknown |
| Persuasive self-review hides uncertainty | Blind verifier packet stripped of rationale/confidence | Style leakage and shared priors remain |

The design will still miss novel false premises for which no layer has an independent source or oracle; coordinated or correlated model misconceptions; poisoned authoritative sources; human approvals made without attention; delayed consequences that are never linked back to the task; and task-taxonomy drift. Defence in depth reduces propagation; it cannot manufacture ground truth.

## 6. Evaluation plan

### Phase 0 — Detector validation

Before asserting that a mechanism catches or misses a failure class, validate each detector on a known-positive and a known-negative. For example:

- citation checker: one source that clearly entails a claim and one that clearly does not;
- postcondition checker: one action known to change state and one no-op;
- verifier-independence parser: one packet with deliberately shared context and one with fresh context;
- trust ledger: one seeded confirmed error and one still-unknown outcome.

No “zero failures” conclusion is valid unless the detector first finds its seeded known-present control. This applies the unit’s required two-signal honesty rule to the proposed harness itself.

### Phase 1 — Labeled replay

Sample completed tasks across task type, performer, consequence, and apparent success. Use two mechanically independent adjudication signals wherever possible: e.g. deterministic test plus human diff review; authoritative source entailment plus subject-matter review; API state query plus external read-back. Preserve “disputed” and “unknown” rather than forcing labels.

For each output, compare:

1. no review;
2. same-context self-review;
3. fresh-context same-model review;
4. blind cross-model review with shared evidence;
5. blind cross-model review with independently retrieved evidence;
6. deterministic/domain-specific oracle where available; and
7. human adjudication.

Measure false acceptance, false rejection, defect class, latency, token/tool cost, and pairwise error correlation. Report results by task class and consequence class. A verifier that catches many errors but blocks most correct work is not automatically useful.

### Phase 2 — Shadow-mode prospective trial

Run routing and proposed gate decisions without changing releases. Log what would have been blocked, escalated, or downgraded. When outcomes become available, compare them with the shadow decision. This directly tests whether conservative gates find consequential defects or merely add ceremony.

Predeclare success criteria. A reasonable starting rule is not asserted as fact: select thresholds only after the owner assigns relative cost to a severe false release, an unnecessary human escalation, and added latency.

### Phase 3 — Randomized rollout on reversible work

Randomize eligible G1-G2 tasks between current handling and STAR/independent-verification handling. Stratify by performer and task type. Keep G3 out of experiments that could expose the owner to irreversible harm; use tabletop or sandbox simulation there.

Primary outcomes:

- confirmed consequential error reaching release;
- wrong-target or wrong-scope action;
- verifier false-accept and false-reject rates;
- owner corrections per completed task;
- time to useful completion;
- cost per prevented consequential error; and
- error correlation by performer/verifier pair.

### Phase 4 — Failure injection and defence independence

Inject bounded, recoverable faults: stale input, swapped target identifier, non-entailing citation, partial tool failure, misleading success message, missing acceptance criterion, and a shared false premise. Require two independent signals before declaring each layer effective. Record whether layers prevent, detect, control, recover, or merely report.

The central falsifier is simple: if blind verification does not lower consequential false accepts relative to self-review after controlling for cost and task class, remove it from that route. Nuclear pedigree is not evidence of harness utility.

## 7. Alternatives rejected

### Universal “two agents must agree”

Rejected. It confuses authorization with epistemic verification, ignores correlated errors, and lets majority agreement substitute for evidence. DOE’s IV distinction and the ICML correlated-error result both cut against it.

### One global trust score per agent

Rejected. It hides task-specific competence, verification mode, consequence, version drift, sample size, and unknown outcomes. A high aggregate score can be Simpson’s-paradox bait when easy tasks dominate.

### Same-agent self-critique as independent verification

Rejected. STAR remains useful as local self-checking, but DOE explicitly distinguishes self-checking from independent verification. The same context and model share cues and causal failure modes.

### Always require a human

Rejected. It ignores the graded approach, creates habituation and approval fatigue, and spends accountable attention on low-consequence reversible work. Humans are reserved for consequence, ambiguity, or lack of an adequate oracle—not used as decorative confirmation.

### Learned trust critic or trained reward model

Rejected under the anti-overfit gate. It would introduce a trained critic, one of the explicitly disallowed previously rejected components, and would itself require calibration, distribution-shift management, and correlated-error analysis.

### New orchestration or durable-execution runtime

Rejected under the anti-overfit gate. The proposed records, action envelope, routing rules, and verifier packet can ride existing hooks/skills/tool calls. A second runtime would add failure modes without evidence that the existing bus cannot carry the controls.

### Vector database, knowledge graph, or observability SaaS for trust memory

Rejected under the anti-overfit gate. The trust ledger is structured, small, and outcome-oriented. JSONL or SQLite is sufficient. Semantic retrieval is not needed to count adjudicated cells or record dependency.

## 8. Anti-overfit gate and minimal architecture

All proposals use the existing harness bus:

- **CONFIG (settings/JSON only):** consequence routing, two-key gate policy, conservative release rules.
- **BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus):** append-only trust record, STAR envelope/postcondition check, blind verifier packet, and dependency audit/routing glue.

No proposal requires a second orchestration runtime, vector database or knowledge graph, durable-execution engine, trained critic, observability SaaS, or guardrail framework. The mechanism is deliberately reducible to a few records and gates. If the empirical evaluation shows no net benefit for a route, that route should have fewer layers, not a more elaborate critic.

The radical part is conceptual rather than infrastructural: stop treating trust as an agent personality trait and treat it as a release decision supported by task-conditioned outcomes, evidence quality, verifier dependence, and consequence. The implementation remains small.

## 9. Gaps and limits

1. **Current-harness coverage is unknown.** This unit was instructed not to ground in the local repo and therefore did not determine which proposed controls already exist, are absent, or are broken. Any implementation unit must perform two mechanically independent checks before making those claims and must validate each detector on a known-present item first.

2. **Benefit sizes are UNVERIFIED.** Every predicted harness benefit above names the replay, randomized rollout, shadow trial, or failure-injection experiment that would settle it. Nuclear operational experience establishes mechanism rationale, not effect size for LLM agents.

3. **Open-ended correctness remains hard.** Where there is no observable state, authoritative source, executable specification, or accountable expert judgment, the system can measure agreement and process compliance but not truth.

4. **Independence is multidimensional and imperfect.** Model provider, family, prompt, context, retrieval, tools, and acceptance criteria can all create dependence. The proposed vector makes assumptions inspectable; it does not prove independence.

5. **Calibration requires delayed labels.** Many real outputs never receive a definitive outcome. The ledger must retain unknowns and avoid presenting a selectively labeled rate as universal reliability.

6. **The DOE handbook is guidance, not a binding universal procedure.** The handbook itself calls its practices illustrative and says organizations should select tools appropriate to their needs ([DOE-HDBK-1028-2009, Vol. 2, Foreword](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2026-04/1028-2009_Volume%202.pdf)). This supports experimentation and grading, not cargo-cult adoption.

## 10. Final recommendation

Adopt the borrow as a **small, graded assurance experiment**, not as “nuclear-style agents.” Begin with the task-conditioned trust ledger and blind replay, because they measure whether the premise is true before changing production behavior. Then add STAR postcondition envelopes to reversible consequential actions. Enable conservative two-key release only for owner-defined G3 actions. Keep agent verification subordinate to deterministic evidence and human authorization, and continuously measure verifier dependence.

It is **very likely (80-90%)** that this is a faithful translation of the useful nuclear mechanisms because it preserves their source-field distinctions—self-check versus independent verification, authorization versus correctness, prevention versus mitigation, and grading by consequence—while explicitly rejecting the non-transferable assumption that two similar agents constitute independent accountable operators. Whether it becomes the best trust-calibration mechanism for this owner is **UNVERIFIED; the deciding experiment is the stratified labeled replay followed by a shadow-mode and randomized reversible-task rollout with false-accept, false-reject, latency, owner-correction, and cost outcomes.**
