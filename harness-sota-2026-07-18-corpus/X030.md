# X030 — Cross-domain borrow: aviation safety → elastic agent orchestration

## Executive result

The useful aviation borrow is **not a formula for choosing worker count**. The sources reviewed do not establish such a formula. The useful borrow is a **crew-expansion safety envelope**: before releasing another concurrent task, a single accountable mission owner checks workload and opportunity cost; every active participant has an explicit operational role; assignment and handoff require positive acknowledgment plus a state brief; the system continuously annunciates “who is doing what”; critical actions receive independent challenge/response; and near misses feed a protected learning channel.

This is a **partial transfer, with a strong disanalogy**. It is **very likely (80-90%)** that these mechanisms transfer to agent orchestration as controls for coordination failure, because FAA guidance directly treats task allocation, overload avoidance, role clarity, cross-checking, interruption recovery, and automation-mode awareness as one connected operating system, while agent orchestration has the same abstract hazards: concurrent tasks, incomplete local views, delayed effects, and a coordinator with finite attention. The evidence is FAA AC 120-51E pp. 1, 10-13 and AC 120-71B pp. 18-29, cited below. It is only **likely (55-80%)** that the envelope improves end-to-end agent outcomes, because the transfer has not been tested in this harness; the settling experiment is the staged replay/A-B test specified under “Evaluation.”

It would be a category error to say “aviation solved optimal swarm size.” Commercial flight decks normally operate with a certificated and procedurally fixed complement, not a worker pool that can be elastically spawned at negligible startup cost. A relevant flight-deck crewing analysis explicitly says crew management itself carries workload and that doubling crew does not halve workload, but it analyzes one-versus-two/distributed crewing rather than arbitrary elastic team size ([Harris, *Applied Ergonomics*, 2016, lines 84-99](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003687015300211)). Therefore aviation supplies the **gate and failure detectors**, while the harness must learn its own stopping threshold empirically.

## What aviation actually does in its own field

### 1. Crew Resource Management is a task-allocation system, not merely “communicate better”

FAA AC 120-51E defines CRM around situation awareness, communication, teamwork, **task allocation**, and decision-making within SOPs (p. 1). It recognizes that inadequate design and management of crew tasks can create group errors and system-performance degradation (pp. 1-2). Its workload-management curriculum calls for proper allocation of tasks, avoiding overload in oneself and others, prioritizing during high workload, and excluding nonessential distractions from critical work (p. 10). It also asks organizations to assess CRM systematically, using observation, participant reports, and repeated measurement (pp. 11-12) ([FAA AC 120-51E](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC120-51e.pdf)).

The important point is operational: “use all resources” is constrained by workload. The objective is not maximum utilization of every person; it is safe allocation while preserving situation awareness and adherence to the critical procedure.

Degani and Wiener make the release decision unusually concrete. Before a captain initiates another checklist, the captain should ask: are other pilots overloaded; what is lost if they run the checklist rather than participate in the current task; and what is the cost of delay? After initiation, if interruption, distraction, time pressure, or workload degrades quality, the captain should stop, make time, and potentially rerun the checklist (pp. 44-45) ([NASA CR-177549](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-11/NASA%20Ames%20Rpt%20CR%20177549%20.pdf)).

That is the closest aviation analogue to a spawn gate: **do not ask only whether another task exists; ask what attention and monitoring capacity its release removes from the mission.**

### 2. PF/PM role separation preserves one owner of the trajectory and one independent monitor

FAA AC 120-71B requires clearly delineated Pilot Flying (PF) and Pilot Monitoring (PM) duties and says shared mental models depend on a standardized task model (pp. 5-6). The PF owns the aircraft trajectory; the PM monitors state and systems, calls deviations, and intervenes. At any time one pilot is PF and one is PM. If the PF must do distracting work, control is transferred; transfer is positive, with verbal assignment, verbal acceptance, and a short state brief (pp. 23-24) ([FAA AC 120-71B](https://www.faa.gov/documentlibrary/media/advisory_circular/ac_120-71b.pdf)).

This is not symmetrical “everyone collaborates.” It preserves:

- one accountable controller of the primary trajectory;
- a distinct monitor who is not silently converted into another executor;
- an explicit handoff boundary;
- a shared state refresh after absence or distraction.

For critical checklist items, FAA guidance uses one crewmember to read and another to confirm/respond; programming may be performed by one and independently confirmed by another; configuration changes require a command and acknowledgment (pp. 18-19). If an interruption makes checklist position unclear, the relevant section is restarted (p. 19) ([FAA AC 120-71B](https://www.faa.gov/documentlibrary/media/advisory_circular/ac_120-71b.pdf)). The mechanism is **challenge-response plus independent confirmation**, not a second person casually reading the first person’s answer.

The checklist report documents the failure mode when roles collapse. In its Northwest 255 discussion, the first officer absorbed captain duties on top of his own; nobody remained to back up and verify his actions, increasing vulnerability to distraction and memory lapses (pp. 45-46) ([NASA CR-177549](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-11/NASA%20Ames%20Rpt%20CR%20177549%20.pdf)). This is structurally close to an orchestrator that begins doing worker tasks and ceases to maintain the global plan.

### 3. Aviation makes automation mode and future behavior visible

Sarter and Woods define automation surprise as an automated system taking an unexpected action or failing to behave as anticipated; their Airbus A-320 corpus links surprise to lack of awareness of current and future automation status and behavior ([NASA record](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20040112190)).

FAA AC 120-71B turns that research into operating guidance. It calls for mode callouts and confirmation appropriate to workload; describes “Confirm, Activate, Monitor, Intervene” and “Verbalize, Verify, Monitor” as structured error traps; and says the Flight Mode Annunciator depicts “who is doing what” (pp. 27-29). It explicitly warns that effects may be delayed and latent errors may appear later. Both crew members must understand current mode, controlling system, consequences, and likely subsequent mode ([FAA AC 120-71B](https://www.faa.gov/documentlibrary/media/advisory_circular/ac_120-71b.pdf)).

For orchestration, worker count is only half the state. The other half is mode: queued, assigned, acknowledged, executing, blocked, waiting on dependency, proposing a write, validating, canceled, or complete. A swarm with an integer “4 active” but no visible modes recreates the exact awareness problem.

### 4. Protected near-miss reporting is a learning mechanism, not proof

NASA describes ASRS as voluntary, confidential, and non-punitive; reports are de-identified, analyzed, and shared to identify hazards and best practices. NASA is an independent third party, while FAA offers reporting incentives and qualified enforcement protections ([NASA ASRS overview](https://www.nasa.gov/human-systems-integration-division/aviation-safety-reporting-system-overview/)). AC 00-46F makes the boundary explicit: deliberate acts, accidents, criminal matters, and competency issues do not receive the ordinary protection, and filing does not erase other required reports ([ASRS/AC 00-46F](https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/overview/immunity.html)).

Equally important, ASRS says it does **not** verify or validate submitted narratives ([ASRS database caveat](https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/search/database.html)). Therefore the correct borrow is: preserve low-friction, candid near-miss narratives as a hypothesis stream, then correlate them with objective execution telemetry. It is not “a worker said coordination was bad, therefore coordination was bad.”

## Concrete translation: the Crew Expansion Envelope

### Proposal A — UNVERIFIED: pre-spawn crew-expansion gate

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).**

Add a pre-spawn hook that requires a compact `crew_release` record before a new parallel worker starts:

```yaml
mission_owner: agent/root
candidate_task: verify API contract against official docs
deliverable: evidence memo, no writes
dependencies_satisfied: true
write_set: []
independent_of_active_tasks: true
worker_role: executor
monitor_for_critical_output: agent/root
opportunity_cost: owner loses one review slot
stop_condition: first primary-source contradiction resolved
handoff_required: false
```

The hook evaluates aviation’s three release questions in harness terms:

1. **Capacity:** Is the mission owner or proposed worker already overloaded? Operational proxy: unreviewed completions, unread worker messages, unresolved conflicts, and pending approval/handoff count.
2. **Opportunity cost:** What current monitoring/integration task becomes slower if another worker is released? A spawn is rejected or deferred when there is no review slot for its eventual output.
3. **Delay cost:** What is lost by keeping the task queued? Time-critical, dependency-unblocking work can outrank more concurrency.

It also rejects a spawn when the task lacks an independently mergeable deliverable, overlaps an active write set, depends on unresolved upstream state, or has no acceptance test. Rejection is not permanent: it returns a reason and the condition under which release becomes safe.

The specific threshold for “owner overloaded” is **UNVERIFIED; replay completed multi-worker traces and sweep thresholds for unread messages, review queue age, and unintegrated completions to settle it**. Starting with a hard-coded universal worker cap would be weak: aviation’s own mechanism gates on phase and workload rather than an invariant headcount.

Expected catch: duplicate research, workers racing on the same files, speculative work launched before a dependency resolves, and a “completion storm” that leaves the owner integrating rather than steering. It is **very likely (80-90%)** to identify those structural conditions when the event data are present, because each is mechanically expressible as overlapping work sets, unmet dependencies, or a growing review queue. It is only **likely (55-80%)** to improve task success, because some high-value tasks benefit from redundant exploration and because the subject harness’s actual event fidelity is **UNVERIFIED; instrument a shadow-mode replay to settle it**.

### Proposal B — UNVERIFIED: mission-owner/monitor role contract with positive handoff

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).**

Require every concurrent run to have exactly one `mission_owner`, even if many workers execute. The owner maintains objective, dependency graph, acceptance criteria, and final integration. For a critical output, designate a `monitor` whose task is to test claims or artifacts independently rather than produce another answer. The owner must not silently become an executor; if ownership transfers, require an assignment, acceptance, and compact state brief:

```text
CONTROL agent/root -> agent/lead-2
STATE objective=v3; active=2; blocked=1; dirty_paths=[...]; next_decision=...
ACCEPT agent/lead-2
```

The “exactly one owner” invariant is a direct translation of one PF at a time; it does not forbid collaborative decisions. The monitor is a scarce role, not a worker attached to every subtask. Independent cross-check is reserved for irreversible writes, security-sensitive changes, externally published claims, or a result on the critical path.

Expected catch: ambiguous authority, double integration, silent ownership changes, a coordinator disappearing into local implementation, and “verification” performed by the same reasoning path that generated the result. It is **likely (55-80%)** to reduce those failures, based on FAA’s explicit PF/PM delineation and positive-transfer guidance, but effectiveness for non-human agents is **UNVERIFIED; inject handoff, stale-state, and conflicting-owner faults in replay to settle it**.

### Proposal C — UNVERIFIED: orchestration mode annunciator and callouts

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).**

Emit one append-only state transition per worker and derive a terse live panel:

```text
W1 docs-verify  EXECUTING  owns:no-files  depends:none       age:04:12
W2 test-linux   BLOCKED    owns:tmp/**    depends:build-17   age:01:03
W3 patch-api    WRITE-READY owns:src/a.ts depends:W1         age:06:41
OWNER           REVIEWING  queue:2        conflicts:1
```

Allowed transitions should be explicit. State-changing calls use the aviation-like sequence `confirm -> activate -> monitor -> intervene`: resolve target and preconditions; activate worker/tool; observe acknowledgment and state transition; intervene on timeout, conflict, or unexpected mode. Delayed worker effects remain associated with the originating assignment so that a late completion cannot masquerade as current work.

The panel should alert on semantic conditions rather than raw worker count:

- two workers claim the same exclusive write set;
- a worker executes without acknowledging its assignment/version;
- a completion is based on a superseded objective;
- owner review queue grows while more workers are spawned;
- all workers are blocked on one dependency;
- a monitor becomes an executor on the artifact it must independently check;
- a worker finishes after cancellation or handoff and its result is integrated without revalidation.

Expected catch: “automation surprise” in the orchestrator—late results, stale briefs, hidden blocks, role drift, and unexpected writes. It is **very likely (80-90%)** to make those states observable if transitions are emitted reliably, because the annunciator directly renders the relevant state. It is only **likely (55-80%)** to cause timely intervention; alert usefulness and false-positive rate are **UNVERIFIED; run fault-injection replays and measure detection latency and unnecessary aborts to settle it**.

### Proposal D — UNVERIFIED: confidential orchestration near-miss card

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).**

At join/end, allow a worker or owner to emit a short, non-scoring `near_miss` card:

```yaml
observed: two agents researched the same source set
why_it_nearly_hurt: review queue delayed the critical patch
recovery: canceled lower-value branch
suggested_detector: source/query overlap + review_queue_age
```

Separate this channel from worker ranking or reward. De-identification is less meaningful for software agents than for employees, but separation from performance scoring preserves the functional incentive: report coordination hazards even when the run ultimately succeeds. As in ASRS, narrative reports are hypotheses. Promote a detector or rule only when a second, mechanically independent signal supports it—for example transcript claim plus event-log overlap, or worker report plus file-write collision.

Expected catch: recoveries and close calls absent from a binary success/failure metric, especially redundant work canceled in time and review saturation that did not yet cause a bad final answer. It is **likely (55-80%)** to improve the discovery stream, because ASRS demonstrates the value of protected frontline reporting, but agent self-report quality and incentive effects are **UNVERIFIED; compare blinded near-miss cards against independently reconstructed event traces to settle it**.

## The stopping rule: empirical, marginal, and safety-constrained

Aviation contributes the safety constraint; the harness must supply the quantitative optimizer.

At each possible spawn, estimate from recent comparable runs:

```text
marginal_value(worker n+1)
  = expected accepted work that advances a critical-path deliverable
    - expected review/integration time
    - expected conflict/rework cost
    - expected context/state-sync cost
```

Spawn only if:

1. the candidate passes the crew-expansion gate;
2. a review slot will exist when it is expected to finish;
3. expected marginal value remains positive;
4. the task is not in a “sterile” phase where integration, destructive action, or final verification needs concentrated control.

Scale down when accepted useful work per additional worker declines across matched tasks, review queue age rises, conflict/rework rises, or the owner spends more time synchronizing than deciding. The actual metric weights and matching scheme are **UNVERIFIED; randomize allowed concurrency over replayable task strata and fit outcome/latency/cost curves to settle them**.

I am **likely (55-80%)** to prefer this marginal gate over a fixed maximum, because both the FAA workload guidance and the Harris crewing analysis emphasize phase-dependent workload and coordination cost, while a universal maximum ignores task divisibility. The range is not higher because historical agent traces may be confounded by task difficulty and model improvements; a randomized prospective trial is needed.

## Adversarial transfer analysis

### Structural correspondences that do transfer

| Aviation structure | Agent-orchestration structure | Transfer judgment |
|---|---|---|
| One evolving aircraft trajectory | One evolving mission/repository state | **Very likely (80-90%)** correspondence: both require one authoritative current state; evidence is FAA’s PF ownership and explicit handoff model. |
| Multiple concurrent demands under limited attention | Workers completing, blocking, and requesting review concurrently | **Very likely (80-90%)** correspondence: FAA defines high workload as multiple demands requiring prioritization; harness applicability remains **UNVERIFIED; measure owner review queues to settle it**. |
| PF/PM role differentiation | mission owner / independent verifier | **Likely (55-80%)** transfer: role separation is structurally sound, but an AI monitor may share failure modes with the executor. |
| Autoflight modes with delayed effects | asynchronous worker/tool states and late completions | **Very likely (80-90%)** correspondence: Sarter/Woods and FAA explicitly address current/future mode and delayed effects. |
| Checklist interruption and uncertain resume point | compaction, user steering, dependency update, or interrupted agent | **Likely (55-80%)** transfer: both create stale progress state; restart cost differs substantially. |
| Confidential near-miss reports | non-scoring worker/owner hazard cards | **Likely (55-80%)** functional transfer for discovery, but human fear of discipline is not the same as model reward bias. |

### Disanalogies that block a stronger claim

1. **Fixed versus elastic crew.** Flightcrew count is generally predetermined by aircraft design, regulation, and operating concept. Agent workers are cheap to instantiate and may be numerous. Aviation therefore does not identify the optimum elastic `N`.

2. **Catastrophic real-time control versus reversible information work.** A missed aviation callout can become irreversible in seconds. Many agent tasks permit rerun, diff, rollback, or delayed integration. Importing cockpit ceremony everywhere would overfit and slow ordinary work.

3. **Stable trained roles versus clone-like workers.** Pilots have certified skills, recurrent training, and differentiated seats. Agent workers may share the same base model and prompt lineage. Nominally “independent” verification may be correlated. The claim that a second agent is an independent checker is **UNVERIFIED; use model/prompt/tool diversity and seeded-fault detection tests to settle it**.

4. **Shared sensory field versus fragmented context.** Pilots see overlapping instruments and hear shared communications. Agents often receive scoped briefs and separate tool outputs. Positive state briefs and an annunciator are therefore more important in the target domain, but also harder to keep correct.

5. **Human reporting incentives versus agent optimization.** ASRS addresses fear of punishment and institutional learning. Agents do not have employment sanctions; they may nevertheless optimize toward appearing successful. The psychological mechanism does not literally transfer. Only channel separation and independent corroboration do.

6. **Safety objective versus multi-objective productivity.** Aviation heavily prioritizes safety margins. A coding harness trades quality, latency, cost, exploration, and user attention. Redundant workers can be valuable for creative search even when throughput per worker falls.

7. **Crew coordination cost is real but not monotone evidence.** Harris states that coordination itself costs workload and doubling crew does not halve workload, yet the article also notes the difficulty of observing errors trapped by a second pilot ([article lines 84-90](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003687015300211)). This supports measuring marginal cost, not assuming “fewer is safer.”

### Bottom-line validity of the analogy

The analogy **does transfer as a safety envelope** and **does not transfer as a sizing formula**. That boundary is **very likely (80-90%)** correct given two mechanically distinct signals: official FAA operating guidance specifies workload-, role-, handoff-, monitoring-, and mode-based controls without an elastic headcount optimizer; the independent academic crewing analysis explicitly identifies coordination workload and rejects the assumption that twice the crew halves workload. This is not a global proof that no aviation subfield has an elastic staffing model. The stronger absence claim is deliberately not made.

## What the envelope catches that a worker-count cap misses

A cap can say “no more than four.” It cannot distinguish four independent read-only investigations from four writers editing the same file, nor four productive workers from four workers whose completions overwhelm one reviewer. The proposed envelope can detect:

- **Coordinator saturation before CPU/token saturation:** review queue and unread completion growth.
- **Role collapse:** the mission owner becomes a local executor and stops tracking the trajectory.
- **False redundancy:** a “monitor” repeats the producer’s method rather than independently checking.
- **Mode surprise:** late, blocked, canceled, or stale-objective workers are treated as current.
- **Unsafe task release:** a worker starts before dependencies, acceptance criteria, or write ownership are clear.
- **Conflict hidden by success:** the final result passes, but duplicated effort or a near miss predicts future failure.
- **Bad interruption recovery:** an agent resumes from a state that is no longer authoritative.
- **Negative marginal scaling:** accepted useful output stagnates while integration and rework rise.

Whether the present owner harness misses each item is **UNVERIFIED; validate by proving the detector finds one known-present orchestration event, then inspect event fields and replay a known multi-worker trace using transcript and filesystem/tool-event data as mechanically independent signals**.

## Evaluation plan

### Phase 0 — detector validation

Before any absence claim, prove each detector against a known-present synthetic trace: overlapping write claims, stale objective version, positive handoff, blocked worker, late completion, and growing review queue. Then confirm the same event through a second independent signal where possible—for example orchestration ledger plus filesystem write log, or state transition plus transcript acknowledgment.

### Phase 1 — shadow-mode replay

Replay a stratified sample of completed multi-worker runs. The gate emits allow/defer/reject without changing execution. Record:

- critical-path accepted outputs;
- time to first useful completion and final accepted result;
- owner review/integration time;
- duplicated task/query/write-set rate;
- conflict and rework count;
- stale or late result count;
- monitor seeded-fault detection rate;
- near-miss report precision against telemetry.

This phase tests observability and false positives, not causal outcome improvement.

### Phase 2 — randomized prospective concurrency trial

Within matched task strata, randomize among current behavior, fixed caps, and adaptive crew-expansion gate. Keep model, tools, prompt family, and acceptance tests fixed. Evaluate outcome quality blind where possible. Stop a treatment early if destructive-conflict or silent-stale-integration rates rise.

Primary decision rule: choose the smallest concurrency policy whose quality is non-inferior and whose latency/cost frontier dominates alternatives. “Non-inferior” margin is **UNVERIFIED; set it before the trial using owner tolerance and baseline variance to settle it**.

### Phase 3 — fault injection

Inject delayed completion, conflicting owner, stale brief, dropped acknowledgment, monitor role drift, dependency invalidation, and misleading self-report. Measure detection rate, latency, unnecessary intervention, and recovery quality. This is the decisive test of the aviation-derived mechanisms, because ordinary successful runs under-sample the relevant hazards.

## Anti-overfit gate

All four proposals ride the existing hook/skill/MCP/event bus and remain small, local mechanisms. They do **not** introduce a second orchestration runtime, vector database or knowledge graph, durable-execution engine, trained critic, observability SaaS, or guardrail framework. The mode panel is a projection of append-only run events, not a new daemon. The near-miss channel is a small structured record, not a memory service.

This matters because the proposal adds only boundary checks and state visibility around whatever native orchestration exists. If the hook bus cannot expose spawn, acknowledgment, state transition, and join events, the BUILD-SMALL classification is **UNVERIFIED; a hook-capability probe is the settling experiment**. In that case, do not silently escalate to BUILD-LARGE; reassess or stop.

## Rejected borrows and dead ends

### Rejected: literal cockpit checklist before every spawn

Too ceremonial. Aviation itself warns that longer checklists take more time and that warning overuse reduces effectiveness (FAA AC 120-71B pp. 14, 21). The transferable element is the three-question release gate and explicit critical-item checks, not a long ritual.

### Rejected: one permanent verifier per worker

This doubles nominal workers without proving independence and creates its own review tree. FAA’s PM role protects the primary trajectory; it is not an unlimited recursive hierarchy. Critical-path/risk-triggered monitoring is the narrower borrow.

### Rejected: “two workers are always safer than one”

The Harris analysis explicitly notes coordination workload, extra error modes, and unknown trapped-error counts. More workers can increase coverage or redundancy, but that benefit is task-dependent and **UNVERIFIED; randomized concurrency trials settle it**.

### Rejected: infer causality directly from near-miss narratives

ASRS itself does not verify or validate reports. Agent narratives must be joined to independent telemetry before becoming rules.

### Rejected: aviation-derived fixed number such as two or three

Cockpit complement reflects aircraft architecture, certification, incapacitation risk, and real-time operations. Those assumptions do not map to elastic software agents. Any numeric default must come from harness evidence, not borrowed prestige.

## GAPS

1. I found no source in this review that validates an arbitrary elastic worker-count rule in aviation. This is a search result, not a claim of global absence. A systematic review of air-traffic-sector staffing, airline disruption control centers, maintenance teams, and distributed UAS crews could reveal closer analogues.
2. The exact subject-harness hook and event capabilities were intentionally not inspected under this ideation-only unit. Therefore every implementation-fit claim is marked UNVERIFIED with a capability probe.
3. The independence of same-model agent monitors is unresolved. Seeded correlated-error experiments are necessary.
4. The optimal workload proxies and thresholds are unresolved. Prospective randomized data are necessary; retrospective correlations alone will confound task difficulty with worker count.
5. ASRS is a reporting system, not a conventional “blameless post-mortem” process. I borrowed its protected reporting and corroboration boundary, not the stronger claim that aviation incident investigation is uniformly blameless.
6. FAA advisory circulars describe accepted guidance and training objectives; they do not by themselves quantify effect size in agent systems. The confidence bands above therefore do not exceed 90% for target-domain outcomes.

## Final recommendation

Build the Crew Expansion Envelope in shadow mode first: pre-spawn workload/opportunity-cost gate, exactly one mission owner, risk-triggered independent monitor, positive handoffs, visible worker modes, and non-scoring near-miss cards. Do **not** ship an aviation-themed fixed worker cap.

This recommendation is **likely (55-80%)** to produce a better orchestration policy than a static headcount limit because it detects the causal precursors aviation actually manages—overload, role ambiguity, lost monitoring, interrupted state, and automation surprise—while leaving the numerical optimum to harness-specific measurement. The remaining uncertainty is material and explicitly testable through shadow replay, randomized concurrency, and fault injection.
