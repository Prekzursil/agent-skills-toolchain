# X133 — Cross-domain borrow: systems safety → finite agent context

**Unit:** X133  
**Source field:** systems safety (STAMP/STPA, Swiss-cheese model, normal-accident theory, safety cases)  
**Target problem:** deciding what belongs in a finite agent context window, what may be compressed or evicted, and how those decisions remain safe over a long horizon  
**Research mode:** external ideation lens only. Per the unit instruction, this analysis does not inspect or make claims about the local harness.

## Executive judgment

The useful borrow is **not** “add more defensive layers.” It is STPA's more precise idea: treat context management as a **feedback-control problem**, identify the unsafe ways that context-management actions can be supplied, omitted, mistimed, misordered, or continued too long, and derive explicit constraints from the resulting loss scenarios. In STPA's own field, accidents need not require a failed component; loss can arise when individually functioning components interact without adequate enforcement of system-level safety constraints. The STPA Handbook makes the controller's internal process model central: a correct control algorithm can still issue an unsafe action when its model of the controlled process, environment, other controllers, operating mode, or human controller is wrong or stale ([Leveson and Thomas, *STPA Handbook*, esp. pp. 18–20, 35–38, 57–63, 180–183](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf)).

That structure maps unusually well to long-horizon context curation. A harness repeatedly performs control actions—admit, place, pin, summarize, refresh, retrieve, and evict—against a changing task state. A locally sensible eviction can become unsafe in a particular mode or sequence: for example, removing an authorization boundary after planning but before execution, preserving an obsolete observation after the external world changes, or compressing two contradictory claims into one unqualified “fact.” The target field independently establishes that larger context is not equivalent to more usable context: long-context performance is position-sensitive, and additional retrieved documents can have diminishing task benefit ([Liu et al., “Lost in the Middle,” TACL 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)); Anthropic describes agent context as a finite resource that must be cyclically refined as the agent loop generates data ([“Effective context engineering for AI agents,” 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)).

**Verdict:** a bounded STPA translation is **very likely (80-90%)** to expose classes of context failure that token thresholds, recency rules, and relevance scores alone do not expose. Evidence: STPA systematically analyzes omission, commission, timing/order, duration, feedback-path defects, and process-model mismatch, while the context literature demonstrates that information utility depends on selection, position, and changing task state—not mere presence. This is a prediction about harness performance, so it is **UNVERIFIED; the settling experiment is the replay-based ablation study in “Evaluation plan,” comparing matched runs with and without the derived constraints.**

STPA does **not** transfer as a completeness or safety guarantee. Open-ended agent work lacks a fully enumerable plant state, stable hazard boundary, reliable sensors, and an inspectable controller state. The transfer is therefore an analysis discipline and runtime checklist, not a claim that all necessary context can be known. That limitation is structural, not an implementation defect.

## 1. The source mechanism in its own field

### 1.1 STAMP: safety as enforced constraints in a control structure

STAMP (System-Theoretic Accident Model and Processes) models safety as a system property maintained by constraints across a hierarchical control structure. A controller sends control actions to a controlled process through an actuator or control path; sensors and other channels return feedback; the controller uses a process model to decide what action is appropriate. The STPA Handbook presents these as the core pieces of the control loop and stresses that software, people, organizations, and their interactions can all participate in inadequate control ([Handbook, pp. 18–25 and 179–183](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf)).

This matters because STAMP rejects a narrow “which component failed?” framing. A component can meet its local specification and the system can still enter a hazardous state because a required constraint was absent, a controller had an inaccurate process model, feedback was delayed, or a normally safe command was given in an unsafe context. That is the precise source-field mechanism being borrowed—not the general slogan “think systemically.”

### 1.2 STPA: derive unsafe control actions with four guideword categories

Basic STPA proceeds roughly as follows:

1. Define the purpose of the analysis: unacceptable losses, system boundary, system-level hazards, and corresponding constraints.
2. Model the relevant control structure: controllers, control actions, controlled process, feedback, and responsibilities.
3. For each control action, identify unsafe control actions (UCAs). The standard guidewords ask whether loss can follow when the action is **not provided**, **provided**, **provided too early/too late or in the wrong order**, or **stopped too soon/applied too long**.
4. Develop causal loss scenarios: why the controller could choose an unsafe action, why a safe action might not be executed, why feedback or the process model could be wrong, and how the controlled process might respond inadequately.
5. Turn UCAs and scenarios into enforceable system constraints and verification work.

The MIT STPA walkthrough displays the four UCA columns explicitly and then crosses them with controller behavior, feedback path, control path, and controlled-process behavior ([MIT PSASS, “STPA Scenarios—New Approach,” 2024](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/wp-content/uploads/2024/STPA-Scenarios-New-Approach.pdf)). Japan's Information-technology Promotion Agency likewise describes applying four guidewords to every control action and provides a workbench organized around accident/hazard/constraint and UCA tables ([IPA, STAMP/STPA overview](https://www.ipa.go.jp/en/digital/complex_systems/stamp.html); [STAMP Workbench procedure](https://www.ipa.go.jp/en/digital/complex_systems/stamp_wb/manual_en/reference/project_view/stpa_view.html)).

The guidewords are important because context systems commonly optimize a scalar—relevance, similarity, age, or token cost—while the guidewords force analysis of **state-contingent temporal behavior**. “Evicting item X is safe” is underspecified; “evicting X after its downstream obligation has been discharged, while its authoritative source remains reachable and its consequence has been recorded” is a testable constraint.

### 1.3 The process model and feedback path are first-class hazards

The most direct bridge to context is STPA's treatment of process-model flaws. The Handbook states that automated control depends not only on the algorithm but also on models of the controlled process, other controllers, automation mode, and sometimes the human controller. It instructs analysts not to stop at “flawed process model,” but to explain why it became flawed—for example, delayed or inaccurate feedback, assumed execution of a command, unconsidered interactions, or incorrect state updates ([Handbook, pp. 180–183](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf)).

In its own field, therefore, STPA does not merely preserve more sensor data. It asks which state variables a controller must know to avoid each UCA, how those variables are updated, and what causal path can make the controller believe the wrong state. This is much closer to the context problem than generic information retrieval: the question is not “which text resembles the query?” but “which state must the decision-maker correctly represent before this control action?”

### 1.4 Swiss cheese: multiple imperfect barriers, with independence caveats

Reason's Swiss-cheese model depicts organizational defenses as layers containing changing weaknesses; an accident trajectory can pass when weaknesses in successive layers align. The system approach focuses on the conditions under which people work and on defenses, rather than assigning the whole explanation to an individual's error ([Reason, “Human error: models and management,” *BMJ* 2000, 320:768–770](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1117770/)).

The transferable part is modest: do not trust a single lossy summary as the only carrier of a critical invariant. Distinct representations can block distinct loss paths. The dangerous non-transfer is to count several LLM-authored summaries or several prompts to the same model as independent barriers. They share a model, source context, and likely failure mode; visually drawing three “slices” does not create independence.

### 1.5 Normal-accident theory: the warning against safety machinery that adds coupling

Normal-accident theory identifies **interactive complexity**—components interacting in unanticipated ways—and **tight coupling**—a failure cannot be isolated and cascades with little slack—as a dangerous combination. The National Academies' treatment of dependable software summarizes those two characteristics and points to modularity, buffering, and redundancy as relevant responses ([National Research Council, *Software for Dependable Systems: Sufficient Evidence?*, 2007, “Coupling and Complexity”](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/11923/chapter/3)). Perrow's original updated book is the source of the theory ([*Normal Accidents: Living with High-Risk Technologies*, Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691004129/normal-accidents)).

For this unit, normal-accident theory functions chiefly as an anti-overengineering test. Every new context index, summarizer, critic, cache, and policy layer creates another transformation and synchronization edge. A context safety mechanism that cannot explain its authoritative state, update order, failure isolation, and recovery path may increase the very interactive complexity it is meant to control.

### 1.6 Safety cases: explicit claims, arguments, evidence, and undeveloped gaps

A safety case makes a structured claim and shows how evidence supports it through an argument, rather than treating a pile of documents as assurance. Goal Structuring Notation (GSN) represents goals/claims, strategies, contexts, assumptions, justifications, solutions/evidence, and references between argument modules. The official GSN Community Standard defines the notation and its interpretation ([SCSC, *Goal Structuring Notation Community Standard Version 3*, 2021](https://scsc.uk/r1386.pdf)). UK defence guidance describes GSN and claim trees as showing how progressively detailed arguments and evidence support a top-level claim such as tolerable safety ([ASEMS, “Goal Structuring Notation and Claim Trees”](https://www.asems.mod.uk/node/165)).

The narrow transferable feature is a **proof obligation for destructive context transformations**: an eviction or lossy summary should carry evidence for why the information is either no longer control-relevant or recoverable before use. The full apparatus of a regulatory safety case would be disproportionate for routine agent turns.

## 2. Structural mapping to context management

The correspondence is:

| Systems-safety element | Context-harness counterpart | Concrete meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Unacceptable loss | Owner-visible task failure | Violate authority; take an irreversible action on stale assumptions; contradict an accepted requirement; falsely declare completion; lose evidence needed to verify work |
| Hazard | Context state that can enable loss | The active decision context lacks, obscures, corrupts, or misstates a control-relevant invariant while the agent remains able to act |
| Controller | Context manager plus model-driven agent | Harness selects/transforms context; model chooses task/tool actions using that representation |
| Controlled process | Task and external work state | Files, tools, services, conversation commitments, plans, tests, external facts, and effects already produced |
| Control actions | Context operations | Admit, exclude, rank/place, pin, unpin, summarize, merge, refresh, retrieve, evict |
| Actuator/control path | Prompt assembly and API boundary | Serialization, truncation, ordering, role assignment, tool-result formatting, provider-side limits |
| Feedback | Observations used to update context state | Tool results, file reads, user corrections, test output, external-state checks, token telemetry, compaction receipts |
| Process model | Explicit task-state capsule plus latent model belief | Current goal, authority, phase, commitments, unresolved questions, evidence/provenance, effect ledger, freshness, and retrieval pointers |
| Safety constraint | Machine-checkable invariant | Conditions under which an item may be transformed or evicted and what must be revalidated before a consequential action |
| Loss scenario | Causal chain | For example: output truncated → completion evidence omitted → summary says “tests pass” without scope → agent declares completion |

The mapping is strongest where the harness controls prompt assembly and has observable task state. It is weakest inside the transformer's latent state: the harness can control the presented representation but cannot establish that the model attended to or interpreted it correctly.

## 3. Proposed design: Context Safety Control Loop

### 3.1 Proposal A — hazard analysis and UCA matrix

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only).** Define a small, versioned context-policy schema and an STPA worksheet for each high-consequence context action. The worksheet is design-time data, not a new orchestrator.

Start with a deliberately small loss vocabulary:

- **L1 — authority loss:** the system acts outside, or forgets a limitation on, owner authorization.
- **L2 — goal/acceptance loss:** the system optimizes an obsolete goal or claims completion without the accepted completion evidence.
- **L3 — state loss:** the system acts on a stale or false representation of external/task state.
- **L4 — commitment loss:** a decision, promise, unresolved blocker, or required follow-up disappears before discharge.
- **L5 — epistemic loss:** provenance, confidence, contradiction, or `UNVERIFIED` status is erased by transformation.
- **L6 — recoverability loss:** the only usable copy or pointer is discarded before the horizon on which it may matter.

Then analyze every context action using the four UCA guidewords. A starter matrix follows.

| Context control action | Not provided when needed | Provided when unsafe | Wrong timing/order | Too short/too long |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Admit/retrieve | Missing updated requirement, correction, or decisive tool evidence | Injects irrelevant, adversarial, unauthorized, or obsolete material | Retrieved after plan/action rather than before it; later evidence placed before earlier claim without chronology | Evidence available for only one turn; raw bulk retained after utility expires |
| Pin | Critical authority or acceptance criterion remains evictable | Low-value or stale content monopolizes scarce context | Pin occurs after destructive compaction; unpin precedes obligation discharge | Pin expires mid-operation; persists across a task/mode boundary |
| Summarize/merge | Raw history grows until useful evidence is obscured | Lossy transform collapses disagreement, negation, scope, or provenance | Summary produced before outcomes arrive; older summary overwrites newer state | Summary refreshed too seldom and becomes stale; repeated resummarization compounds drift |
| Refresh/revalidate | Agent uses stale external or task state | Recheck itself mutates state or imports untrusted data | Revalidation follows rather than precedes consequential action | Freshness interval shorter than cost permits or longer than state volatility permits |
| Evict | Noise remains and dilutes usable attention | Required invariant, open obligation, negative result, or only recovery pointer is removed | Evicted before dependent decision/evidence capture; removal and capsule update are non-atomic | Temporary eviction lasts through the decision that needed the item; stale item is retained indefinitely |
| Reorder/place | Critical item remains buried | Untrusted or superseded item receives privileged placement | Causal sequence or instruction precedence is inverted | Priority boost vanishes before use or permanently suppresses new evidence |

This matrix catches failure modes a scalar relevance score cannot express: **omission versus commission, mode-dependent safety, temporal order, and duration.** It also makes “too much context” a hazard rather than assuming retention is monotonically safe.

**Expected value:** this proposal is **very likely (80-90%)** to reveal missing safety constraints during design reviews because the matrix forces analysts to examine at least four distinct failure relations for every operation. Evidence: those are the standard STPA UCA categories, and context operations genuinely have state- and time-dependent effects. The magnitude of production benefit is **UNVERIFIED; settle it by having blinded reviewers analyze the same historical context failures with the current method and this matrix, then measure unique actionable scenarios, inter-rater agreement, and false-positive burden.**

### 3.2 Proposal B — an explicit context process model (“safety capsule”)

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).** Maintain a compact, typed capsule outside the free-form transcript and inject its rendered form at inference boundaries. It is not durable semantic memory and not a vector database. It is the controller's minimal process model and an index back to authoritative artifacts.

Suggested fields:

```yaml
context_capsule_version: 1
goal:
  objective: "..."
  acceptance_evidence: ["..."]
authority:
  permitted: ["..."]
  prohibited_or_requires_confirmation: ["..."]
task_mode: explore | plan | execute | verify | report
commitments:
  open: [{id: C1, text: "...", source: "turn/tool/artifact pointer"}]
  discharged: [{id: C0, evidence: "pointer"}]
epistemic_state:
  verified: [{claim: "...", evidence: "pointer", observed_at: "..."}]
  unverified: [{claim: "...", settling_check: "..."}]
  contradictions: [{a: "pointer", b: "pointer", status: open}]
effects:
  completed: [{action: "...", evidence: "pointer", reversible: true}]
  pending_or_unknown: ["..."]
freshness:
  - item: "external fact/state"
    observed_at: "..."
    recheck_before: "consequential action class"
recovery_index:
  - invariant: "..."
    authoritative_source: "stable pointer + locator/hash where available"
```

The capsule must preserve **state**, not prose chronology. It distinguishes verified from unverified, open from discharged, permitted from prohibited, observed state from inferred state, and content from its source pointer. Unknown remains a first-class value; it must never be silently coerced to false, done, or irrelevant.

Update constraints:

1. No lossy transformation may remove an open authority constraint, acceptance criterion, unresolved contradiction, irreversible/pending effect, or open commitment from both active context and the capsule.
2. A summary may replace evidence in the active window only if it retains a stable retrieval pointer and the evidence is not required verbatim for the next consequential decision.
3. A state claim with a freshness condition must be revalidated before the named action class; recency is a property of the claim, not merely its token timestamp.
4. Eviction and capsule update form one logical transaction: if the capsule cannot be updated and validated, eviction does not proceed.
5. A user correction updates the superseded claim and the contradiction/decision trail; it must not merely append a newer paragraph that competes with the old one.
6. Compaction must preserve negative results (“X did not work”), scope (“only module Y tested”), and epistemic qualifiers. These are especially vulnerable to fluent summary collapse.

**Expected value:** a typed capsule is **likely (55-80%)** to reduce long-horizon instruction, commitment, and provenance loss while consuming fewer tokens than repeatedly retaining full transcripts. Evidence: STPA identifies inaccurate controller process models and defective feedback updates as causal paths; the proposed fields directly represent the state variables needed for the defined loss classes. It is only “likely,” not “very likely,” because capsule extraction can itself be wrong and the model may ignore the rendered capsule. This premise and recommendation are **UNVERIFIED; settle them with injected-fault trials that delete, contradict, stale, and reposition one field at a time, measuring both capsule-detection recall and downstream action correctness.**

### 3.3 Proposal C — pre-eviction assurance receipt

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).** Before a lossy summary or eviction, emit a compact machine-readable receipt:

```json
{
  "operation": "evict|summarize|merge",
  "targets": ["stable item ids"],
  "hazards_checked": ["L1", "L2", "L3", "L4", "L5", "L6"],
  "open_obligations_carried": ["C1"],
  "claims_changed": [{"id": "K7", "from": "verified", "to": "verified", "evidence": "ptr"}],
  "recoverability": [{"id": "K7", "source": "ptr", "locator_validated": true}],
  "freshness_preserved": true,
  "contradictions_preserved": true,
  "post_transform_token_cost": 0
}
```

This is a deliberately tiny safety-case fragment: the claim is “this destructive context transform preserves the defined invariants,” the argument is the fixed checklist, and the evidence is stable item IDs, validated pointers, and before/after typed fields. A receipt is rejected when a required field is unknown; it is not allowed to generate persuasive prose in place of evidence.

This would catch:

- the only evidence pointer disappearing while the prose conclusion survives;
- `UNVERIFIED` becoming asserted fact;
- an unresolved contradiction being “resolved” by summary omission;
- acceptance evidence being replaced with a bare “done”;
- an open commitment disappearing during phase change;
- eviction occurring before the capsule update is durable;
- a truncated tool result being summarized as a complete observation.

**Expected value:** deterministic before/after validation is **very likely (80-90%)** to catch schema-visible invariant loss caused by a compactor. Evidence: the comparison operates on explicit typed fields rather than requiring the same model to notice its own semantic omission. End-to-end reduction in owner-visible errors is **UNVERIFIED; settle it with a shadow-mode deployment that records would-block decisions, followed by human labeling of true hazards and false blocks before enforcement.**

### 3.4 Proposal D — two genuinely different barriers for high-consequence actions

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).** Use only two barriers, chosen for mechanical diversity:

1. **Semantic barrier:** the model or compactor proposes the capsule delta and classifies the transformation.
2. **Structural barrier:** deterministic code verifies required fields, monotonic obligation IDs, evidence-pointer syntax/existence where locally checkable, freshness rules, and before/after invariants.

Before an irreversible or externally consequential action, a separate revalidation step retrieves the authoritative source named in the capsule rather than trusting a chain of summaries. This is “Swiss cheese” only in the limited sense that different barriers cover different holes. It is not independent assurance if both barriers are prompts to the same model.

**Expected value:** the mechanically diverse pair is **likely (55-80%)** to outperform two independent-looking LLM reviews at detecting capsule corruption. Evidence: a deterministic structural validator has failure modes different from semantic generation, whereas repeated generations share training, prompt, and source-context dependencies. Comparative advantage is **UNVERIFIED; settle it with a factorial experiment: one semantic check, two semantic checks, semantic plus deterministic check, and deterministic check alone, using the same seeded corruption set.**

### 3.5 Proposal E — loosen coupling with checkpoints and reversible eviction

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only).** Separate the active window from the authoritative record. Eviction should normally mean “remove from the next prompt while retaining a stable retrieval pointer,” not “destroy the only record.” Place explicit checkpoints at task-mode boundaries—plan→execute, execute→verify, verify→report—and require capsule reconciliation there. Reserve headroom so an unexpected correction or tool result can be admitted without immediate emergency compaction.

This applies normal-accident theory conservatively: introduce slack, isolate a failed summary from the source record, and reduce synchronous dependencies. It does **not** add a durable-execution engine, vector database, knowledge graph, second orchestration runtime, trained critic, observability SaaS, or guardrail framework.

**Expected value:** reversible eviction and boundary checkpoints are **likely (55-80%)** to make context failures easier to recover from because a bad summary no longer destroys the rehydration path and reconciliation occurs before mode-specific actions. Evidence: normal-accident analysis identifies tight coupling and lack of isolation as cascade enablers, and the context mapping has a natural reversible/irreversible boundary. The size of the effect is **UNVERIFIED; settle it by replaying long tasks with injected summary corruption and measuring recovery rate, tokens spent, and delay to detection.**

## 4. What this catches that common context heuristics miss

The following are mechanism-level deltas, not claims about the local harness:

1. **Unsafe omission:** relevance ranking asks what is similar to the current query; STPA asks whether failing to provide a specific state variable can enable a loss. Authorization and negative constraints can be lexically dissimilar to the immediate subtask yet remain control-critical.
2. **Unsafe provision:** “keep more” can preserve stale plans, superseded requirements, prompt injection, or obsolete external state. STPA treats providing an action in the wrong state as hazardous, not just failing to provide it.
3. **Wrong order:** evidence obtained after a decision cannot retroactively make that decision controlled. The UCA matrix identifies retrieve-after-act, summarize-before-result, and evict-before-capture sequences.
4. **Wrong duration:** some context is temporarily essential (a migration constraint during the migration); other context becomes actively hazardous when retained across a mode or task boundary. Pin/unpin therefore needs lifecycle conditions, not permanent importance labels.
5. **Process-model drift:** the capsule makes mode, commitments, effects, freshness, and contradictions explicit. A high-quality summary can still be unsafe if it accurately summarizes the transcript but fails to represent current controlled-process state.
6. **Feedback-path defects:** truncation, delayed tool results, serialization loss, and assumed action success become distinct scenarios. The harness must distinguish “command issued” from “effect observed.”
7. **Epistemic collapse:** a summary often optimizes readability. The safety constraints instead require provenance, qualification, scope, and contradictory evidence to survive.
8. **False defense diversity:** several LLM passes are not automatically several independent barriers. The design labels where independence actually comes from deterministic validation or authoritative reread.
9. **Context as an action, not a bag:** placement, merging, refresh, and eviction are analyzed as interventions whose consequences depend on task state. That exposes hazards that a static “important/not important” classifier cannot represent.

## 5. Adversarial transfer analysis

### 5.1 Where the analogy genuinely holds

- **Finite controller knowledge:** both a safety controller and an agent choose actions using an incomplete internal representation of a changing process.
- **Feedback can be delayed, absent, or misleading:** tool output and external observations play the same structural role as feedback, even though their semantics differ.
- **Actions are context-dependent:** retaining, evicting, or refreshing an item can be safe in one task mode and unsafe in another, just as a physical control action's safety depends on process state.
- **Component correctness is insufficient:** a retriever, summarizer, model, and tool adapter can each behave as designed while their interaction creates a harmful context state.
- **Timing and duration matter:** the same information can be necessary before execution, noise after verification, and stale on a later session.

These are structural correspondences, not metaphors, because each side contains a controller, a changing target process, state-dependent actions, feedback, an internal model, and system-level losses.

### 5.2 Where the analogy breaks

1. **No complete plant model.** Engineered safety analysis normally chooses a system boundary and hazards. An open-ended agent may encounter new goals, tools, social meanings, and owner preferences mid-run. The context manager cannot enumerate all control-relevant variables in advance.
2. **The controller is partly opaque.** STPA can describe an automated controller's explicit process model. A transformer has a latent, distributed representation; injecting a capsule does not prove use or correct interpretation.
3. **“Sensor truth” is weaker.** Tool results can be truncated, adversarial, stale, ambiguous, or scoped differently from the inferred claim. Natural-language feedback lacks the well-defined measurement semantics common in engineered control loops.
4. **Loss severity and frequency differ.** Many context errors are recoverable quality failures, not physical accidents. Safety terminology can encourage disproportionate ceremony and false confidence.
5. **Eviction may be reversible.** If the authoritative transcript/artifact remains addressable, prompt eviction is not physical destruction. The principal risk is failure to retrieve in time, not necessarily permanent information loss.
6. **Control actions are not cleanly discrete.** Context items interact through attention, ordering, wording, and model priors. Two individually safe snippets can combine into ambiguity; marginal token effects are not fully observable.
7. **Analysis itself consumes context and maintenance effort.** A detailed hazard ledger can become the clutter it was intended to control. This is exactly where normal-accident theory warns that added safety components can increase interaction complexity.
8. **Safety constraints may conflict with performance.** Preserving all provenance, alternatives, and commitments can exhaust the active budget, reducing the model's ability to use any of them.

### 5.3 Result of the adversarial test

The analogy **does transfer**, but only at the level of:

- loss-first analysis;
- state-conditioned context actions;
- the four UCA guidewords;
- explicit process-model variables and feedback paths;
- small assurance obligations for lossy transformations;
- deliberate slack, isolation, and recoverability.

It **does not transfer** as:

- a proof that context is complete;
- an assumption that a written capsule equals the model's belief;
- a justification for regulatory-scale safety-case bureaucracy;
- a reason to stack multiple correlated model reviewers;
- a reason to build a second context orchestration runtime.

The bounded transfer is **very likely (80-90%)** to remain conceptually valid across model generations because it analyzes the harness's selection and transformation actions, not a specific model's token limit. Evidence: even when windows grow, selection, positioning, feedback freshness, and state representation remain separate from raw capacity; Liu et al. demonstrate that nominal long-context capability does not guarantee robust use. Whether future models make the runtime mechanism unnecessary is **UNVERIFIED; settle it by repeating the position, stale-state, contradiction, and compaction-invariant benchmarks for each target model release.**

## 6. Anti-overfit gate

The proposed architecture passes the mandated gate:

- It rides existing context assembly and hook/skill/MCP boundaries.
- The runtime addition is a small typed capsule, deterministic validator, and receipts; no second agent loop is introduced.
- It does not propose a vector database or knowledge graph. Stable pointers target the already-authoritative record.
- It does not propose durable execution. A checkpoint is a context-reconciliation boundary, not a workflow engine.
- It does not propose a trained critic. Semantic extraction can use the existing model; assurance comes from typed invariants and authoritative reread.
- It does not propose an observability SaaS or guardrail framework.
- It keeps the loss vocabulary deliberately small and makes eviction reversible by default.

The radical alternative—an independent “context safety daemon” maintaining a full world model—fails this gate. It would add BUILD-LARGE synchronization, authority, recovery, and split-brain problems before demonstrating that the small control-loop mechanism is insufficient. Normal-accident theory makes that alternative especially suspect because it increases interactive complexity and coupling. Therefore it is rejected, not deferred as the preferred end state.

## 7. Evaluation plan

All performance claims above remain UNVERIFIED until the corresponding experiment is run. A decisive evaluation should use faults that are about control state, not only needle retrieval.

### Phase 1 — static discriminative test

Create 100–300 compact scenarios balanced across the four UCA categories and six loss classes. Include safe controls so the system cannot win by blocking every eviction. Each scenario provides an active context, capsule, candidate transform, authoritative pointers, task mode, and expected allow/block/refresh decision.

Metrics:

- hazard recall by UCA category;
- safe-transform acceptance rate;
- false blocks per 100 transformations;
- qualifier/provenance preservation;
- contradiction preservation;
- recovery-pointer validity;
- tokens and latency per decision.

Detector control: seed known-present violations for every invariant type and prove each detector finds them before interpreting a zero count. Use two mechanically independent signals for any “none missed” claim: deterministic oracle comparison plus downstream behavioral probe.

### Phase 2 — fault-injected trajectory replay

At realistic compaction points, inject one fault at a time and in combinations:

- delete an authorization constraint;
- invert a negation;
- drop `UNVERIFIED` or confidence;
- merge contradictory observations;
- retain a superseded plan;
- mark “command issued” as “effect observed”;
- truncate a tool result before the decisive line;
- invalidate a retrieval pointer;
- stale an external-state observation;
- move critical evidence to middle positions;
- evict an open commitment one step before discharge;
- resummarize a summary repeatedly to induce drift.

Compare:

1. current/default context behavior;
2. token-budget and recency heuristic only;
3. typed capsule without receipts;
4. capsule plus deterministic receipt validation;
5. capsule plus validation plus authoritative reread before consequential action.

Primary outcome: correct next action or correct request to revalidate. Secondary outcomes: owner-visible task success, token cost, latency, unnecessary retrievals, false refusals/blocks, and time to recover after corruption.

### Phase 3 — long-horizon end-to-end tasks

Use tasks with genuine phase changes, corrections, conflicting evidence, external-state freshness, and a later need for an early negative constraint. Randomize model and condition. Blind evaluators to the context policy. Report confidence intervals, not only average success.

The proposal earns adoption only if capsule plus validation improves consequential-action correctness without a disproportionate rise in false blocks or token overhead. A reasonable initial gate is **UNVERIFIED and deliberately provisional; settle the threshold before examining results**: at least a 30% relative reduction in seeded hazardous transforms, at least 95% preservation of safe transforms, and less than 5% median context-token overhead. These numbers are engineering acceptance criteria, not evidence-backed predictions.

### Phase 4 — shadow mode before enforcement

Run receipts without blocking. Sample would-block and would-allow events, label them, then enforce only invariant classes with acceptable precision. Keep a one-step bypass that records the reason, because unknown task modes are expected rather than exceptional.

Shadow mode is **very likely (80-90%)** to reveal policy overreach before it disrupts work. Evidence: the policy makes stateful judgments in an open domain where the loss boundary is incomplete; non-enforcing observation directly measures false blocks. This operational benefit is still **UNVERIFIED; settle it by comparing labeled shadow decisions with subsequent outcomes and reviewer judgment.**

## 8. Failure policy and non-goals

When validation cannot establish safe eviction, the harness should choose among three bounded responses: retain temporarily, replace with a recoverable pointer plus capsule invariant, or ask/revalidate before a consequential action. It should not claim that uncertainty has been eliminated.

Non-goals:

- reconstructing the full conversation in a structured database;
- scoring universal semantic importance;
- proving the model read the context;
- preventing every hallucination;
- retaining every fact indefinitely;
- treating every tool call as safety-critical;
- generating a regulatory-style assurance case per turn.

The central design rule is: **preserve control-relevant state and its recovery path, not the transcript as such.** Raw history, prose summary, and retrieval similarity are inputs to that judgment; none is the process model by itself.

## 9. Alternatives considered and rejected

### “Just enlarge the context window”

Rejected as a complete answer. Nominal capacity does not ensure robust use: Liu et al. found strong position effects and diminishing gains from additional retrieved documents ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)). Larger windows may reduce forced eviction frequency, but they do not resolve stale state, conflicting authority, provenance collapse, wrong ordering, or assumed action success.

### “Retain everything and let attention sort it out”

Rejected. It treats provision as monotonic benefit, while both STPA and context evidence show that unsafe provision and dilution are real categories. It also supplies no lifecycle for obsolete or adversarial state.

### “Use a relevance model for eviction”

Rejected as the sole controller. Relevance is query-relative and typically atemporal; it does not encode authorization, open obligations, irreversible effects, evidence status, freshness, or safe sequencing. It can remain a candidate generator beneath the safety constraints.

### “Add three LLM critics/summarizers for defense in depth”

Rejected. Correlated model calls do not establish Swiss-cheese independence, and the added transformations create normal-accident-style interaction and synchronization paths. One semantic proposer plus one deterministic invariant validator is smaller and mechanically more diverse.

### “Build a comprehensive world-model service”

Rejected by the anti-overfit gate. It is a BUILD-LARGE second stateful runtime whose correctness and synchronization would become a new context problem. The small capsule intentionally represents only variables tied to explicit loss classes.

### “Use full GSN safety cases for every compaction”

Rejected as disproportionate. The transferable piece is the explicit claim–evidence obligation. A fixed receipt captures it without importing the ceremony and maintenance burden of a regulated safety case.
