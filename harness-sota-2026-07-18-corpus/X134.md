# X134 — Cross-domain borrow: systems safety → orchestration concurrency

Date: 2026-07-19  
Lens: systems-safety (STAMP/STPA, unsafe collaborative control, Swiss cheese, normal accidents, safety cases)  
Target: deciding how many parallel agent workers to run and when additional workers stop helping

## Executive judgment

The strongest transfer is not “use more layers of review,” and it is not a formula for the optimal worker count. It is **STPA's context-dependent unsafe-control-action analysis, extended to combinations of actions by Unsafe Collaborative Control Actions (UCCAs)**. In its native field, the analyst defines unacceptable losses and hazardous system states, models a controller acting on a controlled process through a feedback loop, systematically asks how each control action can be unsafe by omission, commission, timing/order, or duration, and then inverts those unsafe actions into enforceable controller constraints. The newer collaborative extension enumerates combinations of commands from multiple controllers, then refines and prunes them to control combinatorial growth. The primary sources are the [MIT STPA Handbook, especially pp. 22 and 35–46](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf) and Kopeikin, Leveson, and Neogi's [NASA-hosted 2024 paper on unsafe collaborative control](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230017753).

This mechanism **does transfer, but only as a negative safety envelope around orchestration**. It identifies when spawning, withholding, continuing, cancelling, or joining workers could create an unacceptable state. It does not identify the utility-maximizing number of workers. That limitation is not semantic hair-splitting: the STPA Handbook says the method identifies behavior to prevent under worst-case context rather than deciding best-case, average-case, or most-likely outcomes (pp. 35–36), while the UCCA paper describes its output as safety requirements, not an optimum. Those are two independent source signals against treating STPA as an optimizer.

It is **very likely (80-90%)**, based on the exact match between STPA's four control-action categories and the harness actions `spawn / do-not-spawn / delay / continue / cancel`, plus the UCCA paper's direct treatment of interacting multi-controller commands, that this borrow can expose orchestration hazards that a fixed `max_workers` ceiling cannot represent. Whether it improves this owner's harness is **UNVERIFIED — the “Concurrency-envelope replay experiment” in §9 would settle it**. No local harness claim is made: the unit instruction explicitly makes this an external ideation lens and says not to ground it in the local repository.

## 1. What systems safety actually solved

### 1.1 STAMP's control-loop model

STAMP treats losses as failures to enforce constraints on a dynamic system rather than only as chains of failed components. The practical model contains a controller, its control algorithm and internal process model, control actions, the controlled process, and feedback. The [MIT handbook](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf) states that a controller's process model contains beliefs about the controlled process and environment, is updated by feedback, and can diverge from reality because feedback is incorrect, delayed, misinterpreted, ignored, or absent (pp. 22, 44–46). It also explicitly accommodates overlapping and interacting control loops (pp. 22–24).

That matters more than the “safety” label. The solved problem is how to reason systematically about a decision that is locally sensible but unsafe in the actual system context because the controller has stale state, incomplete feedback, a flawed decision rule, another controller is acting simultaneously, or the command is not executed as intended.

### 1.2 The four unsafe-control-action prompts

For each control action and actual context, STPA asks four exhaustive prompts at the level of a single control effort:

1. Not providing the action leads to a hazard.
2. Providing the action leads to a hazard.
3. A potentially safe action is provided too early, too late, or out of order.
4. A continuous action lasts too long or stops too soon.

These categories and examples appear in the [STPA Handbook, pp. 35–37](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf). The handbook requires each UCA to name the source/controller, action type, control action, **actual context**, and linked hazard (pp. 35–36). It then inverts each UCA into a controller constraint (p. 40) and develops loss scenarios that ask both why the unsafe action would occur and why a safe command might be improperly executed or not executed (pp. 41–46).

The context requirement prevents a shallow rule such as “parallelism is risky” or “eight workers is safe.” The same spawn can be useful when eight tasks are independent and harmful when eight workers share one write target, one unavailable dependency, or one untested premise.

### 1.3 The multi-controller extension is the most direct source mechanism

Base STPA considers simultaneous or conflicting actions as context, but parallel-agent orchestration is specifically a combination problem. Kopeikin, Leveson, and Neogi's [System-Theoretic Analysis of Unsafe Collaborative Control in Teaming Systems](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230017753) extends STPA to UCCAs: combinations of commands that are safe separately but unsafe together. The method uses abstraction to enumerate, refine, prune, and prioritize combinations rather than naïvely expanding every Cartesian product. The NASA record says the method found causal factors not found by an earlier hazard analysis of the same manned/unmanned-aircraft case.

That result is promising but limited. The NASA metadata describes the publication as a conference paper with “Technical Review: Single Expert,” and the abstract reports one aerospace case study. Therefore transfer efficacy to language-model workers is **likely (55-80%)**, not near-certain: evidence consists of a strong structural match and one relevant multi-agent demonstration, but not agent-harness trials. **UNVERIFIED — the “Concurrency-envelope replay experiment” and “combination fault-injection experiment” in §9 would settle the harness-specific premise.**

## 2. Concrete mapping to agent orchestration

| Systems-safety element | Agent-harness translation | Observable representation |
|---|---|---|
| Loss | Owner-visible damage | wrong/incomplete accepted result; destructive conflict; excess cost; missed deadline; inability to synthesize evidence |
| Hazard | System state which, with a worst-case environment, can produce a loss | workers overwrite shared state; all workers depend on the same failed service; no worker owns an essential subproblem; evidence volume exceeds synthesis capacity |
| Controller | Root agent/orchestrator | component authorized to spawn, assign, continue, cancel, join, and synthesize |
| Process model | Orchestrator's beliefs | live worker count/status, task-dependency graph, write ownership, tool health, remaining budget/time, evidence coverage, feedback age |
| Controlled process | Active work system | workers, tools, files, remote services, user steering, result queue |
| Control actions | Decisions under analysis | `spawn(k, assignment)`, `continue(worker)`, `cancel(worker)`, `join/synthesize` |
| Feedback | Evidence that updates the process model | start/heartbeat/finish events, tool outcomes, write intents, findings/artifacts, budget use, duplicate-work indicators |
| Constraint | Enforceable condition on actions | “do not add a worker when it has no disjoint owned deliverable” rather than “usually keep teams small” |
| UCCA | Unsafe combination | two workers each safely edit a file alone but concurrently target overlapping lines; several workers independently choose the same dead dependency |

This mapping is **almost certain (90-99%)** to be structurally valid, because every mapped controller, command, process state, and feedback path exists by definition in an orchestrated worker system, and the handbook explicitly applies its generic loop to both software and human controllers (pp. 22–24). Structural validity does not imply that any proposed metric accurately measures answer quality.

## 3. A mini-STPA for worker-count control

### 3.1 Losses and hazards

The following are design definitions, not claims about observed local failures:

- **L1 — integrity loss:** a conflicting or unsupported result reaches the owner.
- **L2 — coverage loss:** an essential subproblem is not completed before join/finalization.
- **L3 — state loss:** simultaneous work corrupts or unintentionally overwrites a shared artifact.
- **L4 — resource loss:** additional workers consume material budget or wall time without enough unique progress to justify their coordination burden.

Derived hazardous states:

- **H1, correlated redundancy:** nominally independent workers share the same premise, source, service, detector, or error mode, so “agreement” is not independent evidence.
- **H2, ownership collision:** workers have overlapping mutation authority or incompatible assumptions about shared state.
- **H3, synthesis saturation:** unresolved outputs arrive faster or in greater volume than the root can compare, reconcile, and verify.
- **H4, coverage starvation:** parallel capacity exists, but an independent critical subproblem has no owner.
- **H5, stale control:** the root's worker-status, dependency-health, budget, or ownership model is older than the decision it controls.
- **H6, premature convergence:** workers are stopped or joined while a unique, necessary branch is unresolved.
- **H7, runaway continuation:** workers continue after their marginal unique yield is persistently below their resource and synthesis cost.

### 3.2 Unsafe control actions

| STPA category | Harness unsafe control action | Hazard(s) |
|---|---|---|
| Not provided | Root does not spawn a worker while a critical, independent, parallelizable branch is unowned and enough budget/time remains. | H4 → L2 |
| Provided | Root spawns another worker when no disjoint assignment or write boundary exists. | H2/H3 → L1/L3/L4 |
| Provided | Root spawns several “independent verifiers” that share the same detector, upstream source, or service. | H1 → L1 |
| Too early | Root fans out before interfaces, ownership, required evidence, and join criteria are defined. | H2/H3 → L1/L3/L4 |
| Too late | Root waits until the serial path consumes the deadline before assigning independent research or verification. | H4 → L2 |
| Out of order | Root starts implementers before a blocking decision or shared contract is resolved. | H2/H5 → L1/L3/L4 |
| Stopped too soon | Root cancels a worker solely because peers finished, even though it owns the only path to a required claim. | H6 → L2 |
| Applied too long | Root continues a worker after repeated windows show no new owned evidence/artifact and high overlap or a terminal dependency failure. | H7 → L4 |
| Combination/UCCA | Each worker's assignment is safe alone, but simultaneous execution exceeds a shared API quota, hits the same file, consumes the only synthesis channel, or amplifies the same false premise. | H1/H2/H3 → L1/L3/L4 |

The table adds something a scalar cap cannot: the unsafe state depends on **assignment topology and shared dependencies**, not merely `active_workers > N`.

## 4. Proposal — Contextual Concurrency Safety Envelope — UNVERIFIED

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).**

**UNVERIFIED premise — current lifecycle events expose enough start/status/tool-result/write-intent/completion data to evaluate these constraints; the “event-surface capability probe” in §9 would settle it.** If the bus lacks a required observation, the safe response is to omit that runtime constraint or add the smallest native event field, not create a second orchestration runtime.

Build one pure policy function around existing orchestration actions:

```text
decision = admit(action, process_model, constraints)
decision ∈ {allow, delay, deny, require-root-review}
```

The process model is ephemeral per task:

```text
active_workers; pending_independent_work; assignment_owner;
read_set/write_set or declared scope; shared_dependencies;
last_progress_at; feedback_age; budget_remaining;
new_evidence_or_artifacts; duplicate_queries/errors/findings;
unresolved_join_conflicts; root_synthesis_backlog
```

The initial constraint set should be small and mechanically testable:

1. **Ownership constraint:** admit a spawn only with a named deliverable and an ownership boundary. Deny concurrent overlapping writes unless the root explicitly serializes the join.
2. **Diversity constraint:** a request for independent verification must differ on at least one mechanically visible axis—source, detector, tool path, or measurement—not just prompt wording.
3. **Feedback-freshness constraint:** delay spawn/cancel decisions when worker state or dependency health is stale beyond a declared task-local window.
4. **Coverage constraint:** do not finalize while a declared critical branch is unowned or unresolved.
5. **Synthesis-capacity constraint:** delay further fan-out when unresolved worker outputs exceed a small configured backlog, until the root consumes or delegates a disjoint join.
6. **Marginal-yield constraint:** stop adding workers after two observation windows in which an added worker produces no new owned artifact/evidence **and** exhibits high overlap, or when coordination/synthesis cost exceeds its unique yield. The two-window rule, overlap definition, and thresholds are **UNVERIFIED — the replay experiment in §9 must calibrate them on a training split and test them unchanged on a held-out split.**
7. **Terminal-dependency constraint:** do not fan out retries across workers when a shared dependency is in a confirmed terminal state. “Confirmed” requires two mechanically independent signals, such as a typed tool error plus a health/connection-state field; repeated identical calls are one signal, not two.
8. **Duration constraint:** do not cancel the sole owner of a critical branch merely because a global time threshold elapsed; require branch resolution, explicit degradation, or root review.

The envelope is a **guard**, not the chooser of an exact count. The root/model still proposes work decomposition and count; the envelope rejects contextually hazardous actions and records why. It is **very likely (80-90%)**, based on the small state vector and direct UCA-to-constraint inversion described in the handbook (p. 40), that a first useful version fits the BUILD-SMALL bound. **UNVERIFIED — a line-counted spike against the actual event API would settle that implementation-size estimate.**

## 5. What this would catch that a count cap misses

These are predicted detections, not claims about the present harness:

1. **False independence.** Three workers agree because all queried the same stale source or exercised the same broken detector. Worker count says “three votes”; dependency lineage says “one correlated signal.” Detection is **very likely (80-90%)** when tool/source identifiers are available, because shared lineage is mechanically visible. Semantic shared assumptions without visible lineage remain hard.
2. **Fan-out into a dead shared dependency.** A global cap allows every worker to fail independently against the same unavailable service. A terminal-dependency constraint blocks the combination only after two independent health signals confirm it. Detection is **likely (55-80%)**, because typed error and health state may exist but the event-surface premise is UNVERIFIED by the named capability probe in §9.
3. **Write collisions below the cap.** Two workers can be unsafe even when the cap is ten. Declared ownership/write intent catches overlapping scope before mutations. Detection is **very likely (80-90%)** for declared file targets, based on exact path overlap; dynamic or undeclared targets require runtime write-intent feedback.
4. **Synthesis overload.** Workers individually make progress, yet the root accumulates more unresolved outputs than it can verify. The backlog constraint delays new spawns until join debt falls. Whether backlog is a reliable proxy for synthesis capacity is **UNVERIFIED — the replay experiment must compare backlog against missed conflicts and owner corrections.**
5. **Premature cancellation of the only useful dissenter.** Duration analysis asks not only whether a worker is slow but whether it owns unique required coverage. Detection is **likely (55-80%)**, based on explicit ownership; it fails if branch criticality was never declared.
6. **Under-parallelization.** Safety analysis includes omission: refusing to spawn can be hazardous when critical independent work is unowned. This corrects the common bias of treating only “too many workers” as risky. Whether automatic reminders improve throughput without noisy fan-out is **UNVERIFIED — the replay experiment would settle it.**
7. **Stale-controller decisions.** A worker already failed, changed scope, or finished, but the root acts on an old status snapshot. The feedback-age constraint surfaces the model/reality mismatch described by STPA. Detection is **almost certain (90-99%)** for timestamped state transitions, because age is mechanically decidable; whether the selected freshness window is useful remains UNVERIFIED by the delayed-feedback injection in §9.

Whether the current harness misses any of these classes is **UNVERIFIED — a log-only deployment followed by two-signal adjudication in §9 would settle each class separately**. It would violate this unit's no-local-grounding instruction to state otherwise.

## 6. Adversarial transfer test

### 6.1 Why the analogy holds

- **Same control topology:** orchestration is literally a controller issuing actions to workers and updating beliefs through feedback. This is not a metaphorical resemblance.
- **Context changes action safety:** a fourth worker can be useful on a disjoint research branch and harmful on a single shared file. STPA requires context rather than globally labelling the action safe/unsafe.
- **Interaction creates hazards:** parallel workers share files, quotas, dependencies, budget, root attention, and assumptions. The UCCA extension was created precisely because commands can be safe separately and unsafe together.
- **Omission and duration matter:** orchestration fails not only by spawning too many, but by spawning too few, spawning late, cancelling early, and continuing stale work. The four UCA prompts systematically retain those cases.
- **Model drift is central:** the root necessarily has an internal belief about worker status and task coverage, and asynchronous feedback can make that belief stale.

These correspondences make transfer validity **very likely (80-90%)**, supported independently by the base handbook's generic software/human control loop and the NASA paper's multiple-controller extension. The remaining uncertainty is empirical usefulness in a language-agent harness, not structural fit.

### 6.2 Where it breaks

1. **STPA is not marginal-utility optimization.** It can say a count-changing action is outside a safety envelope; it cannot say whether three workers beats four on expected quality per token. Any claim that it “solves optimal worker count” would overstate the source.
2. **Agent quality is only partly observable.** Aircraft state variables can often be sensed directly; novelty, correctness, and synthesis difficulty are delayed and contestable. File targets and tool lineage are strong signals; “unique insight” is a noisy proxy.
3. **The controlled process is nonstationary.** Model capability, prompt sensitivity, tool availability, and task mix change. A fixed learned threshold can decay. This is **very likely (80-90%)**, based on the harness being composed of changing software/services and task-dependent language models; the useful recalibration interval is UNVERIFIED by longitudinal replay.
4. **Worst-case constraints can suppress valuable exploration.** A dissenter that looks redundant early may later discover the decisive flaw. Therefore low-yield signals may delay new fan-out but must not automatically kill the sole owner of a critical branch.
5. **Combination analysis can explode.** With `n` workers and many actions, naïve subsets are exponential. The UCCA paper's abstraction/pruning is relevant, but a harness should begin with pairwise shared-resource and shared-dependency checks, not full formal enumeration. It is **likely (55-80%)**, from the paper's own need for abstraction and automation, that exhaustive combination analysis would cost more than it returns in ordinary tasks. **UNVERIFIED — the combination fault-injection experiment would measure pairwise recall before any higher-order expansion.**
6. **The safety stakes differ.** Most agent tasks trade quality, cost, and delay rather than catastrophic physical harm. “Hazard” must be operationally defined; importing aviation ceremony would be category error.

The disanalogies limit the recommendation to a thin negative filter with measurable state. They rule out a formal safety bureaucracy or an STPA-branded adaptive optimizer.

## 7. Roles of the other systems-safety ideas

### Swiss cheese: useful warning, poor controller

Reason's Swiss-cheese model says accidents can pass through when weaknesses in multiple defense layers align; the UK Maritime and Coastguard Agency's [official explanation](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/leading-for-safety/2-decision-making-and-risk-management#swiss-cheese-model) distinguishes active failures and latent conditions and warns against blaming only the last individual. This usefully motivates tracing correlated dependency across workers: multiple agents are not multiple defenses when their “holes” align through the same source, tool, or premise.

It does **not** decide worker count, supply a control algorithm, or prove independence. Using “add reviewer layers” as the orchestration policy would be a forced analogy. The model remains an explanatory aid behind the diversity constraint, not the main borrow.

### Normal accidents: architectural veto, not scheduler

Perrow's normal-accident theory connects dangerous cascades to interactive complexity plus tight coupling. The [CDC/NIOSH-hosted technical report summarizing and operationalizing the theory](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/8538/cdc_8538_DS1.pdf) describes unexpected nonlinear interactions and prompt cascading in tightly coupled systems. Perrow's paper [Organizing to Reduce the Vulnerabilities of Complexity](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-5973.00108) recommends decomposed units connected by monitored links and warns that add-on safety measures can themselves become accident sources.

That is a strong anti-overfit gate: keep worker scopes decomposable, links explicit, and the guard tiny. Normal-accident theory does not yield an optimal `k`; it warns that a sophisticated “safety” orchestrator may add the interactions it is meant to control.

### Safety cases: retain the evidence link, discard the ceremony

Safety cases make a claim conditional on context and supported by argument and evidence. The useful fragment is the decision trace in §4: why this fan-out was allowed under this observed context. A full GSN toolchain, approval hierarchy, or durable evidence service would be disproportionate and is not proposed.

## 8. Anti-overfit gate

The proposal passes the mandated gate as designed:

- It rides the existing orchestration/event/hook bus.
- The policy is a pure function plus task-local ephemeral state.
- The trace is JSON/config, not a service.
- It adds no second orchestration runtime, vector database, knowledge graph, durable-execution engine, trained critic, observability SaaS, or guardrail framework.
- It does not use an LLM judge in the admission path; first-version signals are declared ownership, path/dependency overlap, status freshness, typed outcomes, budget, and queue depth.
- It can run log-only and fail open while being calibrated.

If implementing the proposal requires a daemon, cross-task memory store, semantic critic, or general workflow engine, the proposal has failed its own design constraint and should be rejected rather than promoted to BUILD-LARGE.

## 9. Experiments required before adoption

### 9.1 Event-surface capability probe

Purpose: settle the UNVERIFIED premise that the existing bus exposes enough information for BUILD-SMALL.

1. Inject one known-present event of each needed class: worker start, status/heartbeat, tool success, typed tool failure, declared write target, completion, and cancellation.
2. Prove the detector finds each positive control.
3. Compare event fields with an independent artifact/process signal: active-worker registry for lifecycle; filesystem change record for write; tool response for success/failure.
4. Report fields as absent only when the validated detector sees none **and** the independent signal confirms absence.

Exit criterion: ownership, lifecycle, feedback age, tool/dependency identity, budget, and completion are observable without a new runtime. Otherwise reduce the constraint set to what is observable.

### 9.2 Concurrency-envelope replay experiment

Purpose: settle whether the constraints improve orchestration and calibrate only the uncertain thresholds.

1. Select a stratified task set containing research, code modification, verification, shared-file work, independent partitions, and single-threaded dependencies.
2. Replay or rerun with randomized policies: fixed cap; model-selected count without envelope; model-selected count with the envelope in log-only mode; then enforced mode only after review.
3. Pre-register outcomes: accepted unique findings/artifacts, unresolved contradictions at finalization, owner corrections, overlapping writes, repeated terminal errors, token/tool cost, wall time, root join backlog, and critical-branch omissions.
4. Fit overlap/window thresholds on one split and freeze them for a held-out split. Reject thresholds that merely memorize task types.
5. Count a “caught hazard” only with two mechanically independent signals: (a) policy/event evidence that a named constraint was violated and (b) artifact diff, independent tool result, or blinded human adjudication showing the predicted hazard or prevented loss. A second replay of the same event detector is not independent.
6. Record false blocks, especially cases where a delayed or denied worker later proves uniquely valuable.

Adoption criterion: materially fewer integrity/coverage/state/resource losses without a material regression in accepted unique output or wall time. “Material” thresholds must be pre-registered by the owner; choosing them after seeing results would make the conclusion unfalsifiable.

### 9.3 Combination fault-injection experiment

Purpose: test UCCA-specific value beyond single-worker rules.

Inject at least these paired conditions:

- two safe-alone write assignments whose scopes overlap;
- two “independent” verifiers sharing one source/detector;
- multiple workers sharing one unavailable or quota-limited service;
- delayed/out-of-order worker completion feedback;
- a slow sole owner of a critical branch while peers finish;
- several individually productive workers overflowing the root's join backlog.

For every injection, validate the hazard detector on a known-positive control and use a second signal at the outcome boundary. Compare pairwise checks with higher-order checks. Expand beyond pairwise combinations only if a held-out fault requires it often enough to repay the added complexity.

### 9.4 Stop/reject conditions

Reject the transfer if any of the following occurs in held-out evaluation:

- the required state cannot be observed without a new runtime;
- false blocks erase the quality gain from avoided hazards;
- pairwise rules add no detection beyond a fixed cap and ownership locking;
- thresholds require per-task manual tuning;
- the root spends more effort maintaining safety metadata than synthesizing worker results;
- a simpler rule—“one worker per disjoint deliverable, exclusive writer, explicit join”—matches the envelope.

It is **roughly even (45-55%)** that calibrated marginal-yield thresholds add durable value beyond the simpler ownership/diversity/freshness constraints. Evidence is balanced: diminishing returns are plausible and measurable, but output novelty and quality are noisy and task-specific. **UNVERIFIED — the held-out replay is the settling experiment.** This uncertainty is why marginal-yield logic is last in the build order, not the centerpiece.

## 11. Alternatives considered and rejected

### “Choose `k` from a universal formula”

Rejected. Task decomposability, shared dependencies, mutation topology, deadline, and synthesis cost vary by context. STPA supplies no empirical production function for agent quality or cost.

### “Add one independent reviewer per worker”

Rejected. It doubles coordination load and can reproduce correlated failure if reviewers share sources or detectors. Swiss-cheese layers are only protective when weaknesses do not align; the model itself does not establish that independence.

### “Run full STPA/GSN for every task”

Rejected. Full per-task STPA/GSN adds analyst steps, artifacts, and relationships beyond the proposed guard; that directly conflicts with the stated anti-overfit gate. Retain the four UCA prompts, explicit context, constraint inversion, and evidence trace only.

### “Use a learned critic to score marginal worker value”

Rejected by the anti-overfit gate. It introduces another model, calibration problem, and correlated failure mode. Mechanically visible signals should be tested first.

### “Build a durable orchestration/safety daemon”

Rejected. That is BUILD-LARGE, duplicates the orchestration runtime, tightens coupling, and lacks evidence that a small bus-level policy is insufficient.

## 12. Bottom line

Borrow **STPA/UCCA's method of deriving context-specific constraints from unsafe individual and combined control actions**. Implement it as a small, task-local concurrency safety envelope around existing spawn/continue/cancel/join actions. Use Swiss cheese only to reason about correlated “independence,” normal-accident theory as a veto against extra machinery, and safety cases only as a compact decision/evidence trace.

Do not claim that systems safety tells us the optimal number of workers. It tells us when a proposed count-changing decision is unsafe in a known context. The model may choose the count; the envelope prevents known hazardous omissions, commissions, timing errors, duration errors, and combinations. That narrower transfer is **very likely (80-90%)** to be conceptually sound, based on two independent systems-safety sources and an exact control-topology match. Harness benefit remains **UNVERIFIED — only the capability probe, held-out replay, and combination fault injection in §9 can settle it.**
