# X111 — Cross-domain borrow: reliability engineering → orchestration fan-out

**Unit:** X111  
**Lens:** reliability engineering (FMEA, fault trees, redundancy allocation, common-cause failure) applied to deciding how many parallel agent workers to run and when another worker stops helping  
**Date:** 2026-07-19  
**Scope discipline:** external research and translation only. I did not inspect or make claims about the local harness, corpus, audit, or subject trees. This is intentional: the unit brief says this is an ideation lens and local grounding belongs to neighboring units.

## Executive result

The strongest transferable mechanism is **common-cause-aware redundancy allocation**, with FMEA and a fault tree used to discover the failure structure before any arithmetic is trusted. Reliability engineering does not answer “how many copies?” by assuming that every additional copy is an independent win. It (1) defines the unacceptable top event, (2) exposes single points and combinations that can cause it, (3) separates independent branch failures from common-cause failures, and (4) allocates redundancy only while the marginal risk reduction is worth the resource cost. NASA’s recent summary states the critical result directly: independent redundancy can improve reliability, but no amount of redundancy can drive total failure probability below the common-cause failure probability; with significant common-cause failure, more than two or three identical units often adds little improvement ([NASA, *Common Cause Failures Dominate and Defeat Redundancy*, 2024/2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240013667)). NASA’s older optimization work formulates the allocation question as maximizing reliability subject to multiple cost constraints, or minimizing cost subject to a minimum reliability requirement ([Bien, *Redundancy Optimization for Series K-out-of-N Systems*, NASA TN D-7169](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19730009887/downloads/19730009887.pdf)).

That structure maps well to parallel agents, but only partially. Workers are redundant channels attempting the same mission; shared model lineage, prompt, context, tools, evidence, and judge are coupling mechanisms; and tokens, wall time, rate limits, context consumption, and coordination are resource constraints. The structural transfer is **very likely (80-90%)**, because the source mechanism explicitly covers redundant branches, common-cause coupling, and constrained allocation, while a classic multiversion-software experiment found that nominally independent implementations failed together more often than an independence model predicted ([Knight & Leveson, 1985/1986](https://libraopen.library.virginia.edu/entities/publication/4ac33eeb-79b4-46e4-aef9-f6ec56a62286)). The numerical transfer of standard binary component-reliability formulas to open-ended agent work is **unlikely (20-45%)**, because agent outputs are graded rather than merely working/failed, the mission truth is frequently unavailable at dispatch time, worker failures are task-conditional, and the selector is itself fallible. Evidence for that caution is both the binary/monotone assumptions stated for conventional reliability importance models ([European Space Agency reliability handbook, “Important measures”](https://handbook.reliability.space/en/stable/system/handbook/reliability_prediction/important_measures.html)) and Knight and Leveson’s empirical rejection of independent-failure assumptions in software diversity.

**Verdict:** borrow the *analysis and control loop*, not an uncalibrated closed-form worker-count formula. Build a small, existing-bus policy that treats fan-out as a measured redundancy-allocation decision, models correlated/common-cause failure explicitly, and stops on marginal evidence gain. Do not create a second orchestration runtime.

## 1. What reliability engineering has already solved

### 1.1 FMEA/FMECA: enumerate how a branch can fail before assigning it a number

In its own field, Failure Modes and Effects Analysis is a bottom-up analysis. NASA defines FMEA as a systematic, inductive method that identifies and documents failure modes at a prescribed level and specifies their effects; it is commonly used to identify critical single-failure points ([NASA NPR 8715.3 definition](https://nodis3.gsfc.nasa.gov/displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_8715_0003_&page_name=AppendixB)). NASA’s current GSFC handbook describes FMECA as a living risk-assessment document updated as designs, operating parameters, processes, and knowledge change ([GSFC-HDBK-8004, active baseline dated 2024-08-20](https://standards.nasa.gov/standard/GSFC/GSFC-HDBK-8004)).

The relevant mechanism is not merely a risk-priority-number spreadsheet. It is the disciplined decomposition:

1. Define the item/function and mission phase.
2. Enumerate each way the item can fail.
3. Record local effect, next-level/system effect, cause, existing detection, severity, occurrence evidence, and mitigation.
4. Update the analysis as field evidence changes.

Translated to agent orchestration, a “worker” does not have one generic failure probability. Its failure modes include, for example: false factual claim; correct claim with unusable evidence; failure to cover an assigned slice; duplicated coverage; tool outage; shared-source contamination; instruction loss; timeout; malformed handoff; and correct worker result rejected or distorted by the integrator. This matters because adding workers mitigates some modes (a missed line of inquiry) but not others (all workers receive a misleading premise).

### 1.2 Fault-tree analysis: define the mission failure and expose minimal cut sets

In its own field, Fault Tree Analysis starts with an undesirable top event and works downward through Boolean logic to the faults and conditions that can produce it. NASA’s description says the method can model single and multiple failures, and defines minimal cut sets as the smallest combinations that cause system failure ([NASA NPR 8621.1, Appendix I-3.5](https://nodis3.gsfc.nasa.gov/displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_8621_0001_&page_name=AppdxI35)). NASA’s aerospace fault-tree handbook adds that a one-component minimal cut set is a single failure sufficient for the top event, while an n-component cut set requires all n component failures in that set ([NASA, *Fault Tree Handbook with Aerospace Applications*, v1.1](https://extapps.ksc.nasa.gov/reliability/Documents/Fault_Tree_Handbook_with_Aerospace_Applications_August_2002.pdf)). The NRC’s foundational handbook was created to codify and systematize fault-tree construction and evaluation ([NUREG-0492](https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/nuregs/staff/sr0492/index)).

For an agent mission, use the top event **“the harness accepts a materially wrong or materially incomplete result, or fails to return within the mission budget.”** An illustrative fault tree is:

```text
TOP: mission result unacceptable
  OR
  ├─ evidence/reasoning result unacceptable
  │    OR
  │    ├─ all useful worker channels miss the defect/question
  │    ├─ common-cause premise/context/tool failure affects the worker set
  │    ├─ quorum agrees on the same wrong result
  │    └─ independent disagreement exists but is not surfaced
  ├─ integration result unacceptable
  │    OR
  │    ├─ selector chooses a wrong branch
  │    ├─ synthesizer drops a decisive minority finding
  │    └─ shared evidence cannot adjudicate the disagreement
  └─ mission budget exceeded
       OR
       ├─ worker/tool congestion
       ├─ slowest required branch misses deadline
       └─ fan-out and synthesis consume the token/context budget
```

The tree reveals why “run N and vote” is incomplete. A shared bad specification/prompt, a shared unavailable source, and a shared judge are one-component minimal cut sets in the logical architecture even if there are many workers. Adding identical branches does not remove those cut sets.

### 1.3 Redundancy allocation: optimize reliability under finite resources

In its own field, the Redundancy Allocation Problem chooses how much redundancy to place in a system under cost, weight, volume, or other constraints. NASA TN D-7169 treats two dual problems: maximize system reliability subject to multiple cost constraints, and minimize a cost function while maintaining a minimum acceptable reliability. The paper covers series-connected, k-out-of-n subsystems and discusses exact and approximate solution techniques ([NASA TN D-7169](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19730009887/downloads/19730009887.pdf)). A contemporary review states the same canonical objective: maximize reliability or minimize cost subject to a reliability constraint by selecting and allocating redundant components ([Mellal, 2025, *Review of the redundancy allocation problem*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0305215X.2024.2447078)).

For truly independent, interchangeable branches with individual failure probability `q`, a one-out-of-n success structure has failure probability `q^n`; equivalently, success probability is `1 - q^n`. That curve produces sharply diminishing absolute gain as `n` rises. For k-out-of-n/majority success, the analogous binomial tail applies only when branch outcomes are binary, exchangeable, and independent.

Reliability engineering also has a marginal-importance concept. Birnbaum importance is the partial derivative of system reliability with respect to a component’s reliability—how sensitive system reliability is to improving that component ([ESA reliability handbook, “Birnbaum importance”](https://handbook.reliability.space/en/stable/system/handbook/reliability_prediction/important_measures.html)). This translates more usefully than a fixed universal worker count: ask whether the *next particular worker archetype* has material marginal value for the current mission and fault tree.

### 1.4 Common-cause failure: redundancy is not diversity

Common-cause failure (CCF) is the decisive correction. NASA defines a common cause as an origin producing several failures and distinguishes common-event failures from common-mode failures; both online redundancy and offline spares can be defeated depending on the coupling ([Jones, *Common Cause Failures and Ultra Reliability*](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/archive/nasa/casi.ntrs.nasa.gov/20160005837.pdf)). NASA’s 2024/2025 presentation states that the common-cause probability is a floor that redundancy cannot cross and identifies mitigations including different technologies/manufacturers, physical separation, shielding, and avoiding shared control, power, or location ([NASA NTRS 20240013667](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240013667)). NRC practice similarly assigns components to common-cause groups based on design, function, and operating conditions, and uses beta-factor or multiple-Greek-letter models rather than assuming independence ([NUREG/IA-0212 v1, §2.3.9](https://www.nrc.gov/cdn/legacy/reading-rm/doc-collections/nuregs/agreement/ia0212/ia0212v1.pdf)).

A simple beta-factor intuition partitions a channel’s failure probability into a common-cause share and an independent share. If `q` is a representative branch failure probability and `β` the common-cause fraction, a deliberately simplified one-out-of-n model is:

```text
Q_system(n) ≈ βq + (1 - β) q^n
```

The exact model depends on architecture and CCF assumptions; this equation is an explanatory lower-bound/floor model, not a production estimator. Its lesson is robust: as `n` grows, the independent term shrinks, but the `βq` floor remains.

The software evidence is unusually close to the target domain. Knight and Leveson had 27 independently prepared program versions run against one million tests. Multiple-version coincident failures occurred substantially more often than expected under independence, leading the authors to require dependent errors in reliability analysis ([University of Virginia record and paper](https://libraopen.library.virginia.edu/entities/publication/4ac33eeb-79b4-46e4-aef9-f6ec56a62286)). Therefore, “different agent instances” is not evidence of failure independence. Shared specification alone can induce the same mistake.

## 2. Concrete harness translation

### 2.1 **UNVERIFIED — Diversity-aware fan-out controller** — experiment: retrospective randomized replay

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).**

Build a policy function on the existing orchestration bus, not a new scheduler or daemon. Inputs:

- task risk class: consequence of wrong/incomplete output;
- task decomposability: independent slices versus multiple attempts at the same answer;
- available worker archetypes and their diversity attributes;
- remaining token, time, concurrency, tool, and context budget;
- replay-calibrated marginal detection/correction yield by task class and worker archetype;
- estimated pairwise overlap/common-cause group;
- selector/synthesis reliability on the same task class.

Output:

- zero, one, or a bounded number of additional workers;
- the role of each worker (coverage slice, independent solver, falsifier, evidence verifier, integration checker);
- an explicit stop reason.

The core policy is not “N=3.” It is:

```text
dispatch next candidate worker j only when

  conservative_expected_loss_reduction(j | evidence so far)
    > marginal_token_time_congestion_cost(j)

and the candidate attacks a still-open cut set or failure mode.
```

Use a conservative estimate (for example, a lower credible bound) until enough replay data exist. The controller’s benefit is **likely (55-80%)**, because reliability allocation and marginal importance directly support constrained marginal decisions, and CCF evidence shows why identical copies saturate; however, this harness-specific benefit is **UNVERIFIED — settle it with a retrospective randomized replay comparing fixed fan-outs `{0,1,2,3,4}` and the proposed policy on stratified historical tasks with hidden gold labels or executable tests**. The recommendation inherits the UNVERIFIED tag because no local orchestration outcomes were examined in this unit.

### 2.2 **UNVERIFIED — Worker common-cause groups and a “diversity vector”** — experiment: paired-error correlation study

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only).**

Describe each candidate worker with a small declared diversity vector:

```text

```

Do not award “diversity” merely because seed, wording, or agent name differs. Treat two workers as the same CCF group until observed conditional error overlap shows otherwise. Examples of useful separation hypotheses are: implementation versus property-test generation; claimant versus source verifier; broad search versus primary-source-only search; code author versus fresh-context test runner. Those hypotheses are **likely (55-80%)** to create more useful error diversity than identical prompts, based on NASA’s distinction between redundancy and diversity and Knight/Leveson’s dependent-error result, but they are **UNVERIFIED — settle each vector dimension by measuring paired failure overlap on identical replay tasks, controlling for task class and difficulty**.

The controller should prefer the next worker with the greatest expected *new failure-mode coverage per unit cost*, not simply the individually strongest worker. This would catch a currently easy-to-miss condition at the architecture level: five nominal workers that are actually one failure domain because all share the same prompt premise, context omission, evidence endpoint, or adjudicator.

### 2.3 **UNVERIFIED — Stop rule based on marginal novelty and open cut sets** — experiment: sequential replay with counterfactual prefixes

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).**

After each worker return, extract a compact observation record rather than asking another free-form agent whether “we have enough”:

```text

```

Stop when all high-severity open cut sets have either direct evidence or an explicit accepted residual risk, and the conservative predicted gain of another available diversity class falls below cost. This policy is **likely (55-80%)** to stop earlier than fixed high fan-out on easy tasks and continue longer on high-risk disagreements, because it uses marginal allocation rather than constant redundancy; it is **UNVERIFIED — settle it by replaying worker-result prefixes and measuring the counterfactual quality gain, token cost, wall time, and deadline misses attributable to each next dispatch**.

## 3. What this catches that ordinary parallelism misses

1. **False independence.** Multiple workers share model lineage, prompt, context, or source and repeat one error. Ordinary worker counts call this redundancy; CCF grouping calls it one failure domain.
2. **Shared premise failure.** Every branch faithfully solves the wrong problem. More branches increase confidence without reducing the top-event probability.
3. **Integrator single point.** Correct dissent exists but the synthesis/voting step removes it. The fault tree exposes this as a minimal cut set.
4. **Tool/source common event.** All “independent” workers depend on one endpoint, stale corpus, permission, or malformed context preparation.
5. **Negative marginal worker.** Another dispatch increases deadline, congestion, and synthesis load more than it reduces mission risk.
6. **Wrong redundancy location.** Many solvers are added even though the dominant cut set is evidence verification or final integration. Redundancy allocation instead spends the next unit where marginal importance is highest.
7. **Agreement without diverse evidence.** A quorum is treated as weak when it arises from one common-cause group; a well-supported minority from a different failure domain triggers adjudication.

These are mechanism-level detection targets, not claims that the local harness currently exhibits them. Whether each occurs locally is **UNVERIFIED — the settling experiment is the stratified replay and paired-error study specified above**.

## 4. Adversarial transfer analysis

### 4.1 Structural correspondences that do transfer

| Reliability engineering | Agent orchestration | Strength of correspondence |
|---|---|---|
| Component/channel attempts a required function | Worker attempts a task or assigned coverage function | Strong when success criteria are externally testable |
| Parallel redundancy | Multiple workers can independently expose/solve a failure mode | Strong for decomposable or testable tasks |
| k-out-of-n/voter | quorum, verifier, or synthesizer accepts a result | Moderate; semantic answers rarely support literal voting |
| Common cause/coupling factor | shared model, prompt, context, source, tool, judge | Strong architectural correspondence |
| Cost/weight/volume budget | tokens, time, concurrency, rate limits, context and synthesis load | Strong constrained-allocation correspondence |
| Mission reliability target | tolerated probability/severity of wrong, incomplete, or late result | Moderate; target often cannot be numerically known |
| Field failure data | replay outcomes, tests, later corrections, adjudication | Strong only where labels or executable oracles exist |

The common-cause correspondence is **very likely (80-90%)** to be real rather than metaphorical: both systems contain multiple nominal channels exposed to shared inputs and shared downstream components, and dependent errors have been empirically demonstrated in multiversion software. The allocation correspondence is **likely (55-80%)**, because both spend finite resources for marginal mission-risk reduction, but agent cost and benefit are task-dependent and nonstationary.

### 4.2 Disanalogies that prohibit a literal transfer

**Outputs are not binary.** A component is often modeled as working/failed for a mission interval. An agent answer can be partly correct, novel but unverifiable, stylistically unusable, or wrong in only one consequential claim. ESA’s importance-measure formulation explicitly assumes two-state components and a monotone system ([ESA reliability handbook](https://handbook.reliability.space/en/stable/system/handbook/reliability_prediction/important_measures.html)). An answer system can violate monotonicity: adding a persuasive wrong worker can turn a correct synthesis into a wrong one.

**Workers are adaptive and interacting.** Hardware component reliability is usually modeled against a stated mission profile. Workers may see earlier outputs, change strategy, compete for shared tools, and alter the integrator’s beliefs. Their error probabilities are neither fixed nor exchangeable.

**Truth may be latent.** Hardware failure can often be observed. Open-ended research and design may have no immediate oracle, so apparent agreement cannot estimate correctness without delayed adjudication.

**The voter does not receive commensurate symbols.** Majority voting assumes comparable outputs and a rule relating agreement to correctness. Natural-language results may differ in granularity, assumptions, or evidence while all being compatible—or may share the same fluent error.

**Diversity can reduce individual capability.** A genuinely different model/tool/strategy may be less capable on the task. Reliability engineering already recognizes the trade between component reliability, diversity, and cost, but the agent trade is harder to estimate online.

**More workers can create new failure modes.** Context overload, contradictory edits, rate-limit contention, and synthesis loss mean the architecture is not necessarily coherent/monotone. Conventional parallel reliability formulas assume adding a working component cannot make the system fail; that assumption is explicitly part of standard importance models.

Therefore the claim “three workers are 1-(1-p)^3 reliable” is **almost certainly not (1-5%)** defensible for general agent work, based on violated binary, independence, exchangeability, monotonicity, and oracle assumptions. A calibrated policy for narrow task classes with executable tests is **likely (55-80%)** feasible, because those classes restore a meaningful outcome measure; **UNVERIFIED — settle feasibility by the randomized replay, not by fitting the formula to self-judged answers**.

### 4.3 Where the borrow should not be used

Do not use it to manufacture numerical confidence for one-off creative work, value judgments, underspecified product decisions, or tasks with no credible outcome label. Use qualitative FMEA/fault-tree reasoning there, but do not claim a calibrated reliability probability. Do not equate semantic consensus with k-out-of-n success. Do not infer diversity from process labels. Do not optimize worker count against an LLM judge trained from the same family without separately treating that judge as a common-cause channel.

## 5. Alternatives considered and rejected

### Raw MTBF/MTTF model — rejected

MTBF concerns failures over operating time and repair/renewal assumptions. Most agent fan-out decisions concern one mission with a bounded deadline and a task-conditional chance of useful contribution. “Mean prompts between hallucinations” would obscure severity and task mix. The relevant reliability construct is mission success probability and constrained redundancy allocation, not MTBF.

### FMEA risk-priority number as the dispatch score — rejected

Multiplying ordinal severity, occurrence, and detectability scores creates a convenient ranking but does not estimate the marginal benefit of another worker or capture dependence. Use FMEA to enumerate modes and controls; use replay outcomes for dispatch calibration.

### Fixed N-version majority voting — rejected

Knight and Leveson’s experiment directly undermines independent-error assumptions, and open-ended agent outputs are not identical vote symbols. Majority agreement can amplify a shared premise error. A verifier/falsifier selected for a different failure domain is preferable to a third identical solver when the open cut set is shared-cause error.

### Universal “two or three workers” rule — rejected

NASA’s observation that identical redundancy often saturates after two or three units under significant CCF is a warning, not an agent constant. Task decomposability, branch quality, coupling, selector reliability, severity, and resource price vary. The only defensible fixed number is a conservative temporary cap pending measurement, not a SOTA optimum.

### Full probabilistic-risk daemon or digital twin — rejected by the anti-overfit gate

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-LARGE (new daemon/service/runtime — needs its own justification).** This would reintroduce a second orchestration/observability runtime, one of the explicitly rejected component classes. It is unnecessary for the proposed experiment. A small policy plus existing event records can calculate marginal summaries. No BUILD-LARGE component passes the anti-overfit gate here.

### Learned critic or trained diversity predictor — rejected by the anti-overfit gate

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-LARGE (new daemon/service/runtime — needs its own justification).** A trained critic is explicitly rejected by the brief’s gate and would introduce another shared model failure domain. Start with declared diversity groups and empirical paired-error tables. If those fail, document the failure before considering greater complexity.

## 6. Minimum falsification program

The cross-domain borrow should be rejected, not merely tuned, if it fails the following experiment.

1. **Sample and stratify.** Select historical missions across task type, consequence, decomposability, tool dependence, and availability of an external oracle. Do not use only code tasks.
2. **Replay randomized fan-outs.** For each task, randomly assign eligible policies: no extra worker, fixed `N=1..4`, homogeneous workers, declared-diverse workers, and the marginal controller. Keep budgets explicit.
3. **Blind outcomes.** Prefer executable tests, source-grounded claim adjudication, or delayed owner corrections. Hide the policy and worker count from adjudicators.
4. **Measure dependence.** For every pair/group, estimate conditional co-failure and shared false-claim overlap by task stratum. Compare observed joint failures with the independence prediction. Do not compress this to one global beta unless strata are demonstrably compatible.
5. **Audit the integrator.** Score both the union of raw worker findings and the final synthesis. This separates worker-channel benefit from selector loss.
6. **Construct marginal curves.** For each ordered worker prefix, measure incremental defects found/corrected, final quality, tokens, wall time, tool contention, deadline misses, and synthesis omissions.
7. **Evaluate out of sample.** Freeze policy thresholds, then evaluate on later missions or held-out task families. A within-sample optimum is not evidence of a general controller.
8. **Decision criterion.** Retain the controller only if it improves risk-weighted outcome per cost over the best simple fixed cap and does not worsen high-severity tail outcomes. Otherwise retain only the qualitative CCF checklist.

The experiment will provide two mechanically independent signals for a claimed benefit: (a) external/gold outcome change and (b) raw-to-final defect/evidence accounting. Cost reduction alone is not success, and self-judged answer preference is not an independent reliability signal.

Expected outcomes are deliberately not asserted as facts. It is **likely (55-80%)** that measured joint worker errors will exceed an independence prediction, based on NASA CCF evidence and Knight/Leveson’s software experiment; it is **roughly even (45-55%)** that a small adaptive controller will beat the best fixed cap after accounting for estimation noise and nonstationarity. Both are **UNVERIFIED — this randomized, blinded, out-of-sample replay is the settling experiment**. If the adaptive policy does not beat a simple cap, the correct result is to keep the cap and the common-cause checklist.

## 8. Final design statement

The reliability-engineering lesson is not “redundancy makes systems safe.” It is: **model the top event, find the cut sets, identify common-cause groups, then buy only the redundancy whose marginal risk reduction exceeds its cost.** For an agent harness, that becomes a diversity-aware, evidence-calibrated fan-out and stop policy on the existing orchestration bus. Its primary safety contribution is making shared prompts, contexts, tools, sources, and integrators visible as common-cause failure domains instead of counting agent processes as independent votes.

The qualitative mechanism transfers **very likely (80-90%)**, supported by authoritative NASA/NRC reliability practice and empirical multiversion-software evidence. A universally valid numerical worker-count formula transfers **almost certainly not (1-5%)**, because the target violates binary, independent, exchangeable, monotone, and readily observable outcome assumptions. A small calibrated controller may still be useful, but that is **UNVERIFIED — only the specified randomized, blinded, out-of-sample replay can settle it**.
