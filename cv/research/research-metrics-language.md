# What makes a resume bullet effective in 2026 (technical roles): evidence audit

Research question: What makes a resume bullet effective in 2026 for technical roles? Cover the evidence for and against the XYZ/accomplishment formula, whether quantification improves outcomes or is cargo cult, how to quantify honestly without revenue or users, verb choice, length, density; find actual research or large-scale data on what correlates with callbacks and separate it from consultant advice.

## 2. Findings by question

### 2.1 The XYZ / accomplishment formula: evidence for and against

What is verified about its origin: Bock published the formula on LinkedIn in 2014 (C1), Google's recruiters recommend it on the official careers channel (C2), and neither has ever published outcome data. Every 2025-2026 article calling it "the Google formula that gets you hired" traces back to these two sources plus repetition. Bock's own 2026 framing ("after reviewing 100,000 resumes") is experience, not a study (VERIFIED: the post contains no data).

Evidence FOR the underlying idea (accomplishment statements beat duty statements):
- A2 (1999): accomplishment statements were the single most-cited reason recruiters picked a resume (40 of 64 recruiters). Lab study, undergraduate resumes, n=64.
- A3 (2000): adding competency statements raised interview decisions and rankings; two to six statements is the practical dose. n=62.
- A4 (2017 review): the literature's verdict is that elaborated accomplishment statements help, exaggerated self-description does not.
- A1 (2025): clearer, error-free writing causally raised hires by 8% (n~480k). The XYZ structure is, among other things, a clarity device (one result, one measure, one method per line), so this supports the form indirectly, not the formula.
- B5, B6, B7: recruiters and hiring managers say they value measurable achievements (58%), penalise their absence (44%), and now reject "generic" content (28%). Self-report.

Evidence AGAINST the formula as commonly applied in 2026:
- D1: among 12 tech hiring managers, presence of a number was not what separated winners from losers; a decision plus a verifiable noun was. Bullets that follow the formula mechanically ("Improved system performance by 60% through optimization techniques") landed in the bottom four.
- D2, D3, D5, E2: uniform, high-precision multi-metric bullets now read as fabricated or AI-generated; interviewers ask how the number was measured and reject candidates who cannot answer.
- B7: 41% of hiring managers list "vague or inflated descriptions" and 41% "buzzword-heavy writing" as AI tells; 76% say AI-written resumes make it harder to see what the candidate did. The formula executed by an LLM produces exactly this texture.
- A10: at the scale of 641k resumes, "graded quantifiers" (vague quantity words) had no measurable association with seniority either way, and neither did most other prescriptive language rules; the language layer explains little compared with total experience.
- A16: the 1982 study that the formula's academic defenders cite for "specific beats vague" found ambiguity distorted ratings "but not in a consistent manner".

Verdict (INFERRED from the above): the accomplishment-first structure is supported by three small lab studies and one large RCT on clarity; the specific XYZ wording is marketing with no outcome data; the 2026 failure mode is not "too few numbers" but formula-shaped bullets with unlabelled or unverifiable numbers. Write result plus method plus scale, but let the decision and the domain noun carry the bullet, and put a number only where you can name the metric and the baseline.

### 2.2 Does quantification improve outcomes, or is it cargo cult?

Direct evidence linking numbers to callbacks: exactly one dataset, B1 (TalentWorks 2018): "at least one number every 3 sentences" associated with +40.2% interview rate, n=4,068, correlational, hand-selected factor, vendor subscribers. This single number is the ancestor of every "40% more interviews" claim in the genre, which is now attributed to CareerBuilder, Jobvite, Harvard Business Review, Fast Company, Zippia, LinkedIn and Jobscan by different sites (VERIFIED: all those attributions were found; none of those organisations has a traceable study).

Indirect evidence: recruiters say they value it (B5 58%, B6 44% red flag); Google and Bock recommend it (C1, C2); prevalence data show most resumes have few numbers (B8 36% none; B9 18% none, median 4; B11 31.5% none; B12 2.3% of lines), so a number stands out by rarity. B11's "7 points higher" is circular (its own scorer rewards numbers) and should not be cited.

Evidence that numbers can hurt: D1 (unlabelled percentages ranked bottom, read as AI-generated); D2/D3 (implausible precision on every bullet triggers scepticism; inability to explain measurement is "an instant no"); D5 (recruiting software vendor lists context-free metrics as a fraud signal); B7 (inflated descriptions are an AI tell); E2 (Romanian recruiters flag exaggerated or unverifiable results).

Verdict (INFERRED): quantification is not cargo cult, but the evidence is weaker and older than the confidence of the advice, and the marginal value has shifted. In 2018 a number differentiated you from the 36% with none; in 2026 an LLM adds numbers to everyone's resume, so an unexplained number is worth little and a suspicious one is negative. What differentiates now is a number that is (a) tied to a named metric, (b) has a baseline or denominator, (c) is defensible in an interview, and (d) sits next to the specific decision that produced it. Confidence in "some real numbers beat none": likely (55-80%). Confidence in "every bullet needs a number": rejected.

### 2.3 Quantifying honestly when there is no revenue or user base

Sources: C1 (Bock's grocery-store example: "Served 85 customers per day with 100% accuracy"), C2 (Google: "use data and comparisons or averages to provide context"), D7 (Mustain: multiply frequency into annual scale), B8 (Cultivated Culture categories: scope, efficiency, quantity, leveraging others), D3 (npinsker: be specific elsewhere), D1 (small decisions and small consequences count: "chose Postgres over Mongo for the audit log because we needed range queries"; "saved roughly 4 hours of recurring report work per month per analyst"), D6 (replace fake precision with named action and named result). Practice guidance only; no outcome data isolates these substitutes.

Honest quantities available to a technical candidate without business metrics (INFERRED from the sources' categories, applied to this owner):
- Scale of the thing built: lines/modules/services, endpoints, data volume processed, number of test cases, coverage percentage, number of CI gates, number of repos or packages maintained, languages.
- Before/after engineering measurements you took yourself: latency, build time, memory, error rate, flake rate, runtime. State the metric name and both numbers ("p95 from 2 s to 180 ms"), never a bare percentage.
- Time and frequency: hours per week saved, releases per week, weeks to ship, duration of a project, number of iterations.
- People and coordination: team size, reviewers, users of an internal tool, number of contributors to a repo, number of clients (freelance).
- Verification artefacts: number of tests written, bugs found, incidents resolved, PRs merged, issues closed, external contributions accepted.
- Academic: number of experiments, dataset size, models compared, baseline beaten by how much on which metric, submission venue.
- Public counts that are checkable: GitHub stars (4 on Reframe), followers, downloads, citations once works exist.
- Where a number is confidential or unknown: use a range or an order of magnitude and say so ("~10k rows/day"), or drop the number and keep the decision and the artefact.

Rules from the practitioner sources: every number must survive "how did you measure that?" (D3); precision should match how it was measured (D2); one metric per bullet, not a chain of four (D2); give the denominator (D1); if the honest answer is "I did not measure it", write the method and the artefact instead of inventing a percentage (D6, E2).

### 2.4 Verb choice

Data that exists:
- B1: distinct action verbs at the start of sentences +139.6% (the post admits a tiny baseline, so the ratio is inflated); leadership-oriented words (communicated, coordinated, managed, organisation) +50.9%; "team player / supporting member" phrasing more than once or twice -50.8%; any personal pronoun in the employment section -54.7%. Correlational, 2018, hand-picked.
- A10 (641k resumes): personal pronouns negative across sectors (-0.04 to -0.07); passive voice ~0; hedges and qualifiers ~0; past tense small positive in a few sectors; buzzwords strongly POSITIVE (IT 0.239), which the authors call "surprising" and which contradicts the advice literature; outcome is seniority, not callbacks.
- B9 (1.78M): weak verbs are near-universal ("used", "helped" in 39% each; "responsible for" in 9%); "developed" in 54%. B12 (800 resumes): "developed" on 45%; outcome verbs ("reduced", "improved", "achieved") carry a number 43-63% of the time versus 14-18% for activity verbs; the authors' own reading is that the result drives the verb, not the reverse.
- B10: summaries with "ambitious/collaborative/innovative/strategic" were hired ~24% vs "dependable/compassionate/resourceful" 7-12%; correlational; outplacement sample; these are adjectives, not verbs.
- B11: the words most associated with low scores are self-descriptors ("hardworking", "motivated", "quick learner"), not verbs.
- D1: winners used past simple ("shipped", "cut", "removed"); losers used "was responsible for", "involved in", "contributing to"; managers read the latter as a hedge about who actually did the work.
- E1: "let experience speak, without insisting on attributes like hard-working, leader, creative, multitasking".

No experiment isolates verb choice. What is consistent across every source type: drop "responsible for", "helped", "assisted", "involved in", pronouns and self-descriptive adjectives; open with a past-tense verb that names what changed. What is NOT supported: that swapping "managed" for "orchestrated" changes anything (B12's own conclusion; D1's finding that the noun and decision matter more than the verb). On "team" language the vendor data conflict (B1 penalises collaborative phrasing; B10 finds "collaborative" among the best-performing words); D1 resolves it: teamwork is fine, unattributed teamwork is not; name your piece.

### 2.5 Length

- A2 (1999, new grads): one page preferred over two.
- B2 (2018, 6,305 applications): 475-600 words, "a densely-packed single page", 8.2% interview rate vs under 5% outside; exceptions for academics and scientists, where longer is better.
- B4 (2018, 482 screeners): two pages preferred 2.3x overall and 1.4x even for entry-level; readers spent 4m05s on two pages vs 2m24s on one.
- B3 (2018 eye-tracking): "the two-page rule remains for more experienced job seekers"; page two gets time only if page one earns it.
- B6 (2024): 54% of hiring managers expect two pages; B7 (2026): 43% will not read past two pages, 57% will if relevant; "too long or dense" is the lowest-ranked rejection reason (20%).
- D4 (Orosz): one page for juniors; longer for experienced engineers.
- E1 (Romania): maximum two pages; Europass rejected largely because it exceeds two pages.

Reconciliation (INFERRED): the studies disagree on one vs two pages but agree on a ceiling of two and on density. For a 2025 graduate with some employment and freelance work, the data support one dense A4 page (475-600 words) for industry applications, with a second page acceptable only if it carries substantive content; the academic CV is a separate document where B2's exception applies.

### 2.6 Density and layout of bullets

- B3: bold job titles followed by bulleted accomplishments, F/E-pattern reading; short declarative statements outperform paragraphs; clutter, multi-column layouts and keyword stuffing lose.
- D1: 18-28 words per bullet was the high-engagement band; 8-word bullets lacked the decision, 41-word bullets buried it.
- C1/C2: one line per bullet; Google says bullets, not long descriptions.
- D4: no sub-bullets.
- B7: 72% say inconsistent spacing hurts; 62% say overly designed resumes hurt; 90% say a clear summary speeds evaluation; 85% expect a skills section.
- A8: recruiters who advanced a resume dwelt longer on Experience; the section has to reward a second look.
- Consultant consensus (3-5 bullets per role, most recent role longest) has no data behind it beyond B3's F-pattern logic; treat as convention.

### 2.7 Actual research on callbacks vs consultant advice: what the callback data actually reward

Field experiments and incentivised employer studies (A1, A5, A6, A7, A9) measure what moves callbacks. None of them manipulated bullet wording or numbers. What they found moves callbacks: clearer, error-free writing (A1, causal); verifiable credentials such as a relevant internship, a programming-plus-data-analysis credential, GPA, honours (A5, A6, A7); extracurricular and academic evidence for recent graduates (A9). What they found does NOT move employers: skills lists ("cheap talk", A5; computer skills even negative in A7; number of listed skills ~0 in IT in A10). The eye-tracking work (B3, A8) says the first pass is titles, companies, dates and education, and the second pass is the Experience section.

Consultant advice with no data behind it (see section 3): the "75%" ATS number, the various "2.5x / 3.2x / 40%" quantification multipliers not traceable to B1, "ATS prioritises numbers", the University of Washington misattribution, "every bullet needs a number", and most action-verb lists.

### 2.8 The 2026 layer: ATS, LLM screeners and AI-written resumes

ATS (C3, C4, B2, B7): no major ATS rejects on bullet content; keyword COUNT did not correlate with interviews even in 2018 (B2); what matters is being findable in a recruiter's search (exact skill terms present) and surviving knockout questions. Greenhouse (largest share) is philosophically anti-auto-reject; Workday has no native match score. 37% of hiring managers do let the ATS screen out on criteria they set (B7), which in practice means knockouts and required-skill filters, not prose quality.

LLM screening (A11-A14, B7): 35% of hiring managers say their company uses AI to screen or rank applications; 19% use it to screen out before a human looks (B7). The research says these systems are unreliable in ways a candidate cannot target: they hallucinate requirements and infer skills that were never stated (A14), often cannot pick the more qualified of two comparable resumes (A13), favour their own model's prose (A11, with a contested design), and reward hidden-text injection only when few candidates do it and punish it as it spreads (A12). A14's practical finding matters more than any of this: certifications listed under the wrong heading were ignored, so standard section names and explicit statements beat cleverness.

AI-written resumes (B6, B7, B10, D5, E2): the #1 red flag in 2024 (53%), still a top-five rejection reason in 2026 (28% "generic or AI-heavy"); 80% of managers believe they can spot it (self-report; B7 also shows what they use as tells: unnatural phrasing, uniform generic language, inflated descriptions, buzzwords, perfect uniform grammar, em-dash habits). Romanian recruiters describe the same tells (E2). The tension is real: A1 shows algorithmic help with errors and clarity causally increases hires, while B6/B7/E2 show generated content is penalised. The resolution the sources converge on (E2 explicitly): use tools for correction and structure; write the substance yourself in your own register.

## 3. Contested and unevidenced claims (things everyone repeats and nobody evidences)

C1. "75% (or 70% or 88%) of resumes are rejected by ATS before a human sees them." Traced to a 2012 sales pitch by Preptel, a vendor that closed in 2013 without publishing any method (C4). Vendor documentation and the largest ATS's stated philosophy contradict it (C3). Still printed in 2026 by ATS-optimisation sellers.

C2. "Resumes with quantified achievements get 40% more interviews" (attributed to CareerBuilder, Jobvite, HBR, Fast Company, Zippia, LinkedIn, Jobscan). The only traceable origin is TalentWorks 2018 (+40.2%, n=4,068, correlational, vendor subscribers, hand-selected factor). The other attributions have no study behind them. "2.5x more interview invitations (LinkedIn Talent Report 2023)", "3.2x (Resume Research Institute)", "78% of hiring managers say metrics are most persuasive (LinkedIn Talent Solutions)" and "34% skip resumes lacking measurable results (Resume.io)" could not be traced to any published survey; treat as fabricated or laundered.

C3. "Every bullet needs a number; if it doesn't have a number it didn't happen." Contradicted by D1 (3 of the top 8 bullets had no number; unlabelled percentages ranked bottom), D2/D3/D5/E2 (context-free numbers read as AI or fraud), and by the absence of any study showing per-bullet quantification density predicts callbacks.

C4. "ATS prioritise numbers / metrics trigger ATS keywords / resumes with percentages are 2.5x more likely to pass an ATS scan." No vendor documentation says any such thing; ATS parse and match skills, titles, education and knockout answers (C3); keyword count did not correlate with interviews (B2).

C5. "A University of Washington study found resumes with measurable achievements scored 40% higher in AI screenings." Misattribution. The UW study (A15) varied only names and measured race/gender bias in LLM retrieval; it contains no quantification finding.

C6. "Only 26% of candidates include measurable data (2026)." Cultivated Culture's 2021 scanner data (B8), self-selected users, "not scientifically gathered", re-dated by 2026 articles.

C7. "Recruiters spend 6-7 seconds on a resume." True of the initial triage in two Ladders studies (30 recruiters in 2012; 7.4 s in 2018), but the same vendor found engaged readers spend as long on page two as page one, ResumeGo timed 2-4 minutes in a simulation, and 57% of hiring managers self-report 1-3 minutes (B6). Both stages exist; the 6-second figure describes the cut, not the read.

C8. "One page, always" vs "two pages are preferred." Genuinely mixed: A2 (one page for new grads, 1999), B2 (475-600 words, dense single page), B4 (two pages 2.3x, even entry level 1.4x), B6 (54% expect two), B7 (43% stop after two). Level-dependent; the only firm ceiling in the data is two pages.

C9. "Avoid buzzwords" vs "buzzwords help." A10 found buzzword use strongly and positively associated with senior titles; B1 found a buzzword every 3-6 sentences +29.3%; B3, B7, B10, B11 and E1 find buzzword-heavy or self-descriptive language penalised. Likely resolution (INFERRED): technical keywords in context help findability; self-descriptive adjectives ("dynamic", "passionate", "results-driven") hurt; the datasets use the word "buzzword" for both.

C10. "Use strong/leadership action verbs" (+139.6%, +50.9% in B1). Correlational, from an inflated baseline, never replicated; A10 found most syntactic language rules near zero; B12 shows verb-number co-occurrence runs from the result to the verb. Dropping "responsible for / helped / assisted" and pronouns is supported across source types; verb "power" beyond that is not.

C11. "Team-player language is penalised" (B1, -50.8%) vs "collaborative" among the best-performing words (B10). Conflicting vendor data; D1's resolution (name your contribution inside the team result) is the only version with a mechanism.

C12. "LLMs prefer AI-written resumes, so write with the same model." A11's effect was measured on executive summaries evaluated in isolation, not whole resumes (HN critique); A12 shows any gaming advantage saturates as adoption spreads; A13/A14 show LLM screeners are too unreliable to target; B7 shows humans penalise the resulting texture. Speculative at best.

C13. "The XYZ formula is proven / Google-validated." No outcome data from Google or Bock exists (C1, C2). Supported only indirectly by small lab studies on accomplishment statements (A2, A3).

C14. "Algorithmic writing help mattered most for non-native English writers." A1 found larger error-rate improvements for non-native speakers but NO significant difference in the hiring effect (8.4% vs 7.35%). The hiring effect is real for everyone; the "largest for non-native" phrasing in secondary sources describes spelling, not hires.

## 4. What this means for this owner (actionable synthesis)

Verified facts about him used here: 2022-2025 BSc, Romania, 68 public repos (mostly forks; ~20 originals; top repo Reframe at 4 stars), no registered publications yet, undocumented employment and freelance work, non-native English writer, three targets (engineering roles, MSc/PhD, master CV).

1. Fix clarity and errors before anything else (A1, causal, 8% hires). He writes English as a second language; run every bullet through a non-generative grammar and clarity pass and read it aloud. This is the single intervention with randomised evidence behind it.

2. Convert duties into accomplishment statements, two to six per role, and lead with the outcome (A2, A3, A4, C1, C2). Structure: past-tense verb, what changed, the named metric with baseline and new value, the specific method or decision. Keep the XYZ elements; do not keep the XYZ rhythm on every line (D1, B7).

3. Put a number only where he can name the metric, the baseline and the measurement method in an interview (D2, D3, D5, E2). Never a bare percentage. Preferred form: "cut CI wall time from 14 min to 6 min by caching the build layer" rather than "improved CI performance by 57%".

4. Mine his real, checkable quantities (section 2.3): test counts and coverage in his repos, CI gates, latency or runtime measurements he actually took, dataset sizes and model comparisons from coursework or research, freelance client count and deliverables, repo/star/contributor counts where non-trivial. Where he did not measure, write the decision and the artefact instead of inventing a figure (D1, D6).

5. Make each bullet carry one specific technical decision and one domain noun that only someone who did the work would write (D1, D3 "instant no" test). For Reframe, WebCoder, quality-zero-platform, agent-skills-toolchain: name the architecture choice, the rejected alternative and what it cost.

6. Verbs: open every bullet with a past-tense verb; delete "responsible for", "helped", "assisted", "involved in", all pronouns and all self-descriptive adjectives (B1, A10, B9, D1, E1). Do not spend time on "power verb" upgrades (B12, D1).

7. Team work: keep it, but attribute ("owned the auth layer of a four-person project; my pieces shipped in week 6") (D1 resolving B1 vs B10).

8. Length and density for industry roles: one dense A4 page, 475-600 words, 18-28 words per bullet, no sub-bullets, bold titles, standard section names, single column, text PDF (B2, B3, B4, B7, D1, D4, E1). Two pages only when page two has substance. Academic CV separately, longer, with the B2 exception.

9. Skills lists are cheap talk (A5, A7, A10). Keep a compact skills block for recruiter search terms (B5 76% filter by skills; C3), but spend the space on verifiable evidence: internships and employment (once extracted by interview), repos with tests and CI, and publications once registered on ORCID. For the MSc/PhD track, A9 says academic and extracurricular evidence carries weight for recent graduates; put research projects, competitions and teaching there.

10. Do not optimise for LLM screeners beyond hygiene (A12-A14): standard headings, certifications under "Certifications", explicit statements of tools used, no hidden text, no white-font keywords.

11. AI use: assistant for correction, structure and tailoring; never for content (E2, B7, B6). Vary sentence rhythm, avoid "leveraged / spearheaded / dynamic / passionate", avoid em-dash-heavy prose, keep his own register. Romanian recruiters and US hiring managers name the same tells.

12. Romania-specific (E1, E2; single publisher, quoting Adecco RO and Microsoft EMEA; treat as UNVERIFIED beyond eJobs): no Europass; two-page ceiling; a professional photo is conventional locally (but omit it for international tech applications per D4); a short summary; numbers placed early in the sentence; "achievements, the contribution you brought" is what Adecco RO says it looks for.

## 5. Limitations of this audit

- The only randomised evidence (A1) is about writing quality, on a freelance platform, with a non-generative tool in 2021. Nothing randomised tests numbers, verbs or the XYZ structure.
- The only vendor data tying language to interview rates (B1, B2) is 2018, one vendor, its own subscribers, hand-selected factors, never replicated.
- The 2026 practitioner evidence for "decision beats number" is one 12-manager ranking exercise run by someone who sells a rewriting tool (D1), plus Hacker News comments. Directional, not generalisable.
- Survey percentages (B5, B6, B7, B10) are self-reports by hiring managers about what they value or can detect; they are not measured behaviour.
- Romanian evidence is one publisher (eJobs) quoting agency recruiters; I found no Romanian survey with methodology on quantified achievements.
- Several old I/O studies (A2, A3, A16) were read through abstracts and secondary summaries because the full texts are paywalled.
