## 0. Verdict in one paragraph

In 2026 the "AI engineer" the market hires is a **software engineer first who has shipped LLM-backed systems to production**, and the loop tests exactly the things a demo does not show: an evaluation harness that caught a real regression, a production failure story with the metric attached, unprompted cost/latency numbers, judgment about when *not* to use an LLM, and the ability to explain and own code an agent wrote. "Uses AI tools" now carries zero weight; "builds with AI" is table stakes; "builds AI systems that survive real users" is the scarce thing. Orchestrating agents is treated as a **strength only when paired with visible verification and ownership** (Augment, Elicit, Canva, Meta, Karpathy's "agentic engineering"), and as a **red flag when it is the whole story** (maintainers' zero-tolerance AI-slop policies; hiring managers who say they would rather hire someone "behind on AI" with judgment than someone with "complex agent setups and prompt libraries"). For a 2025 graduate the structural headwind is severe and well-measured (new-grad hiring down 50-65% at big tech vs 2019; junior SWE vacancies down ~15% relative to senior after ChatGPT; AI-specific skills did *not* protect junior postings), so the winning move is not more repos but **one to three inspectable, measured, deployed artifacts with users**, plus referrals/communities rather than cold applications.

## 2. Claims, with source counts and what each means for the owner

### Claim 1. The 2026 "AI engineer" is an applied-LLM software engineer; evals and agents now outrank every ML framework in job descriptions, and framework names carry almost no signal.
- VERIFIED: Dexity 390 JDs (evals 56%, agents 50%, LangChain family 10%); Metix 259 agent JDs (continuous evaluation 73%, reliable operation 87%, LangChain 9.3%); InterviewStack 3,449 (RAG 40% "now baseline"); AI Dev Jobs 8,967 (agents 30.9%); Standout 534 (infra skills outrank vector DB and MCP); Lightcast/AI Index (agentic-AI skills +280% YoY). Practitioner convergence: Ergini ("software engineer first who has built and shipped LLM-backed systems"), Cadence ("They do not train foundation models. They do not publish papers"), Bouchard ("Writing LangChain or Pinecone on a resume does not show...").
- Source count: 6 data corpora + 5 practitioner accounts (11). Confidence: **high**. Recency: 2026-04 to 2026-09.
- INFERRED for owner: title yourself "AI/agent infrastructure engineer" or "applied AI engineer", not "AI orchestrator"; list two or three tools you have opinions about, not thirty; make evals and production operation the spine of the CV, not model names.

### Claim 2. What separates a credible candidate from a tool user is a short, convergent list: a system shipped to real users, an eval harness that caught a real regression, a production-failure story with the metric attached, unprompted cost/latency numbers, and knowing when *not* to use an LLM.
- VERIFIED practitioner accounts naming the same signals independently: Ergini (five screening questions; "/evals folder... one of the strongest senior signals"; "I would not use AI here" instinct); tianpan.co (eval from a vague spec, debug a hallucinating prompt, estimate pipeline cost); Kothari ("tell me about an AI system you shipped, and what went wrong with it"; rubric weights reliability 2/3); Cadence ("tell me about your eval harness" as the single best filter); RocketDevs (three non-technical checks: how did you measure, what can a compromised agent reach, what does one interaction cost); Roell (six must-haves incl. own eval harness and failure stories); TryCrucible ("almost nobody can build a real eval pipeline... you are immediately in the top tier"); Bouchard (baseline + verified test set + error analysis); gitGood; Canva's pilot observations; Pragmatic Engineer HMs (buzzword resumes with "little substance").

## 3. Contested, over-repeated, or unsupported claims

1. **"78% of hiring managers find GitHub contribution analysis more predictive than algorithmic interviews" (starfolio.dev, "survey of 500 hiring managers", correlation 0.72 vs 0.24).** Vendor selling GitHub-based assessment; no methodology, no sample description, contradicted by CoderPad 2026 (portfolio review rated predictive by only 11% of recruiters / 23% of developers). Treat as marketing.
2. **johal.in statistics ("72% of senior FAANG+ hires had zero public GitHub activity", "IEEE Software 2025 survey of 142 hiring managers", "2026 ACM survey of 1,200 hiring managers", "Gartner 2026: 60% of roles will require verified production impact documentation", "recruiters spend a median 47 seconds on GitHub per 2026 LinkedIn Talent Insights").** None of these surveys could be located; two articles by the same author cite mutually inconsistent numbers and read as generated. UNVERIFIED; do not cite.
3. **"AI is why juniors can't get hired."** The decline is real (Claim 5) but attribution is contested: NY Fed attributes recent-grad unemployment more to remote work; Deming notes junior hiring fell ~6 months before ChatGPT; SIEPR: with added controls, entry-level declines "not notable until 2024"; Ramp/Revelio: heaviest AI-spending firms *grew* entry-level headcount +12%; NACE employers mostly say AI is not reducing entry-level tasks. Two hypotheses (AI vs remote/rates/over-hiring normalisation) remain live; the Stanford lab itself calls it "an early signal, not a verdict".
4. **"Evals are the #1 skill" as a universal law.** Strongly supported for applied LLM products (Dexity 56% of JDs, Metix 73%, Hamel/Shreya, OpenAI) but Hamel himself concedes coding-agent products can lean on upstream evals and dogfooding; and Amplify's survey shows most teams still just edit prompts. "Almost nobody can build a real eval pipeline" is a single vendor claim (TryCrucible). The safe reading: evals are the highest-leverage *differentiator* precisely because most candidates lack them.
5. **"Companies now let you use AI in interviews."** True for a named minority (Canva, Meta pilot, Rippling, Shopify, Red Hat, CoderPad, Elicit, many startups); false as a default: Karat 62% prohibit, CoderPad 34% ban + 20% case-by-case, Anthropic bars it in assessments, Google bans it in virtual interviews. Robert Half's "slightly more than 25% allow, heading to 50%" is SECONDARY (via HeroHunt/IEEE-USA).
6. **"Take-homes are dead."** Karat: signal degrades fastest; CoderPad recruiters rate them predictive at only 12%; Elicit's take-home was one-shot by a new model. But TryCrucible says standardised take-home challenges are the fastest-growing format, Kothari and Cadence recommend paid take-homes scored on failure-mode handling, and gitGood calls the take-home "the most important round" at AI startups. Reality: take-homes survive when redesigned around evals/tradeoffs and followed by a live defence.
7. **"Interview cheating is 38.5% / 48%."** Fabric's figures are *flags* from a proprietary classifier (threshold 40% probability), on AI-conducted interviews, from a vendor that sells detection. Directionally corroborated (Karat >50% use AI despite bans; interviewing.io 81% suspect) but the exact rates are unverifiable.
8. **"Anthropic RCT: juniors using AI scored 50% vs 67% on comprehension."** Reported only via SoftwareSeni's summary of Anthropic research; not located at the primary. UNVERIFIED (single secondary source).
9. **Headline salary and shortage numbers (ManpowerGroup 72% unfillable; PwC 56%/62% premium; "3.2:1 demand/supply"; "$206K average AI engineer").** All SECONDARY through staffing-vendor content; plausible direction, unverified magnitudes.
10. **"AI-generated code is 2.74x more vulnerable / 45% has high-risk flaws / 41% of all code is AI-written."** Repeated across CTO-guide content without primary citations in what I read; not load-bearing here but do not repeat as fact.
11. **A number inside a primary source is internally inconsistent:** IZA DP 18723 text as extracted says junior AI/ML vacancies "fell by 86.1%, from 1,915 to 1,029"; the absolute figures imply −46%. Use the absolute figures.
12. **"Senior practitioners must be on a Max 20x plan / spend >$100/month" (ElevateX).** A single recruiter's heuristic; not evidence of market practice.

## 4. What this means for the owner, concretely (INFERRED synthesis)

Positioning
- Title: "Applied AI / Agent Infrastructure Engineer (software engineering first)". Avoid "AI orchestrator", "vibe coder", "prompt engineer", and "ChatGPT power user" (Ergini lists the last as an automatic red flag).
- One-line thesis for the CV: you build and operate agent systems with the verification around them — specs, evals, harness debugging, cost control, postmortems.

Artifacts to have before applying (30-60 days)
1. One flagship, deployed system with a live URL that someone other than you has used for weeks (Willison's test), with a README that answers: problem/for whom; approach and alternatives; how you evaluated it and the numbers; failure modes and handling; what you would change. Add `make eval` (or equivalent) that produces a number on a held-out set, a non-LLM baseline it beats, tests + CI, cost per request and p95 latency.
2. A written postmortem of a real failure in your harness/fan-out work, with the metric, hypothesis, fix, and what changed structurally (ties to Claims 2, 6, 12).
3. A technical write-up with measurements (e.g., "harness tuning moved task success by N points on M tasks"). This is the cheapest thing that counts as "published work" at employers that list it (Datalab-style JDs) and is a public forcing function for tradeoff thinking (Ergini).
4. Curated GitHub: pin 3-5 repos, archive the rest, remove anything that looks generated-and-unused. Recent, dated, human-readable commits; disclose AI assistance in contribution notes.
5. One or two merged, non-trivial PRs to a tool you actually use (eval frameworks, MCP servers/SDKs, agent harness libraries). Follow the project's AI policy explicitly; never drive-by.

Interview preparation
- Two parallel drills: (a) AI-allowed live build in a real repo, narrating plan → constrained prompts → review/reject → tests, and (b) unaided coding + "design a RAG/agent system with eval and monitoring" system design. Ask every recruiter which mode applies.
- Ready answers: "Tell me about an AI system you shipped and what went wrong"; "How do you know your system is better this week than last?"; "What does one user interaction cost?"; "What can the agent reach if compromised?"; "What is in your CLAUDE.md/AGENTS.md and how did it evolve?"; "When would you not use an LLM?"; "Explain exactly what happens when a model calls a tool"; "Which tools are models and which are wrappers?".
- Keep fundamentals: 30 min/day unaided coding and one system-design problem per week; the Meta loop still has a no-AI algorithm round.

Channels and geography
- Bucharest holds ~73% of Romanian AI openings; junior AI roles surface at banks/telcos/digital services (Demeter). Use the University of Bucharest network and local meetups for referrals.
- Remote-for-US/UK pays 2-3x local but competes globally at a senior bar; target FDE, solutions and AI-platform roles where shipping and customer-facing judgment matter more than years.
- Communities over cold applications: latent.space, Hamel/Shreya eval-course alumni, Anthropic/OpenAI hackathons (Cadence's sourcing list), plus a three-sentence note with a 60-second demo to the hiring manager for every role you care about.

What not to do
- Do not list frameworks by the dozen, describe "built a RAG chatbot" without numbers, or present agent fleets as the achievement.
- Do not fake "no AI" in a banned round; detection has moved to behavioural signals and in-person rounds, and the follow-up questions ("why this line?") are where it fails.
- Do not chase ORCID/publications for applied roles.

## 5. Limits of this research
- WebSearch was unavailable (budget exhausted) and four MCP search providers were dead; coverage relied on Exa/Tavily indexes, which over-represent English-language vendor and Substack content. US-centric data dominates; Romania data is thin and mostly vendor-produced.
- Most "what hiring managers look for" sources are practitioner essays or vendor content; their convergence (13 independent accounts naming the same five signals) is the evidence, not any single one.
- Cheating rates and shortage/salary figures are vendor-sourced and reported as flags/estimates.
- Causation for the junior-hiring collapse is unresolved in the academic literature as of mid-2026.
