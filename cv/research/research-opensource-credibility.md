## 2. Claims with evidence

Format: claim; evidence and quotes; independent source count; confidence; recency; what it means for the owner.

### C1. A project claim reads as credible when each bullet exposes scope, mechanism, a reproducible result, and its boundary, and separates "I" from "we"

VERIFIED. Knowledge Gate's "Resume Project Section: Defensible Interview Claims" treats every bullet as "an interview contract" with four claim types (scope, mechanism, result, ownership) and prescribes a six-column ledger: exact draft phrase / what I personally built / proof artifact / measurement method / boundary or caveat / likely interviewer probe; it tells you to delete any phrase whose proof cell is empty, to strip labels like "scalable, production-ready, secure, end-to-end" unless you can define the test behind them, and to attach a boundary sentence to every number ("local Docker test", "100,000 seeded rows", "50 virtual users", "p95"). SWE Resume: a project "feels credible instead of decorative" when the reader can see "failure cases, design choices, and operational thought"; list stack, a line of context, two or three bullets of real engineering work, "a clear ownership signal", and "any real credibility signal like active users, contributors, production deployment, stars, uptime, revenue, or adoption". Ethan Roseman (ex-Amazon VP, via messa.ai): "The key distinction: We vs. I. Participant vs. owner. Exposure vs. expertise." ResumeSolving's 18 red flags: "borrowed impact", "unrealistic scope for your level", "scope is floating in space"; the fix is "one region, one product line, one quarter, one workflow". Jan Tegze (Director of Talent Acquisition): describe "what you actually did, why it was hard, and what became possible because you did it". hackajob (2026-09-18): "Built, shipped, owned, debugged, migrated" beat "familiar with, exposure to, worked with".

Sources: knowledgegate.ai; sweresume.app; blog.messa.ai (Roseman); resumesolving.com; newsletter.jobsearch.guide (Tegze); hackajob.com; skilldential.com. Count: 7. Confidence: high. Recency: 2025-2026.

Owner: every repo is solo, so write "sole author" once per project and never "led" or "we". Before the resume exists, build the six-column ledger for the two or three projects that will appear, and cut any phrase whose proof column is empty. Use "scalable" nowhere.

Sources: resumebuilder.com; career.io; Novoresume; monster.com; FlexJobs via Inc.; standout-cv.com; LinkedIn (Rao). Count: 7 (all self-report, all commercial). Confidence: likely for the 25-37% band; the 64% figure is not trusted. Recency: 2025-2026.

Owner: an unpadded resume whose numbers carry boundary sentences is itself a differentiator in a pool where a quarter or more admit padding and reviewers price that in.

### C15. Whether a recruiter will open the GitHub link at all is genuinely uncertain and stage-dependent; the resume must stand alone, and the repo is for the technical reviewer

VERIFIED that the sources disagree. Marlow & Dabbish's employers all used GitHub, but they were recruited because they did. HN hiring managers: "I'll visit it about a fifth of the time"; "I rarely look at GitHub profiles and candidates rarely list them"; "if you list it in your resume, expect me to read it"; "A hiring manager ... I've looked at maybe two of them ... I noticed no correlation with quality". Orosz: recruiters "won't click through to links in a resume if there are a LOT of applications". CoderPad 2026: portfolio review is used by 11% of recruiters and 23% of developers who hire. A 60-hiring-manager survey (profy.dev): most would look at a portfolio site, but its absence "wouldn't matter much". An ex-Meta hiring manager (YouTube, Feb 2026): "Hiring managers don't care about your personal projects. they don't read the code on your GitHub." Counter: Ondsel and the HN 2024 thread: at FOSS-centric and small companies it is decisive.

Sources: Marlow & Dabbish; HN threads (4); Orosz; CoderPad 2026; profy.dev; YouTube (ex-Meta HM); ondsel.com. Count: 9. Confidence: uncertain about frequency; high that it is stage-dependent (recruiter screen rarely, hiring-manager/engineer stage often). Recency: 2018-2026.

Owner: write each project bullet so it is complete without the click (airesume.guru and SWE Resume say the same); link the specific file, PR or release rather than the profile root (HN 2024: "linking them directly to specific files of a project, specific PRs and contributions ... makes the job much easier for the other side").

### C16. For an early-career candidate with no employment history, the projects section is the primary evidence and is weighed more heavily than for experienced candidates

VERIFIED. Akkodis: "The earlier you are in your software engineering career, the more important this section actually is"; for seniors "personal projects are much less important". Orosz: "For new grads, side projects are a great way to show that you've done something"; juniors should "work on their projects just so they have some code in production to show at job interviews". Marlow & Dabbish's six job seekers were all students or under one year into a first job and used GitHub "to supplement their application materials". HN 2018: "Honestly, it should only matter for New Grad positions." airesume.guru: "Projects matter most for entry-level; become supplementary with experience."

Sources: akkodis.com; Orosz (2 pieces); Marlow & Dabbish; HN 2018; airesume.guru. Count: 5. Confidence: high. Recency: 2013-2026.

Owner: lead the resume with two or three projects presented like jobs (title, role "sole author", stack, dates, three bullets, proof links), then education. Everything else is secondary.

## 3. Consolidated checklist: what makes a solo project claim trusted

1. Ownership stated once, precisely: "sole author" or "I wrote the API and migration; the UI was a teammate's".
2. One-line problem that a stranger can picture, and the constraint it had to respect.
3. One or two mechanisms named concretely (index, constraint, cache, pipeline stage, eval gate), not adjectives.
4. A result with a boundary sentence: what was measured, how, on what data, in what environment, how many runs, which percentile.
5. Verification visible: tests (count), CI (badge and history), releases (tags), a benchmark script or saved output that reproduces the number.
6. Adoption evidence only if real: downloads, dependents, external issue reporters, named users, merged upstream PRs. Otherwise say "no external users yet".
7. AI assistance disclosed in the same sentence structure the OSS world now uses: tool, extent, what you owned, how you verified.
8. A "graveyard": one thing that broke, was killed, or was found wrong by your own review. Interviewers explicitly probe for it.
9. Proof adjacent to claim: the bullet links the release, the PR, the DESIGN.md or the demo, not the profile root.
10. Nothing on the page you could not explain for two minutes, from memory, while screen-sharing, without AI.

## 4. What interviewers do with the claim (rehearsal map)

- Layer 1: "Walk me through it." (architecture from memory; draw it)
- Layer 2: "Why that choice? What did you reject?" (trade-off with a named alternative)
- Layer 3: "What broke? How did you find it? What was your first wrong hypothesis?" (the unglamorous story)
- Layer 4: "Where did that number come from? Baseline, method, environment, percentile?" (boundary sentence)
- Layer 5: "Would it handle 10x? What would you change today?" (the honest answer may be "I did not test that; here is how I would")
- Ownership check: "Open the migration / the test; change it live." (RemoteEngine, Knowledge Gate, Canva, Meta)
- Consistency check: the same topic re-asked later from a different angle; the resume cross-checked against LinkedIn and GitHub (SalesFirst; the freethoughtblogs "interviewed a fraud" account shows LinkedIn cross-checks ending an interview).

## 5. Templates for the owner (fill from repo logs; do not invent numbers)

Project bullet (solo, AI-agent-executed):

`<Project> (sole author; <stack>; <first tag> to <latest tag>, <N> releases) — <one-line problem>. Built <mechanism>; measured <result> on <data/environment>, <runs/percentile>; <T> tests, CI on every push. Implementation largely generated by Claude Code / OpenAI Codex agents under my direction: I wrote the specifications, reviewed every change, own the test and release gates, and found <defect class> that the agents missed. Proof: <release link> · <DESIGN.md> · <benchmark log>.`

README "AI assistance" section (modeled on Ghostty and the Python devguide):

`AI assistance: code in this repository was produced with Claude Code and OpenAI Codex under my direction. I wrote the requirements and design notes, reviewed every diff, and maintain the tests, CI and releases. I can explain any file here without AI tools. Contributions to this repo must disclose AI use the same way.`

When challenged on a number:

`"To be precise about what I measured: <metric> on <setup>, <N> runs, <percentile>. The resume line compresses that; the honest range is A-B and I can send the saved log tonight."` If it was overstated: `"That wording is imprecise. What I actually did was <X>; <Y> was not mine / was not measured."` Then move on. Do not manufacture a figure.

Things to remove before the resume ships: "scalable", "production-ready", "end-to-end", "led", "we", "10x", any round percentage without a saved measurement, any tool in the skills list that has no project story, the ORCID iD (it lists zero works; a click shows nothing).

## 6. Contested claims, myths, and sources excluded

1. "Recruiters spend 6 (or 7.4) seconds on a resume." Origin: Ladders eye-tracking, 2012 (30 recruiters, 10 weeks, commercial sponsor, methodology never peer-reviewed) and a 2018 re-run reporting 7.4 s with no headcount published (Ladders PDF; Distinct Recruitment; ResumeHeatMap; MyLiveCV; Spectacle). Counter-evidence: Jan Tegze timed 114 recruiters on three CVs: averages 17, 27 and 46 s, minimum 12 s, maximum 2 min 27 s; a "coin flip" resume study reported a median of 31 s; CareerBuilder: 68% under two minutes. Resolution: the 6-second figure describes an initial fit/no-fit triage, not the read a surviving resume gets. Sources disagree; the popular version is unsupported.
2. "Quantify every bullet." Orosz (2020) still advises numbers and reports only ~15% of engineer resumes carry them; 2025-2026 practitioners (The Resume Writers, Poyser, Blair, hiration, Tegze, ResumeWriting.net) say a round number on every bullet now reads as an AI tell and that "34% of hiring managers discard resumes without measurable results" (Resume.io, uncited) has created a perverse incentive. Resolution: only numbers you can source, scope and survive a follow-up on; scope/volume/time/frequency counts are safer than percentages.
3. "Precise numbers beat round numbers." Batson and BirJob say "18.6% feels measured"; a commenter on Joiner's thread notes "more precise numbers are also kind of BS ... an illusion of knowledge". Unresolved; spurious precision is its own tell. Precision is only safe when traceable to an artifact.
4. "X% of recruiters check GitHub." fonzi.ai's "60 to 80 percent ... industry surveys" names no survey; starfolio.dev's "78% of 500 hiring managers" comes from a vendor selling GitHub-based assessment and reports a "correlation coefficient of 0.72" with no method; johal.in's "68% never click", "median 47 seconds (2026 LinkedIn Talent Insights)" and "2025 IEEE Software survey of 142 hiring managers" could not be traced to any primary and sit beside an invented GitHub feature ("GitHub 2.10 Contribution Context Graph", "cryptographic signatures ... tampering is detected 100% of the time", "89% of companies will require a CCG link"). All treated as unverified; the johal.in pages as fabricated.
5. "80% of hiring managers value personal projects (Developer Hiring Survey, 2024)." Repeated by airesume.guru; no such survey located. Unverified.
6. "Hiring managers don't care about projects / never read your GitHub." Asserted by an ex-Meta hiring manager (YouTube, 2026) and several HN commenters; contradicted by Marlow's employers, Ondsel, Orosz and other HN hiring managers. Resolution: depends on company type and seniority; for a candidate with no employment history projects are the only evidence, so the assertion does not change the owner's plan.
7. "1 in 10 AI PRs is legitimate." A single maintainer's quote in a GitHub community discussion, generalised by a sourcing vendor (Refolk). curl's measured numbers concern bug-bounty reports (about 20% AI slop, about 5% genuine), not PRs. Weakly evidenced.
8. "AI made me N times faster." METR's RCT (n=16, 246 issues): 19% slower while perceiving 20% faster; GitHub's 2023 controlled-task study claimed 55% faster; the two are not contradictory (different tasks and experience levels) but together they show a personal multiplier is unmeasurable without a baseline. Any such number on a resume is unsupported.
9. "ATS or recruiters detect AI-written resumes." A May 2026 audit (reported second-hand) found none of ten major ATS platforms detect AI authorship; a September 2026 vendor-doc review found no authorship check in Greenhouse, Workday, Lever or iCIMS, and an Oracle "authenticity check" whose signals are undisclosed; LinkedIn posts claiming shipped classifiers cite nothing. Related: "ATS rejects 75% of resumes" has no study behind it (Tegze). Contested; what is documented is human pattern-matching (round percentages, parallel three-part bullets, verb clusters), not software detection.
10. "Stars prove adoption." Orosz suggests citing stars of projects you contributed to as a proxy for their reach; Marlow's employers, BVP, Bacon, Bernard and Riem call stars on your own project vanity. Resolution: stars of an upstream project you were merged into say something about the bar you cleared; stars on your own repo say little.
11. "Hiring managers can spot inflated metrics within 7 seconds" (Blair, LinkedIn) is rhetoric, not data.
12. "GitHub is your new resume" (2012 slogan quoted by Marlow) versus employers' own caution ("someone who has a wife and kids and not much time ... that doesn't necessarily mean they are not as good of a developer", E7): a strong GitHub is a plus, a sparse one is rarely disqualifying (fonzi's "fewer than five percent of cases" is unsourced).

Excluded as T4: johal.in (three pages; fabricated statistics and features), personalgoldclub.com and diywireless.net (long-form fiction with content-farm number patterns), staroneship.com (same), starfolio.dev (vendor, unverifiable method). LockedIn AI (an interview-cheating tool) was used only to corroborate the list of companies allowing AI rounds, which Canva's and interviewing.io's own pages confirm.
