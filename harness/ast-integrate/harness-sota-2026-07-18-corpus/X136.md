# X136 — Systems-safety borrow for failure recovery

## Executive finding

The strongest transferable mechanism is not the Swiss cheese metaphor by itself. It is the combination of (1) **fault containment and failure detection/isolation/response/recovery (FDIR)**, (2) **STPA analysis of recovery as a control action whose timing, context, duration, and feedback can be unsafe**, and (3) a **small recovery assurance case backed by kill-point fault injection**. This transfers because an agent resumer and a safety controller share the relevant structure: each observes an incompletely known process state, chooses a consequential action, may receive stale or missing feedback, and can worsen the loss by acting on a wrong internal model.

The transfer is **very likely (80-90%)** to identify recovery failure modes that a checkpoint-only design misses. Evidence: the STPA Handbook explicitly analyzes unsafe control actions that are omitted, provided in the wrong context, mistimed/out of order, or applied for the wrong duration, and separately traces them to flawed process models and feedback; NASA's fault-management guidance requires detection, isolation, containment, and response and warns that a response arriving after time-to-criticality is useless ([STPA Handbook, pp. 35-46](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf); [NASA-HDBK-1002 Draft 2, pp. 47-51, 149-165](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/636372main_NASA-HDBK-1002_Draft.pdf)). The remaining uncertainty is empirical transfer to this harness, not the structural match. It would be settled by the kill-matrix experiment specified below.

The proposed implementation is deliberately small and rides existing hook/skill/filesystem buses. It is not a second scheduler and does not promise exactly-once execution. Its central rule is: **after an ambiguous interruption, isolate the old execution authority, reconstruct state from evidence, reconcile every uncertain side effect, and only then issue a fenced resume action**.

## Scope and method

This is an IDEATION lens. Per the unit instruction, I did not inspect the local harness, corpus, audit findings, or subject repository. Consequently, statements about what a generic checkpoint-oriented harness misses are design hypotheses, not claims about a particular existing implementation.

I used primary or authoritative sources where available:

* Leveson and Thomas's MIT STPA Handbook for the control-theoretic method and unsafe-control-action categories.
* NASA's Fault Management Handbook draft and current NASA Software Engineering Handbook guidance for containment, isolation, recovery, degraded states, timing, and fault-injection evidence.
* Reason's original BMJ article for the Swiss cheese model.
* Perrow's published abstract and a US Government technical report for normal-accident theory's tight-coupling/interactive-complexity diagnosis.
* NASA material on assurance cases for claims-argument-evidence structure and its limits.

NASA-HDBK-1002 is explicitly a 2012 **draft**, and NASA's current reliability page still labels it “In Progress”; I therefore use it as expert guidance, not as a binding or finalized standard ([NASA Reliability and Maintainability policy/guidance page](https://sma.nasa.gov/sma-disciplines/reliability-and-maintainability); [NASA-HDBK-1002 Draft 2](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/636372main_NASA-HDBK-1002_Draft.pdf)).

## What systems safety solved in its own field

### 1. FDIR separates detection, isolation, containment, response, and recovery

NASA defines failure detection as determining that something unexpected occurred; fault isolation as locating a hypothesized cause to a defined granularity; containment as preventing further propagation; and failure recovery as restoring functions needed for existing or redefined goals. Fault management covers preventing, detecting, isolating, diagnosing, responding to, and recovering from conditions that interfere with nominal operation ([NASA-HDBK-1002 Draft 2, definitions at pp. 17-19](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/636372main_NASA-HDBK-1002_Draft.pdf)).

This decomposition matters. Detection is not diagnosis; diagnosis is not proof that a retry is safe; recovery need not mean restoring the original full mission. NASA's current software guidance says that isolation should prevent propagation and recovery may reach either a safe state or a degraded-but-stable state. It also recommends testing recovery under worst-case operating conditions and using fault injection as evidence ([NASA Software Engineering Handbook, HR-37 Fault Recovery](https://swehb.nasa.gov/spaces/SWEHBVD/pages/171508207/HR-37%2B-%2BFault%2BRecovery)).

Fault management is also temporal. NASA defines “time to criticality” as the propagation time from a failure mode to its critical effect and observes that FDIR completing only after loss of function is partially effective at best and useless at worst ([NASA-HDBK-1002 Draft 2, pp. 19, 152-153](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/636372main_NASA-HDBK-1002_Draft.pdf)). It requires the architecture to show which failure conditions are identified, which recovery actions follow, and that multiple requirements do not interfere negatively (pp. 118-119).

This is more precise than “take checkpoints.” A checkpoint is stored state. FDIR is a decision discipline for deciding whether that state is trustworthy, what must first be isolated, and whether the correct outcome is retry, reconciliation, degradation, goal change, or stop.

### 2. STPA treats recovery itself as a potentially hazardous control action

STAMP models safety as a control problem. A controller maintains a process model, issues control actions through an actuator/control path, and updates its model from feedback. The STPA Handbook says mismatches between the controller's model and reality—caused by wrong, absent, delayed, or misinterpreted feedback—can produce unsafe actions ([STPA Handbook, pp. 22, 45-46](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf)). This is a particularly close match for a resuming agent whose “world model” is a checkpoint plus observations.

STPA systematically asks four questions of each control action:

1. Does not providing it cause a hazard?
2. Does providing it cause a hazard?
3. Can it be provided too early, too late, or in the wrong order?
4. Can it stop too soon or continue too long?

The method then develops causal loss scenarios: why the unsafe action would be selected, and why a nominal action might be improperly executed or not executed ([STPA Handbook, pp. 35-46](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf)). It explicitly includes human controllers and software controllers in the same control structure.

This contributes something ordinary retry taxonomies omit: “resume” is not intrinsically corrective. It can be unsafe because it was issued while the prior worker is still live, before external effects are observable, after a lease has changed hands, out of order relative to reconciliation, or for long enough to repeat a side effect.

### 3. Swiss cheese shifts attention from the last worker error to penetrated defenses

Reason's system model places successive engineered, human, and administrative defenses between hazard and loss. Each has weaknesses arising from active failures and latent conditions; loss occurs when weaknesses across layers align. The important analytical move is away from blaming the proximal operator and toward asking how the defenses were penetrated ([Reason, “Human error: models and management,” BMJ 2000](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/320/7237/768.full.pdf)).

Applied carefully, this says a worker death should not destroy useful work merely because several latent weaknesses align: no durable intent record, ambiguous tool completion, stale ownership, a non-idempotent side effect, and a resumer that trusts the checkpoint. It motivates independent containment, state-integrity, reconciliation, and resume-policy barriers.

It does **not** specify those barriers, prove their independence, or calculate their coverage. Reason describes the “holes” as shifting; later criticism notes that the metaphor is simplified and nonspecific about slices and holes ([Reason 2000](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/320/7237/768.full.pdf); [Larouzee and Le Coze, critical history of the model](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssci.2020.104660)). Therefore Swiss cheese is retained only as a review question—“which co-failures align?”—not as the implementation architecture.

### 4. Normal-accident theory says to reduce coupling and interactive complexity, not merely add recovery machinery

Perrow's normal-accident theory identifies interactive complexity and tight coupling as the combination that permits unexpected failure interactions to cascade. His later architectural recommendation is decomposition into units joined by monitored links and designing redundancy in from the start; he warns that safety add-ons can themselves be accident sources ([Perrow, “Organizing to Reduce the Vulnerabilities of Complexity”](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-5973.00108)). A US Government technical report summarizes the same core mechanism: nonlinear, unforeseen interactions plus tight coupling allow multiple failures to cascade ([NIOSH report discussing Normal Accident Theory, Chapter 3](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/8538/cdc_8538_DS1.pdf)).

For recovery, the lesson is negative but valuable: do not create a second durable workflow runtime whose state must remain consistent with the agent runtime, tool runtime, Git state, and external services. More recovery machinery can create more ambiguous cross-system states. Insert slack at the interruption boundary: fence first, observe, reconcile, and permit a human/degraded stop instead of forcing immediate continuation.

### 5. Assurance cases make the recovery claim and its evidence auditable

An assurance case decomposes a top-level claim into subclaims until they are supported by observed or measured evidence. NASA emphasizes that the argument links claims to evidence about design and implementation; it is not just a folder of tests ([NASA/CR-2015-218802, pp. 1-2](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160000772/downloads/20160000772.pdf)). NASA also describes assurance reasoning as defeasible: new information can defeat a previously reasonable inference, so assumptions and counterarguments matter ([Chaki et al., “Assurance Cases for Proofs as Evidence”](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150004720/downloads/20150004720.pdf)).

For this target, the useful artifact is a very small claim tree: recovery preserves authority uniqueness, does not silently duplicate uncertain effects, restores only evidence-supported state, and either makes useful progress or enters an explicit degraded stop. Evidence is generated by kill-point tests, not by prose asserting that checkpoints exist.

## Concrete translation: the recovery envelope

### UNVERIFIED — Proposal A: Recovery contract and evidence journal — NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)

Add a compact append-only recovery record associated with each task. It is an evidence journal, not a general event-sourced workflow engine. Each record should be atomic and checksummed and contain only recovery-critical facts:

* stable `task_id`, objective/owner-intent digest, workspace identity, repository revision, and recovery generation;
* current owner/lease identifier and last heartbeat observation;
* last verified invariant and the artifact hashes supporting it;
* each consequential action's intent, idempotency/reconciliation key, target, issue time, observed result, and one of `planned | issued-unknown | verified-applied | verified-not-applied | compensated | manual`;
* workspace/external-resource dirtiness known at the checkpoint;
* next safe action plus the preconditions and observations that justify it;
* explicit terminal marker: `completed`, `degraded-stop`, or `abandoned`.

The journal must distinguish “command was issued” from “effect was observed.” A success response is evidence, but for consequential external effects the stronger completion evidence is a read-after-write observation or domain receipt. On restart, an `issued-unknown` action is never blindly replayed.

It is **likely (55-80%)** that this record can remain under roughly 200 implementation lines if implemented as one hook plus a resume skill using the harness's existing artifact store. Evidence: the data model and transition rules are small, and NASA's FDIR separation is conceptual rather than runtime-heavy. This is **UNVERIFIED** for the target harness; the settling experiment is to implement a throwaway hook that atomically appends and validates 10,000 records across forced process exits, then count corrupt/torn records and source lines. If existing hooks cannot intercept every consequential action, the recommendation must be narrowed rather than expanded into a new daemon.

What it catches that a checkpoint alone misses:

* a tool call dispatched immediately before death whose effect may have occurred;
* a checkpoint whose prose says “done” without an evidence-bearing completion observation;
* resumption against a different worktree, revision, objective, or external target;
* lost ordering between artifact creation, validation, and publication;
* corruption or partial write of the recovery record itself.

### UNVERIFIED — Proposal B: Authority fencing and fault-containment region — NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)

Treat one task generation as a fault-containment region. A supervisor grants a monotonically increasing generation/fencing token. Every side-effecting tool invocation carries that token; a pre-tool hook rejects a token older than the task's current generation. A resumer first advances the generation and only then reconciles state. The old worker may still compute or write its private scratch area, but it cannot cross the consequential side-effect boundary.

Do not claim the worker is dead from one timeout. The death declaration requires two mechanically independent signals, for example (a) expired heartbeat/lease and (b) an independently sourced session/worker status. The second detector must first demonstrate that it can find a known-live control worker. If the second signal is unavailable after machine loss, classify the worker state as `ambiguous`, advance the fence to contain it, and do not assert death. This preserves the distinction between safe containment and epistemic certainty.

This is the direct translation of NASA fault containment: locate/isolate to the smallest practical region before attempting recovery. It is **very likely (80-90%)** to prevent concurrent old/new workers from duplicating harness-mediated effects if all such effects pass the fencing hook. Evidence: a monotonically ordered fence makes old authority mechanically distinguishable, and NASA requires isolation before recovery for catastrophic faults ([NASA Software Engineering Handbook, HR-37](https://swehb.nasa.gov/spaces/SWEHBVD/pages/171508207/HR-37%2B-%2BFault%2BRecovery)). The coverage premise is **UNVERIFIED**; the settling experiment is a tool-path inventory plus a race test in which the old worker is paused before each side-effect boundary, the generation advances, and the old worker is released. Any unfenced side-effect path invalidates the broad claim and must be listed as outside the containment region.

What it catches that heartbeats/checkpoints miss:

* a slow or partitioned worker mistaken for a dead one;
* an old worker waking after a replacement begins;
* two resumers racing for the same task;
* stale queued tool actions arriving after ownership changes.

### UNVERIFIED — Proposal C: STPA-derived resume gate — NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)

Implement `RESUME` as an explicit control action with four STPA gates:

| STPA category | Agent-recovery unsafe control action | Concrete constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Not provided | No resumer is started even though the worker is unavailable and recoverable work exists. | Escalate an un-terminated stale task to recovery review; never silently abandon it. |
| Provided when unsafe | Resume is issued while old authority remains valid, or against the wrong objective/workspace/target. | Fence first; match task, objective, workspace, and target identity before mutation. |
| Too early / too late / out of order | Resume precedes external-effect reconciliation; is delayed until credentials, leases, or temporary artifacts expire; or publishes before validation. | Evaluate time-to-criticality; reconcile `issued-unknown`; restore dependency order; choose degraded stop when a deadline has passed. |
| Stopped too soon / applied too long | Recovery stops after reconstructing state but before producing a useful verified handoff; or loops retries and repeats effects indefinitely. | Require a terminal marker and evidence; bound attempts; after the bound, change goal to diagnosis/manual handoff rather than “try again.” |

The resumer's process model must be explicit enough to compare checkpoint beliefs with reality: task objective, authority generation, workspace/revision, file hashes, pending side effects, external receipts, tool availability, credentials/leases, deadlines, and already-published artifacts. For each mismatch the policy chooses `re-observe`, `reconcile`, `compensate`, `degrade`, or `manual`; it does not synthesize certainty.

This gate is **very likely (80-90%)** to reveal timing/order/context recovery hazards during design review because those are the four mandatory STPA categories, supported by the handbook's causal analysis of missing/delayed/out-of-order feedback ([STPA Handbook, pp. 35-46](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf)). It is **UNVERIFIED** whether a compact policy will materially improve this harness's recovery success; the settling experiment is to label historical or synthetic interrupted tasks with the true external state, compare checkpoint-only decisions with gated decisions, and measure unsafe duplicate actions, false stops, and time to useful progress.

### UNVERIFIED — Proposal D: Reconciliation classes and degraded recovery — NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only)

Give every consequential tool/action class a recovery policy in configuration:

* `replay-safe`: deterministic read or idempotent operation with validated key;
* `verify-before-repeat`: mutation with an authoritative read-back/receipt probe;
* `compensatable`: mutation with an explicit, separately authorized compensation path;
* `manual-on-ambiguity`: irreversible, externally visible, destructive, monetary, credential, or human-communication action with no authoritative reconciliation.

The classification is a recovery constraint, not blanket authorization to compensate or mutate. A resumed task inherits the original scope; it cannot compensate outside that scope without new authority. If required evidence is unavailable, the safe result is `degraded-stop` with the objective, confirmed state, uncertain effects, and next diagnostic action—not fabricated completion.

It is **likely (55-80%)** that this classification will reduce unsafe retries without blocking most read-heavy engineering work. Evidence: it directly implements FDIR's separation of response strategies and NASA's allowance for degraded-but-stable recovery. The workload distribution and tool semantics are **UNVERIFIED**; the settling experiment is to classify a stratified sample of 200 real tool calls, have two reviewers independently assess replay safety, adjudicate disagreements, and replay only in a disposable environment to measure false-safe and false-manual rates.

### UNVERIFIED — Proposal E: Minimal recovery assurance case and kill matrix — NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus)

Maintain the following machine-readable claim tree beside recovery tests:

* **C0:** A single interruption cannot cause silent loss of confirmed useful work or an unrecognized duplicate consequential effect.
  * **C1 authority:** at most one generation can cross each fenced effect boundary.
  * **C2 integrity:** the latest accepted recovery record is atomic, checksummed, and bound to task/objective/workspace identity.
  * **C3 epistemics:** issued-but-unobserved effects remain explicit and are reconciled or handed off; they are never promoted to “done” by inference alone.
  * **C4 progress:** after recovery, the task either reaches a verified next milestone or emits a degraded-stop artifact with sufficient evidence for another controller/human.
  * **C5 containment independence:** the state store, authority fence, and completion observation do not share a single failure mode claimed to be covered.

Evidence comes from a kill matrix, not unit tests of serialization alone. Kill the worker at each boundary: before intent record, after intent/before issue, during issue, after effect/before response, after response/before observation, after observation/before checkpoint, during checkpoint write, during fencing, and during resumer reconciliation. Repeat at worker-process, session, and machine/container termination levels. Inject delayed responses, stale queued calls, corrupt/truncated final records, changed worktree, expired credential/lease, external service unavailability, and two simultaneous resumers. For every cell, assert C1-C4 and record recovery latency and lost/duplicated-effect counts.

The assurance case is defeasible: tool semantics, external APIs, and storage guarantees are named assumptions; a change invalidates the dependent evidence until retested. The test runner should produce links/hashes to observations rather than a self-certified Boolean.

It is **very likely (80-90%)** that this matrix will find defects missed by graceful-shutdown and restart tests. Evidence: NASA explicitly calls for fault injection including disconnections, resets, redundancy/failover, boundary/overload conditions, and measurement of detection/isolation/recovery time ([NASA Software Engineering Handbook, HR-37](https://swehb.nasa.gov/spaces/SWEHBVD/pages/171508207/HR-37%2B-%2BFault%2BRecovery)); NASA's own lessons say anomalous behavior, power-on reset, and all redundancy combinations require testing ([NASA-HDBK-1002 Draft 2, pp. 191-198](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/636372main_NASA-HDBK-1002_Draft.pdf)). Exact defect yield is **UNVERIFIED**; running the matrix and publishing per-cell counterexamples settles it.

## End-to-end recovery protocol

The five proposals form this deliberately short control loop:

1. **Record intent.** Before a consequential action, append identity, preconditions, target, policy class, and reconciliation key.
2. **Issue under authority.** The side-effect hook validates the current generation; stale generations fail closed at that boundary.
3. **Observe completion.** Append the tool result and, where consequential, an independent domain observation or receipt. Lack of response remains `issued-unknown`.
4. **Detect interruption.** Use stale heartbeat plus an independently validated worker/session detector. If only one exists, record `ambiguous`, not `dead`.
5. **Contain.** Advance the generation/fence before authorizing any resumer mutation.
6. **Reconstruct the process model.** Load the last valid journal prefix, then re-observe task identity, workspace/revision, artifacts, external receipts, deadlines, and capabilities.
7. **Reconcile uncertainty.** Apply the configured action policy. Never replay `manual-on-ambiguity`; never infer external success solely from intended local state.
8. **Apply the STPA gate.** Check context, ordering, timing/deadline, and termination conditions for `RESUME`.
9. **Continue or degrade.** Proceed from the last evidence-supported milestone, or emit `degraded-stop` containing confirmed facts, uncertain effects, failed probes, and the smallest next action needing authority.
10. **Close with evidence.** Append a terminal marker only after milestone/output verification; link the relevant kill-matrix assurance evidence for the mechanism version.

## Failure modes this catches beyond “restart from the latest checkpoint”

1. **The zombie predecessor:** the first worker was partitioned, not dead, and later resumes issuing actions. Fencing contains it without needing a metaphysically certain death diagnosis.
2. **The Schrödinger mutation:** the machine died after the external service applied a mutation but before the response was stored. `issued-unknown` forces receipt/read-back reconciliation rather than retry.
3. **The truthful but stale checkpoint:** the checkpoint accurately described the world when written, but another actor changed the repository, issue, branch, calendar, mailbox, or deployment afterward. The resumer compares model to current feedback.
4. **The wrong-context recovery:** the same task identifier now points at a different objective, workspace, revision, account, or target. Identity preconditions block mutation.
5. **The temporally unsafe recovery:** restoring work is technically possible but a lease/credential/deadline has expired, or a late publish would now be harmful. Time-to-criticality selects degradation or renewed authority.
6. **The recovery deadly embrace:** worker A waits for a result from B while B's recovery waits for A's state, or retries re-trigger each other's monitors. Bounded recovery and explicit goal change terminate the loop. NASA's lesson database excerpt warns that “try again” responses may be undesirable unless a recovery mode exists ([NASA-HDBK-1002 Draft 2, pp. 197-198](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/636372main_NASA-HDBK-1002_Draft.pdf)).
7. **Recovery mechanism failure:** corrupt last record, unavailable state store, or broken recovery hook. Checksum/prefix validation plus degraded stop exposes the failure; it does not silently treat the recovery layer as infallible.
8. **False completion:** narrative state claims work is done but no result observation supports it. The assurance claim requires evidence-bearing closure.
9. **Unsafe compensation:** a resumer attempts to undo an effect outside original authority or after another actor depended on it. Policy classification and manual handoff stop automatic compensation.
10. **Layer correlation:** journal and checkpoint share storage, heartbeat and liveness share one supervisor, or primary and “backup” calls share credentials/network. C5 requires the case to name these common modes rather than count correlated layers as independent defenses.

## Adversarial transfer analysis

### Why the core analogy transfers

The structural homomorphism is strong:

| Systems-safety structure | Failure-recovery structure |
|---|---|
| Controller | supervisor/resumer |
| Controlled process | task, workspace, tools, and external effects |
| Process model | checkpoint plus reconstructed task/world state |
| Sensor/feedback | heartbeats, tool results, file hashes, receipts, read-back probes |
| Control action | fence, retry, reconcile, compensate, resume, stop |
| Unsafe control action | duplicated mutation, resumed wrong objective, missed deadline, endless retry, false completion |
| Fault-containment region | task generation and its fenced side-effect boundary |
| Safe/degraded state | explicit evidence-bearing handoff without further ambiguous mutation |

Both domains have partial observability, stale feedback, latent conditions, time-dependent response, and recovery actions capable of causing loss. STPA was explicitly designed to include software and human controllers and complex interactions rather than requiring a simple component-failure chain ([STPA Handbook, introduction and pp. 22, 35-46](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf)). The match is therefore not merely linguistic.

### Where the analogy breaks

1. **Agent task utility is not physical safety.** A spacecraft can often define a conservative safe mode; an open-ended coding/research task may have no monotonic safe state. `degraded-stop` protects authority and epistemic integrity, but may sacrifice progress. The mechanism must not pretend that “no mutation” satisfies the owner's goal.
2. **The controlled process includes external services outside the harness boundary.** The harness may be unable to fence an already accepted API request or obtain authoritative receipts. Containment applies only at named interception points.
3. **Natural-language objectives drift.** A physical controller normally has engineered state variables and constraints. Owner intent may be underspecified or revised across sessions; a digest detects change but cannot prove semantic equivalence.
4. **Fault independence is hard to establish.** Checkpoint, journal, heartbeat, and fencing may all share a filesystem, process, credential, or host. Swiss-cheese rhetoric can easily count correlated mechanisms as layers.
5. **Exactly-once external effects are generally not constructible by local journaling.** A crash between remote commit and local receipt remains ambiguous unless the remote domain supplies an idempotency key or authoritative query. The honest outcomes are reconciliation or manual-on-ambiguity.
6. **STPA produces scenarios, not probabilities or automatic completeness.** Quality depends on the control structure, hazards, and analyst coverage. The four UCA categories organize search; they do not prove every recovery loss scenario was found.
7. **Safety-case prose can become ceremonial.** A claims tree does not make its premises true. Only linked observations, fault injection, configuration control, and explicit defeaters make the recovery claim credible.
8. **Machine death may destroy both worker and evidence store.** Local atomic append protects against torn writes, not shared-host loss. A remote/durable store might improve this but risks becoming the prohibited second runtime. The design should first use whatever durable artifact channel is already native to the harness and state its failure boundary.

These breaks narrow the conclusion: this proposal offers **at-most-one-current-authority at instrumented boundaries and evidence-preserving recovery**, not exactly-once global execution, perfect progress, or proof of semantic intent preservation.

## Rejected or limited borrows

### Swiss cheese as “add many guardrails” — rejected

This would violate both the model's limits and the anti-overfit gate. More layers are not automatically independent; they can add shared failure modes and interactive complexity. Use the metaphor only to enumerate active plus latent co-failures and to demand independence evidence.

### Full STAMP organizational control hierarchy — not proposed

A complete management/regulator/operations model is disproportionate for task resumption and would create process overhead without direct runtime containment. Use only the control-loop/UCA/scenario machinery on the recovery boundary.

### Quantitative risk score for every resume — not proposed

The sources do not provide calibrated probabilities for agent recovery, and invented scores would launder uncertainty. Use categorical action semantics, explicit deadlines/time-to-criticality, and measured fault-injection outcomes.

### Second orchestration or durable-execution runtime — explicitly rejected

This is a previously rejected component under the unit's anti-overfit gate. Normal-accident theory gives an additional reason: synchronizing two runtimes increases coupling and ambiguous interaction paths. The proposed journal is passive evidence and the fencing logic rides the existing tool boundary; neither schedules workflows.

### Vector database / knowledge graph memory — explicitly rejected

Recovery needs a small authoritative record of intent, authority, effects, and evidence. Semantic retrieval does not resolve whether a specific external mutation committed and adds an additional consistency boundary.

### Automatic compensation for all effects — rejected

Compensation is another mutation, may be irreversible, may violate current authority, and may race with downstream users. It is permitted only for action classes with an explicitly authorized and tested compensation contract.

## Anti-overfit gate result

All proposals ride existing hooks, skills, configuration, and artifact storage. No daemon, service, trained critic, vector memory, observability SaaS, guardrail framework, or durable-execution engine is introduced. The minimum viable slice is one append-only recovery hook, one fencing check on existing side-effect hooks, one resume skill implementing the STPA gate, and one kill-matrix test script. Each component must remain independently removable.

The minimal design is **likely (55-80%)** to outperform a larger recovery orchestrator on net reliability for this use case because it adds fewer coupled states while targeting the specific ambiguity window. Evidence: Perrow recommends decomposition through monitored links and warns about add-on safety components; NASA warns that recovery mechanisms can be late, interact negatively, and require system-level anomalous testing ([Perrow 1999/2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-5973.00108); [NASA-HDBK-1002 Draft 2, pp. 118-119, 152-165, 191-198](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/636372main_NASA-HDBK-1002_Draft.pdf)). Comparative superiority is **UNVERIFIED**; settle it by running the same kill matrix against checkpoint-only, this recovery envelope, and any proposed larger runtime, then compare unsafe duplicates, confirmed-work loss, false stops, latency, and mechanism-induced failures.

## Acceptance and falsification experiments

The proposal should be rejected or narrowed if any of these tests fail:

1. **Interception completeness:** enumerate all consequential action paths using one static route inventory and one dynamic canary run. First prove each detector finds a known-present route. If a material path bypasses fencing, the containment claim excludes it.
2. **Dual liveness:** demonstrate the heartbeat detector on a known-live and known-stopped worker; independently demonstrate the session/process detector on the same controls. Partition a live worker and ensure the system records ambiguity and fences it rather than claiming death from the heartbeat alone.
3. **Journal atomicity:** kill during every byte/record boundary and verify that recovery accepts exactly a checksummed prefix, never fabricated suffix state. Repeat with disk-full/read-only and shared-store-unavailable conditions.
4. **Ambiguous-effect reconciliation:** inject remote commit followed by connection loss. For every tool policy, verify idempotent read-back, explicit uncertainty, or manual stop; any blind retry is a failure.
5. **Fence race:** pause old worker before every side-effect entry, advance generation, start resumer, release old worker. Zero stale-generation crossings is required for instrumented boundaries.
6. **Identity mismatch:** alter objective, worktree, revision, account, target, and recovery record independently. Each must prevent mutation until explicitly reconciled.
7. **STPA timing/order:** delay receipts, expire credentials/leases, reorder queued responses, and pass deadlines. Verify the policy chooses renew/reconcile/degrade rather than nominal resume.
8. **Recovery-of-recovery:** kill during fence acquisition, reconciliation, degraded-stop writing, and terminal marking. A subsequent resumer must not multiply authority or erase uncertainty.
9. **Common-cause challenge:** remove the shared host, filesystem, network, credential, and supervisor one at a time. The assurance case must accurately lose the claims that depend on each; it must not report all layers healthy through one correlated detector.
10. **Useful-progress metric:** measure confirmed milestones retained, time to first useful verified action, duplicate consequential effects, false completions, false stops, human interventions, and new failures introduced by recovery. A design that is safe only because it always stops fails C4.

Minimum comparative arms: no recovery, latest-checkpoint retry, recovery envelope, and—only if another unit advocates it—the larger alternative. Use identical kill seeds and external-service simulators. Report per-cell results, not a single blended score that hides catastrophic cells.

## Confidence ledger

* **Very likely (80-90%)**: STPA's four unsafe-control-action categories plus flawed-process-model analysis will expose meaningful resume hazards beyond a checkpoint/retry checklist. Evidence: direct structural mapping and the method's explicit coverage of omission, provision, timing/order, duration, and feedback failures ([STPA Handbook](https://psas.scripts.mit.edu/home/get_file.php?name=STPA_handbook.pdf)). Settling experiment: blinded comparison on labeled interruption scenarios.
* **Very likely (80-90%)**: fencing prevents stale-worker effects at every correctly instrumented boundary. Evidence: monotonic generations mechanically distinguish stale authority; uncertainty is whether instrumentation is complete. Settling experiment: interception inventory plus fence-race matrix.
* **Likely (55-80%)**: evidence journaling plus reconciliation reduces both lost confirmed work and duplicate ambiguous effects compared with blind checkpoint retry. Evidence: it preserves the distinction between issued and observed and selects action by semantics. Settling experiment: remote-commit/connection-loss injection across real tool classes.
* **Likely (55-80%)**: the core can remain a set of BUILD-SMALL components rather than becoming a runtime. Evidence: the protocol stores facts and gates existing actions; it does not schedule. Settling experiment: working prototype with source-line and dependency count.
* **Roughly even (45-55%)**: independent read-after-write evidence will exist for most consequential external tools. Evidence: many APIs offer receipts/query endpoints, but arbitrary shell, messages, and third-party tools may not. Settling experiment: classify and test a stratified sample of 200 real calls.
* **Unlikely (20-45%)**: Swiss cheese alone will generate actionable recovery requirements. Evidence: the original is a generic barrier metaphor and later literature documents its ambiguity; FDIR/STPA supplies the missing operational detail ([Reason 2000](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/320/7237/768.full.pdf); [Larouzee and Le Coze 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssci.2020.104660)).
* **Very unlikely (5-20%)**: local journaling can guarantee exactly-once effects across arbitrary external systems. Evidence: remote commit can occur before local receipt, leaving an indistinguishable ambiguous state without remote idempotency/query support. Settling experiment: two-generals-style commit/response-loss injection against each API; a counterexample is sufficient to refute the general claim.
* **Almost certainly not (1-5%)**: adding a second general orchestration runtime is necessary for the proposed safety benefit. Evidence: detection, fencing, evidence capture, reconciliation, and fault injection can all ride the existing bus; Perrow's analysis cautions against complexity-increasing add-ons. Settling experiment: implement the minimal slice and compare kill-matrix coverage with any claimed missing capability.

## Gaps

* No local harness inspection was performed, by explicit task instruction. Therefore existing native checkpoint, hook, fencing, receipt, or resume facilities are unknown. Any claim that a named facility is absent would be unsupported and is intentionally not made.
* UNVERIFIED: whether every consequential target-harness tool traverses a common pre-tool hook. Experiment: static route inventory plus dynamic canary coverage, each validated on a known-present route.
* UNVERIFIED: whether the existing artifact channel survives machine loss independently of the worker host. Experiment: persist a canary, terminate the machine/container, and read it from a separately provisioned recovery session; repeat with the storage service unavailable.
* UNVERIFIED: empirical frequency of ambiguous external effects versus clean worker deaths. Experiment: instrument issue/response/observation boundaries over representative work, without relying on the corpus hooks section identified by the parent brief as broken.
* UNVERIFIED: acceptable recovery latency and what “useful work” means across task types. Experiment: owner-defined milestone rubric applied to a stratified set of interrupted coding, research, browser, and communication tasks.
* The direct NASA fault-management handbook source is a draft. Its definitions and design reasoning are corroborated in current NASA Software Engineering Handbook pages, but it should not be cited as a finalized normative standard.
