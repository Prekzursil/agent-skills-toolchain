# X123 — Cross-domain borrow: forensic-science contamination control, blinding, and proficiency testing for orchestration parallelism

## Scope and result

This is an ideation-lens unit. I did not inspect or make claims about the local harness or repository. I used external forensic-science sources to answer one question: can forensic contamination control, blind or double-blind protocols, and proficiency testing help an agent harness decide how many parallel workers to run and when another worker stops helping?

**Result: the borrow transfers, but only as an empirical calibration method—not as a theorem that “more independent examiners means more accuracy.”** The useful mechanism is a **blind, contamination-controlled, ground-truthed proficiency curve**: keep worker judgments sealed; insert representative tasks whose truth is known to the evaluator but hidden from workers; score correct, incorrect, inconclusive, and missing responses; and estimate the marginal *unique correct yield* and correlated-error cost of worker 2, 3, 4, and so on. Then use the smallest worker count whose next-worker upper confidence bound is below a configured value/cost threshold.

It is **very likely (80-90%)** that this structural borrow is valid for benchmarkable agent tasks, because both fields involve repeated judgments over ambiguous evidence, susceptibility to contextual anchoring, and measurable inter-examiner/worker disagreement; the evidence is the forensic black-box, blind-verification, and proficiency-testing literature cited below. It is **unlikely (20-45%)** that one curve will generalize across open-ended software work, because forensic studies themselves show strong item and examiner effects and warn that aggregate error rates depend on the tested conditions ([NIST handwriting foundation review](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.IR.8282); [Luby and Kadane on black-box missingness and dependence](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10041348/)). Therefore the output must be a curve by task class and orchestration pattern, not a global magic number.

## What forensic science has actually solved

Forensic science has not solved “optimal team size.” It has solved three narrower experimental-control problems that are prerequisites to measuring whether an additional opinion is useful.

### 1. Analytical contamination control: isolate, run controls in parallel, and trace the source

Forensic DNA quality systems do not infer purity because multiple samples agree. They physically or temporally separate contamination-sensitive stages, run controls alongside evidence, and use known reference or elimination profiles to detect introduced material. The FBI Quality Assurance Standards define amplification blanks and reagent blanks specifically to detect contamination, require separation of pre-amplification work from amplified products, require documented evidence control, and require positive/negative controls and reagent blanks in casework ([FBI Quality Assurance Standards](https://archives.fbi.gov/archives/about-us/lab/forensic-science-communications/fsc/july2000/quality-assurance-standards-for-forensic-dna-testing-laboratories)). An OSAC proposed standard defines controls as known positive and negative samples run in parallel with evidence and defines elimination databases as profiles from people or recognized contaminants used to detect contamination ([NIST/OSAC contamination standard](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/2020/05/08/136%20Standards%20for%20Prevention%20Monitoring%20and%20Mitigation%20of%20DNA%20Contamination_OSAC%20PROPOSED.pdf)).

In its own field, the mechanism has four parts:

1. Separation prevents contamination where feasible.
2. A blank travels through the process without the target material, so a positive result reveals process contamination rather than evidence.
3. A known positive demonstrates that the process can detect a known-present signal.
4. Elimination profiles help attribute contamination instead of merely announcing disagreement.

The important orchestration lesson is not the DNA metaphor. It is the experimental logic: **agreement is not evidence of independence; independence needs a contamination detector and known controls.**

### 2. Blind verification and sequential unmasking: freeze an opinion before exposing it to another opinion

NIST's forensic lexicon defines blind verification as verification in which subsequent examiners have no knowledge of another examiner's decisions, conclusions, or supporting observations ([NIST, “blind verification”](https://www.nist.gov/glossary-term/41466)). The large latent-fingerprint black-box study by Ulery et al. found that independent examination of the same comparisons—analogous to blind verification—detected all false-positive errors and most false-negative errors *within that experiment* ([Ulery et al., 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3093498/)). This is not proof that blind verification always improves casework, but it is direct evidence that a sealed second judgment can expose errors that a single judgment misses.

Linear Sequential Unmasking–Expanded (LSU-E) controls not only another examiner's conclusion but also the order of contextual information. It says to begin from raw evidence, document the initial impression, and only then expose the examiner to other information ordered by biasing power, objectivity, and relevance; it also requires documenting opinion changes ([Dror and Kukucka, LSU-E](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8385162/)). This matters because an “independent” verifier who sees the first conclusion is not independent in the relevant cognitive sense. A recent PNAS review reports that, in one firearm casework study, the odds of disagreement were five times higher under blind than nonblind verification, while explicitly noting that unknown ground truth prevented that study from determining which decision was accurate ([Koehler et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2528192123)).

In its own field, blind verification measures reproducibility: can a second examiner reach a conclusion without being anchored by the first? It does not by itself measure accuracy when ground truth is unknown. That distinction must survive the translation.

### 3. Blind proficiency testing and black-box studies: test normal-looking work against hidden ground truth

NIJ defines a forensic DNA proficiency test as biological samples used to assess an analyst's continuing competence and the laboratory's ability to produce accurate results; in a blind test, analysts do not know they are being tested ([NIJ proficiency-testing module](https://nij.ojp.gov/nij-hosted-online-training-courses/principles-forensic-dna-officers-court/05-assuring-quality-dna-testing/introduction-quality-assurance/proficiency-testing)). Peterson and Gaensslen's NIJ-funded work found that blind testing exercises more of the laboratory system, while open proficiency tests mainly exercise analytical accuracy ([OJP, model external DNA proficiency testing](https://www.ojp.gov/library/publications/developing-criteria-model-external-dna-proficiency-testing-final-report)). A later operational report describes inserting blind tests as real casework to test the whole system from evidence receipt through report release, while also documenting that doing this convincingly is difficult ([Koehler et al., blind proficiency program](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31922611/)).

Black-box studies extend the idea across many examiners and many known-source comparisons. They estimate accuracy, repeatability (same examiner, same item), and reproducibility (different examiners, same item) without requiring the examiner's internal reasoning to be objectively specified. NIST's digital-forensics study applied mock examinations to practitioners and reported the distribution of correct, wrong, and skipped answers rather than claiming that a single score described every failure mode ([NISTIR 8412](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.IR.8412)).

This literature also supplies the warnings that make the borrow useful rather than decorative:

- Black-box observations are not automatically independent: examiners answer multiple items and multiple examiners answer the same items. Statistical uncertainty must cluster by item and examiner rather than pretending that every response is an independent trial ([Luby and Kadane](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10041348/)).
- “Inconclusive,” “no value,” skips, and nonresponse can dominate results. Dropping them can make error rates look artificially small; in one palm-print dataset discussed by Luby and Kadane, more than half of comparison decisions become unobserved under some treatments ([Luby and Kadane](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10041348/)).
- Knowing that one is being tested can alter effort and decision thresholds. Blind proficiency tests are intended to reduce that Hawthorne-like distortion, but convincing disguised tests are uncommon and operationally difficult ([Hofmann et al., Hawthorne effect in firearm/toolmark studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12223340/); [Koehler et al., blind proficiency program](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31922611/)).
- A single error rate is not sufficient. NIST's analysis of the Bullet Black Box study emphasizes a multidimensional trade-off among false identifications, false exclusions, correct decisions, and inconclusive/no-value decisions; some examiners with no errors were also less effective because they made fewer correct decisions and more inconclusive calls ([NIST, Implications of the Bullet Black Box Study](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=960209)).

## The concrete translation

### Proposal A — UNVERIFIED until Experiment E1: sealed worker reports

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only).**

For every parallel *replication* branch, give each worker the same task evidence but do not reveal other workers' drafts, conclusions, confidence, tool transcripts, status, or worker identity until that worker has emitted a signed/frozen report. The report schema should include: atomic claims or candidate changes; evidence references; abstentions; uncertainties; elapsed time; token/tool cost; and a digest of the input evidence version. Randomize report order and hide worker identity from the first-pass synthesizer.

This is blind verification translated literally. It is not appropriate for cooperative decomposition branches that must share state; those must be labeled “collaborative” and excluded from independence claims.

The premise that sealing materially changes same-model worker errors is **UNVERIFIED; Experiment E1 settles it by randomly assigning matched tasks to sealed versus cross-visible workers and comparing ground-truthed accuracy, pairwise wrong-answer overlap, and marginal unique correct findings.** It is **very likely (80-90%)** that sealing will reduce direct anchoring and answer copying, because forensic decisions demonstrably change when verifiers see prior conclusions and LSU-E documents the mechanism of order/context effects. It is only **likely (55-80%)** that sealing will improve final agent accuracy, because same-model workers retain shared training, prompt, retrieval, and tool biases even without cross-talk.

What it catches that naive parallelism misses:

- apparent “consensus” created by one worker's conclusion contaminating later workers;
- status or prestige effects caused by role labels or report ordering;
- revisions that occur only after another report is unmasked;
- branches that were collaborative copies but are counted as independent confirmations.

### Proposal B — UNVERIFIED until Experiment E2: a blind parallelism proficiency curve

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).**

Add a small calibration runner on the existing orchestration bus. It should not schedule production work autonomously. It should replay a versioned fixture manifest, collect sealed JSON reports, score them, and write a compact lookup table keyed by task class and orchestration pattern.

For each representative task fixture:

1. Define ground-truth atomic findings, acceptable abstentions, forbidden false claims, and value weights before running workers. Include known-positive tasks, known-negative tasks, ambiguous tasks where abstention is correct, and “contamination challenge” tasks containing an attractive but wrong anchor.
2. Make fixtures resemble normal work. The worker must not receive a “benchmark” label. Separate roles so the packager knows the fixture truth, the dispatcher knows only task routing, workers know neither test status nor truth, and the first-pass scorer sees de-identified reports. This is double-blind in functional roles, not a claim that no human or component knows the truth.
3. Randomize the assigned worker counts, for example `k = 1, 2, 3, 4, 6`, and randomize model/role order. Run enough tasks in each class that uncertainty is reported rather than hidden.
4. Preserve every timeout, tool failure, skip, abstention, and schema failure as an outcome. Never drop a worker because it produced no conclusive report.
5. Score at least five dimensions: weighted correct-union yield, weighted false-claim cost, abstention/missing rate, wall-clock and token/tool cost, and pairwise overlap of *wrong* atomic claims. Lexical diversity is not an independence measure.
6. Compute the incremental value of worker `k`: the change in final verified utility from `k-1` to `k`, including synthesis. Cluster bootstrap intervals by task fixture, because workers on the same fixture are dependent observations.
7. Compare three patterns separately: sealed replicas; specialist decomposition; and primary-plus-blind-verifier. Do not average them into one “parallel agents” result.
8. Publish a curve and its applicability envelope: task class, task difficulty bins, model/version, prompt version, tool access, evidence version, orchestration pattern, and date.

The premise that a small representative fixture suite predicts live marginal yield is **UNVERIFIED; Experiment E2 settles it by pre-registering the curve on a calibration set and testing its predicted stop points on a disjoint, time-later live-shadow set with adjudicated outcomes.** It is **likely (55-80%)** that a class-specific curve will identify some wasteful high-`k` regimes, because forensic black-box studies repeatedly find item/examiner variability and non-independent trials, while the proposed experiment observes agent cost directly. It is **roughly even (45-55%)** that the curve will remain calibrated after a model, system-prompt, or tool change, because those changes can alter both worker competence and error correlation; evidence for stability must come from E2 reruns, not from the analogy.

The runner catches:

- **correlated wrong consensus**: three workers independently repeat the same false claim, yielding high vote agreement but near-zero independent information;
- **diminishing unique yield**: worker 4 adds prose or duplicate facts but no new verified atom;
- **false economy**: more workers increase synthesizer load and introduce false claims even if raw recall rises;
- **outlier-worker concentration**: a role/model produces a disproportionate share of false claims or timeouts;
- **difficulty masking**: easy tasks make high `k` look harmless while difficult tasks drive the actual marginal value;
- **selective attrition**: failed or inconclusive branches disappear from success-only summaries;
- **Hawthorne/benchmark distortion**: declared benchmark performance differs from disguised live-shaped proficiency trials;
- **verification theater**: a nonblind verifier agrees much more often but detects fewer seeded errors than a blind verifier;
- **synthesis loss**: the union of reports contains the right answer, but the synthesizer drops it or selects a correlated error.

### Proposal C — UNVERIFIED until Experiment E3: a conservative marginal stop gate

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: CONFIG (settings/JSON only).**

Store the E2 output as a small static policy table. For a calibrated task class, choose the smallest `k` such that either:

- the one-sided upper confidence bound on the next worker's expected net utility is below a configured threshold for two adjacent `k` values; or
- expected false-claim and synthesis cost exceeds expected new correct yield.

“Net utility” must be owner-configured: weighted correct atoms minus weighted false atoms minus token/tool/time cost. The stop policy must expose uncertainty and fall back to an explicit conservative default when the task class or current model/tool version is outside the calibration envelope. It must not extrapolate from `k=4` to `k=20`.

The premise that a two-point confidence-bound gate is an acceptable production decision rule is **UNVERIFIED; Experiment E3 settles it with an A/B shadow evaluation against fixed `k` policies, measuring quality, severe-error rate, tokens, tools, and latency on pre-registered task classes.** It is **likely (55-80%)** to reduce wasted replication on calibrated task classes, because it directly rejects workers whose measured incremental value is below the owner's threshold. It is **unlikely (20-45%)** to be sufficient for rare catastrophic errors, because ordinary calibration suites may contain too few high-cost tail events; E3 must report severe failures separately rather than absorbing them into an average.

### Proposal D — UNVERIFIED until Experiment E4: recurring disguised proficiency and contamination challenges

**NATIVE-vs-BUILD: BUILD-SMALL (<~200 lines, rides the existing hook/skill/MCP bus).**

Periodically inject a small number of versioned, live-shaped fixtures through the same dispatch and synthesis path as ordinary work. Rotate surface forms and hold out fixture families so workers cannot simply memorize them. Include a blank-like negative control (no defect or no relevant fact), a known-positive control, a seeded wrong anchor visible to only one branch, and a task with deliberately unavailable evidence where abstention is correct. Compare the observed result to the calibration envelope and disable the table entry when drift exceeds a predeclared tolerance.

The premise that fixtures can remain operationally disguised without polluting owner work is **UNVERIFIED; Experiment E4 settles it in shadow mode where outputs cannot mutate the workspace, followed by review of detector leakage and false side effects before any live insertion.** It is **likely (55-80%)** that recurring proficiency tests will detect drift missed by a one-time benchmark, because forensic proficiency testing is explicitly ongoing and blind programs exercise the full intake-to-report process. It is **unlikely (20-45%)** that fixtures alone will detect novel failures outside their constructed families, because controls detect what their design makes observable.

## Why the analogy transfers—and where it breaks

### Structural correspondences

1. **Ambiguous evidence plus judgment.** Pattern-evidence examiners and agent workers both transform incomplete inputs into conclusions whose criteria may not be fully formalized. That makes contextual anchoring and inter-rater variability relevant.
2. **Replicated opinions can be contaminated.** A second examiner who sees the first result and a second worker who sees the first draft are not independent replications. Blind verification directly addresses this structure.
3. **Ground truth can be manufactured for tests.** Known-source forensic comparisons and seeded agent fixtures allow accuracy measurement even when ordinary live cases do not.
4. **The whole pipeline matters.** Blind proficiency casework tests receipt, routing, analysis, and reporting; an agent proficiency task can likewise test dispatch, tools, worker reports, and synthesis rather than model answers alone.
5. **Abstention is a first-class outcome.** Both domains can look safer by replacing decisions with “inconclusive.” The orchestration curve must price that trade-off explicitly.

It is **very likely (80-90%)** that these correspondences are strong enough to justify E1-E4, because each proposed experiment preserves the forensic mechanism—blinding, known controls, normal-path testing, and multidimensional outcomes—rather than borrowing forensic vocabulary alone.

### Disanalogies that prohibit a stronger borrow

1. **Forensic evidence is normally observational; agents can change the object being examined.** Two workers editing one workspace interfere causally. Sealed replication therefore applies to read-only diagnosis/research or isolated worktrees, not concurrent mutation.
2. **Agent clones are less independent than different trained examiners.** Identical model weights, system prompts, retrieval, and tools create common-mode errors even with perfect communication isolation. Worker count cannot be converted to an effective independent sample size without empirical covariance measurements.
3. **Open-ended tasks lack complete ground truth.** A forensic test can use known-source samples; “best architecture” or “complete review” often has no enumerable answer key. Human adjudication may itself be biased. The proficiency curve is strongest for testable claims and weakest for generative design.
4. **Parallel workers may divide labor rather than replicate judgments.** Specialist decomposition has complementarity and dependency, not examiner-style reproducibility. Its marginal value must be measured as a distinct orchestration pattern.
5. **Synthesis is an additional decision-maker.** Forensic blind verification often compares conclusions; agent orchestration usually merges them. The merger can recover complementary findings or manufacture a new error, so the whole pipeline and pre-synthesis reports both need scoring.
6. **Loss functions differ.** Forensic false identification, false exclusion, and inconclusive outcomes have legal consequences; agent tasks trade quality against latency, token/tool spend, reversibility, and severity. No forensic error rate supplies the owner's utility weights.
7. **Fixtures are gameable.** Agents may recognize repeated synthetic tasks or benchmark phrasing. Surface rotation and held-out families reduce but do not eliminate this risk.

It is **almost certain (90-99%)** that the forensic literature does **not** justify a universal fixed worker count, because the cited studies report item/examiner variability, dependence, missingness, and condition-specific error rates. Any recommendation such as “always run three” would force the analogy beyond its evidence.

## Decision rule in compact form

For task class `c`, orchestration pattern `p`, and worker count `k`, record:

`U(c,p,k) = value(correct verified atoms) - cost(false atoms) - execution cost - synthesis cost`

and:

`Delta(c,p,k) = E[U(c,p,k) - U(c,p,k-1)]`.

Choose the smallest calibrated `k` for which the upper confidence bound of the next `Delta` is below the owner's threshold, subject to a separate severe-error constraint. This is a sequential *policy lookup based on prior trials*, not an online bandit and not permission to keep launching workers until a noisy metric happens to decline.

The exact utility function, threshold, sample size, confidence procedure, and task taxonomy are **UNVERIFIED; E2 and E3 settle them through preregistered held-out prediction and shadow comparison.** It is **very likely (80-90%)** that reporting the full curve is more informative than reporting only the chosen `k`, because it exposes uncertainty, cost/quality trade-offs, and nonmonotonic behavior rather than hiding them behind one number.

## Anti-overfit gate

All four proposals ride the existing configuration/hook/skill/MCP bus. They require sealed report fields, a small fixture runner/scorer, and a static policy table. They do **not** introduce a second orchestration runtime, vector database or knowledge graph, durable-execution engine, trained critic, observability SaaS, or guardrail framework. The proficiency fixtures are ordinary files; results can be append-only JSON/CSV artifacts; the gate is a table lookup.

It is **likely (55-80%)** that the runner and gate logic can remain within the BUILD-SMALL envelope, because the mechanism needs randomized dispatch metadata, JSON collection, scoring, bootstrap summaries, and table output—not a service. This size estimate is **UNVERIFIED; a 200-line spike implementing one task class and one existing hook path would settle it.** If it exceeds that envelope, the correct response is to reduce scope or keep it as an offline evaluation script, not create a new daemon.

## Alternatives rejected

### Majority vote over unsealed workers

Rejected. It confuses correlated agreement with independent confirmation and gives no answer to “when does one more worker stop helping?” The forensic evidence specifically distinguishes blind from nonblind verification.

### A fixed “three-worker quorum”

Rejected. No cited forensic mechanism yields a universal examiner count, and item/examiner effects make such a number an overfit prior.

### Output-diversity heuristics as a stop rule

Rejected. Different wording can encode the same wrong claim, while identical wording can repeat a correct fact. Ground-truthed unique correct and wrong atoms are the relevant calibration signals.

### An online multi-armed bandit or autonomous orchestration service

Rejected by the anti-overfit gate. It would add a second runtime and learn from noisy, selectively observed live rewards. A small offline curve plus static configuration is sufficient to test the borrow first.

### A trained critic to adjudicate worker truth

Rejected by the anti-overfit gate and by the analogy. It replaces known controls with another correlated model judgment. For incomplete ground truth, adjudication uncertainty must be shown, not laundered through a critic score.

## Evidence boundaries and gaps

- This unit makes no claim that the current local harness lacks sealed reports, proficiency fixtures, or a marginal stop policy. Under the assignment's ideation-only rule I did not inspect the local repository, and the required two mechanically independent absence signals therefore do not exist here.
- The claim that these proposals would catch *currently observed* harness failures is **UNVERIFIED; a separate grounded unit must demonstrate both (1) known-present detector controls and (2) two independent signals from live traces/configuration before making that claim.** Here, “what it catches” means the failure classes the proposed controlled experiments are designed to reveal.
- No source located establishes an optimal number of parallel forensic examiners. The novelty is the translation of contamination-controlled proficiency measurement into a marginal worker curve, not a claim that forensic labs already use this exact orchestration rule.
- Blind verification without ground truth establishes disagreement/reproducibility, not correctness. Any harness metric that treats disagreement as proof that the first worker was wrong would repeat that category error.
- The forensic evidence base itself is contested. Missingness, self-selection, test awareness, inconclusive treatment, and lack of pairwise independence can bias published rates ([Luby and Kadane](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10041348/)). Those are design requirements for E1-E4, not footnotes to discard after adopting the analogy.

## Final verdict

Adopt the analogy as a **small, falsifiable evaluation layer**: sealed reports, known positive/negative controls, disguised representative proficiency tasks, multidimensional scoring, and a task-class-specific marginal-value curve. Do not adopt “more examiners equals more truth,” majority voting, or a universal worker count.

The recommendation is **likely (55-80%)** to improve decisions about parallel-worker count on benchmarkable task classes, because it directly measures marginal verified yield, correlated error, abstention, and cost under controlled blinding. That benefit remains **UNVERIFIED until E1-E3 predict held-out live-shadow outcomes**. For open-ended generative work with mutable shared state and no reliable ground truth, the analogy is **unlikely (20-45%)** to support an automatic stop decision; its useful output there is diagnostic evidence about correlation and contamination, not an authoritative `k`.
